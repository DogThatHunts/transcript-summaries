NVIDIA World Models Explained: What Developers Can Build

NVIDIA’s Mingyu Liu explains how the Cosmos model family fuses simulation, action, and understanding to power the next generation of physical AI and robotics.

In this episode, host Nate B. Jones sits down with Mingyu Liu, Vice President of Cosmos Lab at NVIDIA, to discuss the architecture, capabilities, and future of NVIDIA's Cosmos world model platform. Liu shares his decade-long journey at NVIDIA pioneering deep learning for visual generation, emphasizing that NVIDIA operates primarily as a developer-first software company. The conversation explores how world models differ from traditional LLMs by outputting actions and physical simulations rather than text tokens. The evolution of visual AI and NVIDIA's developer-first approach

Mingyu Liu reflects on his ten-year career at NVIDIA, recalling a time when using deep learning for visual content generation was highly controversial. A decade ago, AI-generated images were low-resolution and often looked unnatural. However, Liu believed that scaling a data-driven, deep learning paradigm would eventually replace hand-designed computer graphics rules. Today, visual AI generation is growing exponentially.

While NVIDIA is globally recognized for its GPUs, Liu highlights that the company employs more software engineers than hardware engineers. NVIDIA builds layers of software—including CUDA, developer libraries, and specialized AI models—on top of its hardware architecture. This vertical integration allows developers to maximize GPU performance and helps NVIDIA understand what future computer architectures are required to solve upcoming challenges.

Defining world models and the Cosmos platform

Liu contrasts world models with LLMs, noting that humans develop models of the physical world long before they acquire language. While LLMs excel at symbolic and semantic reasoning, world models are built to understand, simulate, and navigate physical space.

NVIDIA's Cosmos platform is designed specifically for physical AI developers working on self-driving cars, robotics, and factory orchestration. In June, NVIDIA released the Cosmos Suite, which fuses world understanding, world simulation, and world action into a single mutual transformer architecture. This unified model shares an underlying representation of the physical world to simplify development.

To accommodate different deployment environments, Cosmos is offered in three sizes: * Super: The largest model, designed for frontier performance in data centers. * Nano: A mid-sized model balancing performance and efficiency. * Edge: The smallest model, optimized to run in real-time on edge devices like NVIDIA Jetson Thor.

Scaling laws, physics approximation, and synthetic data

Scaling in world models occurs across multiple dimensions, including data volume, model size, test-time inference, and agentic execution. Liu explains that providing highly detailed descriptions of motion during test-time inference significantly improves the fidelity of visual simulations.

When asked whether world models possess an emergent understanding of physics, Liu explains that neural networks remain largely a "black box." Rather than truly understanding physical laws, the models approximate the rules governing observed data. To improve this approximation, NVIDIA leverages physics engines like Omniverse and Isaac to generate high-fidelity synthetic data (such as simulating Newtonian motion or object interactions), which is then used to train the models.

The role of policy models and agentic execution in physical AI

Liu defines a "policy model" as an AI designed to control a physical device by predicting trajectories and generating real-time actions based on visual observations. For example, a self-driving car's policy model takes visual inputs and constantly recalculates waypoints to safely navigate changing road conditions.

Both Liu and the host discuss how agentic AI—the process of decomposing a complex problem into smaller, specialized sub-problems—is transitioning from software to physical AI. Because physical robots face strict compute and cost constraints, they cannot run massive, general-purpose supercomputers onboard. Instead, robots will rely on specialized architectures and localized agentic execution to perform specific tasks efficiently.

Verifiable rewards and the future of robotics

One of the primary bottlenecks in physical AI development is establishing verifiable rewards. In digital domains like coding or mathematics, verifying success is instantaneous. In physical domains, verification is much harder.

Liu points out that tasks with easily verifiable visual or software-based feedback loops will be automated first. For example, a robot folding laundry can visually verify its success, and a robot inserting cables in a data center can run a software test to check connectivity. Conversely, subjective tasks like cooking the perfect scrambled egg require human taste testing, making them incredibly difficult to verify and train in simulation. Consequently, Liu predicts that household laundry robots will become viable much faster than robotic chefs.

Looking forward, Liu is excited by the rapid progress in 3D world generation and believes the next major breakthrough will involve connecting these generative 3D simulations directly to physical AI and robotic tool use.
