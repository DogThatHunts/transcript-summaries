EurodollarU | Europe’s 20-Year Economic Collapse Is Finally Impossible to Hide

European tourists visiting the U.S. experienced a profound culture shock that exposed a compounding, two-decade economic divergence driven by broken banking systems.

The world cup culture shock

During a recent summer, thousands of European soccer fans traveled to the United States and quickly flooded social media with videos expressing genuine shock at American material abundance. Visitors marveled at the size of suburban homes, wide roads, large vehicles, pervasive air conditioning, and cheap gasoline.

While social media reactions are not formal economic data, Jeff Snyder argues that this collective culture shock revealed a massive, overlooked material gap. The divergence is not a story of a perfect American recovery—as the U.S. has also underperformed historical standards—but rather a stark contrast between two disappointing post-2008 systems, where Europe fell much further behind and stayed there.

The broken textbook model of interest rates

Standard economic textbooks teach that central banks control the economy like an accelerator pedal: lowering interest rates makes loans cheaper, prompting banks to lend, businesses to invest, and the economy to grow. However, Snyder points out that European banks are currently doing the exact opposite.

Even with the European Central Bank (ECB) benchmark rate at historically low, supposedly accommodative levels, commercial lenders are operating defensively. According to the ECB's bank lending survey, banks are tightening standards, experiencing deteriorating risk tolerance, and aggressively buying safe government securities instead of financing the private economy.

Snyder explains that central banks only administer overnight benchmarks; they cannot force commercial banks to extend credit. When economic risk is high, energy costs are volatile, and collateral is questionable, a lower benchmark rate does not turn a bad credit risk into a good one. Instead, lower rates often signal economic weakness, flattening the yield curve and reducing bank profitability, which actually discourages private lending.

Why Europe suffered more than the United States

The root of the divergence lies in how both regions structured their financial systems prior to the 2008 crisis. The United States possesses highly developed capital markets, including robust corporate bond markets and non-bank securitization channels. When American banks pulled back after 2008, commercial enterprises had alternative routes to secure funding.

Europe, by contrast, relied almost entirely on its banking sector. When European banks became structurally impaired during the global eurodollar crisis and the subsequent 2011 sovereign debt crisis, the credit mechanism for the entire European continent broke down.

This structural blockage prevented a true recovery. Snyder distinguishes between a recession (a temporary period of negative growth) and a depression (a persistent, multi-year shortfall with no upside growth). Because Europe has spent nearly two decades in a depression-like trajectory, the missing growth has compounded, resulting in a massive cumulative loss in wages, productivity, housing quality, and overall living standards.

The illusion of central bank stimulus

The ECB did not ignore these structural issues; instead, it engaged in escalating rounds of intervention, including quantitative easing (QE) and negative interest rates.

In 2014, the ECB introduced negative deposit rates to penalize banks for holding idle reserves, hoping to force them to lend. However, European banks frequently chose to pay the penalty rather than take on risks they deemed unacceptable. Snyder asserts that this behavior was a clear signal that the expected returns on private lending did not justify the credit risks.

During brief periods of optimism, such as the "globally synchronized growth" narrative of 2017–2018, central bankers prepared to normalize policy. However, bond and money markets never bought into the recovery narrative. Yields remained subdued, and when economic weakness returned in 2018, rates quickly fell back, proving that the underlying economic system had never truly healed.

Compounding stagnation and the 2020s supply shock

Because stagnation happens gradually, citizens inside an economy rarely notice the slow decline because they compare their current state to the previous year rather than an alternative, healthier trajectory.

This long-term weakness left European households highly vulnerable to the supply shocks of the early 2020s. Snyder argues that the post-pandemic price spikes were not driven by excess bank credit creation (monetary inflation) but by supply-side energy and resource scarcity.

Because European incomes had stagnated for over a decade, households had no financial cushion to absorb these higher costs. The result was severe demand destruction: families were forced to cut discretionary spending, delay home purchases, and reduce consumption. This ongoing contraction further discourages cautious banks from lending to struggling businesses, reinforcing a self-fulfilling loop of economic decline.
