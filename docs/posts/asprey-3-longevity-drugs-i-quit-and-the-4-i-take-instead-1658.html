Dave Asprey dismantles three popular longevity drugs and reveals four lesser-known pharmaceuticals he actually takes for aging prevention.

Three longevity drugs Asprey quit—and four he takes instead

Asprey argues that three widely recommended pharmaceuticals for longevity don't hold up to scrutiny when examined against actual human data, despite their popularity in biohacking circles. He frames pharmaceuticals as "scalpels"—tools to target specific aging pathways when lifestyle interventions alone fall short—rather than replacements for foundational practices like sleep, movement, and proper nutrition.

The "no" list: drugs to avoid for longevity

Metformin

Asprey acknowledges metformin's 700-year track record in medicine (originating from French lilac in medieval Europe) and its legitimate role for type 2 diabetes. However, he argues against using it for longevity in healthy people. His primary concern: a 2019 MASTER trial found that healthy adults over 65 taking metformin during resistance training built significantly less muscle than the placebo group, confirmed by CT scans. Asprey attributes this to metformin's suppression of mTOR, the biological pathway driving muscle growth. He also notes that metformin creates a persistent B12 deficiency risk lasting years after discontinuation, which can impair brain function. Additional side effects include gastrointestinal upset. Asprey suggests berberine as a superior alternative, offering similar pathway effects without these downsides.

Rapamycin

Rapamycin originated from soil bacteria discovered on Easter Island in 1964 and was initially developed as an antifungal before becoming an immunosuppressant for organ transplant patients. The National Institute on Aging's mouse testing program found rapamycin extended lifespan more consistently than almost anything else tested—but, Asprey emphasizes, "humans aren't mice." The drug's mechanism involves inhibiting mTOR, but it has two separate arms: mTOR1 (tied to growth and repair, where longevity benefits appear to come from) and mTOR2 (handling insulin signaling and metabolic housekeeping). At higher doses or with long-term use, rapamycin spills into mTOR2, causing problems. Asprey cites the 2025 PERL trial, a year-long placebo-controlled study in healthy adults 50+ using low intermittent dosing. While it appeared safe with adverse effects matching placebo, rapamycin failed to produce meaningful results: visceral fat didn't change, and only women on higher doses saw modest gains in muscle mass and reduced pain—insufficient evidence for lifespan extension. Asprey notes side effects include mouth ulcers, impaired wound healing, elevated blood lipids, and insulin resistance. He personally feels "like crap" even at low doses and doesn't recommend internal use, though he acknowledges rapamycin cream (compounded) as "massively good for skin longevity."

Statins

Asprey presents statins as the most controversial recommendation, noting that even physicians debate their value. His core argument centers on the harm-reduction ratio: in the general population, 100 people must take statins for one person to benefit from reduced cardiovascular risk, while approximately 30% of users experience side effects. He contrasts this unfavorably with supplements like zinc, which he argues have better safety and protective data. Asprey traces statins' history to Japanese biochemist Akira Endo, who in the 1960s dedicated his career to finding a cholesterol-lowering drug after witnessing heart attack victims in New York. Endo screened 6,000 microbial strains and discovered mevastatin from a penicillium mold on rice in a Kyoto grain shop (1973), but his own company rejected it. Merck later brought lovastatin to market in 1987. Asprey argues the foundational assumption—that lowering cholesterol reduces mortality—is disproven, yet the pharmaceutical industry persists in promoting it. He notes that people living past 100 universally have high LDL, not low LDL. Statins' most common side effect is muscle pain, the leading reason people discontinue them. A meta-analysis found statins cause 11 extra muscle pain events per 1,000 person-years. More concerning to Asprey: statins lower coenzyme Q10 in muscle and mitochondria, critical for longevity. He takes 300 mg of CoQ10 daily for 20 years based on strong data. Merck even holds a patent for packaging CoQ10 with statins to address muscle problems but never launched the product. Asprey argues this reveals pharmaceutical priorities: statins generate enormous revenue despite questionable longevity benefits. He recounts learning from cardiologists Julian Whitaker and Steven Sinatra in early longevity nonprofit meetings that statins harm rather than help, a lesson that shaped his independent research.

The "yes" list: pharmaceuticals Asprey actually takes

Tadalafil (Cialis), 5 mg daily

Asprey has taken tadalafil nightly for about seven years. The drug works by relaxing and widening blood vessels, increasing blood flow throughout the body—not just to reproductive tissues. He frames this as preventing endothelial dysfunction, one of the earliest visible symptoms of which is erectile dysfunction in men. Endothelial dysfunction also drives heart disease and cognitive decline. Tadalafil originated as sildenafil (Viagra), developed by Pfizer in the early 1990s to treat angina (chest pain from restricted heart blood flow). After eight years and planned abandonment, a trial in a Welsh mining town revealed an unexpected side effect: male participants didn't want to stop the study. Pfizer connected the dots, and the FDA approved sildenafil as Viagra in 1998—one of the fastest-selling drugs in pharmaceutical history. Tadalafil (Cialis) followed as a longer-acting alternative in the same PDE inhibitor class. At 5 mg daily (the prostate/urinary symptom dose, much lower than the 20–40 mg recreational dose), there is long-term human safety data. Asprey cites vascular studies: men with elevated cardiovascular risk taking tadalafil showed 4.2%–9.3% improvement in flow-mediated dilation, nearly doubling small vessel dilation compared to placebo. Nitric oxide markers increased (beneficial for brain function), and endothelin-1 (a vessel-constricting compound) decreased. Daily low-dose tadalafil in men with prostate enlargement improved arterial stiffness and peripheral circulation markers over 3–12 months. Asprey recommends starting around age 30 as preventive medicine: "It is so much cheaper and easier to just not age than it is to reverse your age." He notes that in the US, tadalafil requires a prescription, but in most of the world it's available over-the-counter at pharmacies.

SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., Jardiance, dapagliflozin), 10–25 mg daily

These newer diabetes medications block glucose reabsorption in the kidneys, allowing excess sugar to be excreted in urine. The history traces to phlorizin, a compound in apple tree bark known since the 1800s to cause glucose spillage into urine; decades of pharmaceutical work transformed this into modern drugs. Originally developed for diabetes, large outcome trials revealed broader benefits: reduced heart failure hospitalization, reduced cardiovascular death, and stopped kidney disease progression—even in non-diabetic patients. Asprey, born with only one kidney, uses SGLT2 inhibitors to protect his single "super mega kidney" that is twice as large as normal. The mechanism appears to put the body into a state mimicking mild caloric restriction or ketosis, improving mitochondrial efficiency and reducing systemic inflammation. Preclinical models show lifespan expansion and reduction of senescent cell burden (zombie cells). A 2023 review found SGLT2 inhibitors also affect autophagy and oxidative stress response—cells refresh themselves, zombie cells are eliminated, oxidative stress is reduced. Asprey calls these "longevity drugs" and recommends them at minimum as "healthspan" drugs, though he prefers the framing of life extension toward age 120 rather than mere healthspan. Major outcome trials used 10 mg or 25 mg once daily; Asprey suggests keeping doses light since 10 mg and 25 mg perform similarly.

Acarbose (Precose), up to 100 mg three times daily

Acarbose is an older, unglamorous diabetes medication that slows enzyme breakdown of complex carbohydrates in the gut, reducing blood sugar spikes. It came to market in the early 1990s but remained obscure due to gastrointestinal side effects (gas, bloating). However, mouse longevity data changed the picture: the National Institute on Aging's testing program found acarbose increased male mouse lifespan by 22% and female by 5% (Asprey notes sex differences are real, not just scaling). Maximum lifespan increased 8–11% in males. Follow-up studies confirmed the effect and found reduced lung tumor burden, better liver and kidney health, and improved glucose response. Asprey notes acarbose moves starch digestion further into the lower intestine, altering gut bacteria composition. Studies found specific bacterial blooms and increased fecal propionate (a short-chain fatty acid). Higher short-chain fatty acids, including butyrate, correlate with mouse lifespan. In humans, taking butyrate or propionate is linked to better metabolic health and gut barrier function. Asprey describes acarbose as "a 30-year-old, unglamorous diabetes drug that got ignored because it made you fart" but "has some of the best rodent longevity data of anything on this list." Standard dosing starts at 25 mg three times daily with the first bite of each meal, titrating to 100 mg three times daily over weeks. Asprey suggests taking "almost as much as gives you bad farts and then stop there." Activated charcoal can mitigate gas, though it should not be taken simultaneously with drugs.

Low-dose naltrexone (LDN), 1.5–4.5 mg daily

Naltrexone, developed in the 1980s for opioid and alcohol addiction, blocks opioid receptors at standard doses (50 mg). At a tiny fraction of this dose (1.5–4.5 mg, less than 10% of addiction dosing), it appears to produce opposite effects: calming an overactive immune system, especially in the brain. In 1985, New York physician Bernard Behari pioneered LDN as a concept, discovering immune effects the drug wasn't designed for. Asprey first learned of LDN around 1995–96, ordered naltrexone from Europe, and crushed pills to create his own low-dose formulation, but it produced no effect for him personally. He has tried it dozens of times since without benefit, noting "I don't have a problem with my opioid receptors, unfortunately." However, he acknowledges strong data for others and notes the mechanism: blocking opioid receptors briefly at low doses triggers the body to produce more endogenous endorphins, which rebound and activate pathways regulating cell growth and repair. Human data supports LDN for autoimmune conditions (MS, Crohn's, fibromyalgia—which Asprey suffered from in his 20s) and chronic arthritis pain, with minimal side effects. One effect worth noting: LDN can dramatically lower IL-6 and TNF-alpha (inflammatory cytokines) in some people. Asprey controversially references a Stockholm incident where city elite allegedly took pharmaceutical-grade heroin from a pharmaceutical CEO for longevity and appeared 20 years younger than their actual age, suggesting opioid receptors warrant attention for longevity—though he explicitly disclaims heroin use. He emphasizes LDN is worth trying, especially as part of a longevity immunity strategy.

Dosing summary and access

Asprey provides specific dosing for the "yes" list: - Tadalafil: 5 mg daily, continuous (taken at night; men may notice "morning kickstand," women may experience "morning bean") - SGLT2 inhibitors: Jardiance 10–25 mg once daily, or dapagliflozin 10 mg; Asprey recommends keeping doses light - Acarbose: Start 25 mg three times daily with meals, titrate to 100 mg three times daily over weeks; adjust to tolerance - LDN: 1.5–4.5 mg daily

All four require a prescription in the US. Outside the US, many are available over-the-counter at pharmacies for pennies. Asprey advises presenting research to a friendly physician; if they refuse to support your longevity plan, find another doctor. He notes many physicians are fleeing traditional practice (average retirement age ~40 due to stress) and becoming longevity or functional medicine doctors, many working for telehealth longevity clinics that can prescribe via Zoom. He recommends cash payment to avoid insurance hassles and coverage denials. Compounded rapamycin is available but Asprey is not a fan. Online ordering from Indian pharmacies is common; US law allows importing up to a 90-day supply with a doctor's note from anywhere on earth.

Foundational principles

Asprey emphasizes these pharmaceuticals are "tweaks" and "big guns," not substitutes for fundamentals: sleep, movement, proper nutrition, and lifestyle-based vascular health improvements. He frames them as "frosting on the cake," not the cake itself. The core message: do the biohacking fundamentals first, then consider pharmaceutical optimization.
