The premise of the income tax claim

Dave Champion, author of Income Tax: Shattering the Mist, asserts that the federal government has never legally imposed an income tax on ordinary, hardworking Americans. Having personally abstained from filing tax returns or paying income taxes for 31 years without legal ramifications, Champion argues that the entire federal income tax system is maintained through deliberate legal chicanery designed to prevent the actual letter of the law from ever being evaluated in a court of law.

Shifting the burden of willfulness under Section 7203

Champion analyzes 26 U.S.C. § 7203, the statute governing the misdemeanor of "willful failure to file." He notes that a key element of this crime is "willfulness," which legally requires the defendant to have known they had a legal duty to file but failed to do so.

According to Champion, the government avoids its burden of proving that a defendant is legally a "person required" to file by shifting the focus to whether the defendant believed they were required to file. By presenting past tax filings, signed W-4s, or W-9s as evidence of the defendant's active participation in the system, the prosecution demonstrates that the defendant considered themselves obligated to file. Champion claims this maneuver effectively immunizes the government from having to prove that Congress actually imposed the tax on the individual in the first place.

Redefining "person" under Chapter 75

Champion highlights a distinction in how the tax code defines a "person." While the general definition under 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1) broadly includes individuals and legal entities, Champion points out that Chapter 75 (which contains the tax evasion statute, 26 U.S.C. § 7201) uses a more restrictive definition.

Under 26 U.S.C. § 7343, a "person" for the purposes of Chapter 75 is defined to include officers or employees of corporations, or members or employees of partnerships, who are under a specific duty to perform the tax-related act. Champion argues that this definition does not apply to ordinary citizens, but is instead legally restricted to individuals associated with foreign entities or domestic entities handling foreign-source income.

Transmuting tax payments into voluntary gifts

The core of Champion's argument rests on 31 U.S.C. § 321(d), which outlines the general authority of the Secretary of the Treasury. Section 321(d)(1) allows the Secretary to accept voluntary gifts and bequests to aid the department's work, while Section 321(d)(2) states that for the purposes of federal income, estate, and gift taxes, property accepted under paragraph 1 shall be considered a gift or bequest to the United States.

Champion claims this statute is a legal mechanism designed to protect the government from constitutional challenges. He argues that because the government has no constitutional authority to levy or keep taxes from individuals upon whom no tax is legally imposed, it uses this statute to legally reclassify erroneously paid income taxes as voluntary "gifts." In his view, this allows the government to claim in court that any funds sent by taxpayers were given voluntarily, shielding the state from accusations of unlawful collection.
