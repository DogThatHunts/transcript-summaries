The Freemasons: spooky rituals and ancient secrets

The entered apprentice initiation

An inner guard holds a dagger to his bare chest before he kneels before the worshipful master. At an altar, the candidate places his right hand on a Bible and presses a compass point against his chest, swearing a binding oath of secrecy. The oath includes gruesome penalties: "my throat be cut across, my tongue be torn out by its root, and that I be buried in sand a cable's length from shore." The assembled brothers then reveal three sacred objects—the Holy Book, the Square, and the Compass—and teach the candidate a secret grip and the word "Boaz." Secrecy, the narrator emphasizes, is paramount.

Key insights

  1. Secrecy is performative. The narrator notes that Masonic rituals and oaths have been publicly available for centuries; the "illusion of secrecy is more important than the reality." Outsiders seeking forbidden knowledge are called "Cowans."

  2. Freemasonry is not monolithic. No single authority governs all lodges. Variations exist in admission age (18, 21, or 25), gender policies, and ritual details. African American lodges split between two Grand Lodge jurisdictions, and the Grand Lodge of England recognizes some irregular bodies while rejecting others.

  3. Symbolism, not substance. Modern Freemasonry is formally defined as "a system of morality veiled in allegory and illustrated by symbols." The gavel, square, and apron are based on stonemason tools but are never used for actual work; members are "symbolic masons," not operative ones.

  4. Higher degrees attract esoteric and political intrigue. Beyond the three craft degrees (entered apprentice, fellow craft, master mason), optional rites—the Scottish Rite (33 degrees), York Rite, and others—introduce mystical and occult doctrines. These rites operate independently of grand lodges and can "pretty much do and believe whatever they want."

  5. Origins are invented, not documented. Freemasonry's true beginnings are lost. Early Masonic documents invoke Euclid, King Solomon, and even Adam, but lack historical evidence. This blank canvas has allowed speculation about links to the Knights Templar, the Illuminati, and occult traditions.

The three craft degrees and the path to higher mysteries

The three basic degrees—entered apprentice, fellow craft, and master mason—form the foundation of Blue Lodge Masonry. No Mason is required to advance beyond them. However, higher degrees are conferred by separate rites, which the narrator compares to merit badges earned by an Eagle Scout. These rites operate as "speculative or esoteric masonry," where mystical and occult doctrines take center stage.

The Scottish Rite, despite its name, traces its origins to France, not Scotland, and has no documentable link to Scotland itself. It offers up to 33 degrees. The York Rite combines three separate bodies: Royal Archmasonry, Cryptic Masons, and the Knights Templar Commandery. The Memphis and Misraim Rite awarded a staggering 99 degrees, including titles such as "Knight of the Rose Cross," "Patriarch of Isis," and "Pontiff of the Mystic City." The narrator poses the question of whether such elaborate titles were taken seriously or merely for show, concluding that "it all depended on the initiate."

Esoteric interpretations: Pike, Hall, and the inner brotherhood

The American esoteric scholar and 33rd degree Mason Manley P. Hall argued that Freemasonry is "a fraternity within a fraternity"—an outer organization concealing an inner brotherhood of the elect. The visible society pursues ethical, educational, and humanitarian concerns; the invisible society is dedicated to "the secret of secrets" (Arum Arcanorum).

The 19th-century Masonic scholar Albert Pike, who commanded the Supreme Council of the Scottish Rite, presented Freemasonry as a search for light rooted in occult mysteries, Kabbalah, Hermeticism, and alchemy. Pike argued that all divine intelligences divide into principles of good and evil, light and darkness, and viewed regular Blue Lodge Masonry as merely "the entrance to the temple." However, Pike's esoteric ideas in his book Morals and Dogma were his personal learned opinion, not doctrine binding on ordinary Masons, most of whom remain unaware of such interpretations.

From operative to speculative: the transformation of Freemasonry

The terms "free" and "accepted" reflect a historical shift. Originally, "free" meant a man unbound by servitude and able to travel; later it signified someone with no conflicting obligations. An operative mason is a real stonemason; an "accepted" or speculative mason is admitted without any intention of laying bricks or stones. The widely accepted but undocumented theory is that stone mason guilds began admitting wealthy non-working members—aristocrats and tradesmen—who eventually dominated, and the actual masons dropped out. The narrator notes the puzzle: "Why would rich guys want to pretend to be common workmen? And why would guilds admit a bunch of dilettantes?"

The modern history: 1717 and beyond

Modern Freemasonry officially began in 1717 with the creation of the Grand Lodge of England, which grew from four existing lodges and expanded steadily. By 1800, over 300 affiliated lodges existed in England alone. However, the 1730s brought schism: the "Ancients" and "Moderns" disputed the importance of rituals and mystical traditions. The Moderns, influenced by Enlightenment rationalism, wanted to abandon ritualism; the Ancients sought to preserve it. The underlying question was whether Freemasonry would be a social and political order or a mystical and religious one. This rift healed in 1813 when the Moderns yielded on rituals and the Ancients became more tolerant of politics. Notably, in 1799, both factions were forced to cooperate to avoid being banned under Britain's Unlawful Societies Act, which targeted groups spreading French revolutionary ideas. Freemasonry received an exemption on condition of maintaining membership records—a testament to the prestige and influence of an organization that counted members of the royal family among its initiates.

The contested Templar connection

The narrator explores the popular but undocumented claim that Freemasonry descended from the Knights Templar. While a Freemason-Templar connection became widely accepted, there is no documentary evidence. The possible exception is the 15th-century Kirkwall Scroll from Scotland, an illustrated tapestry in which some claim to see Templar and Gnostic symbols, though it may be a later forgery.

Historian John Robinson argues for a genuine link in his 1989 book Born in Blood, focusing on the 1381 Peasants' Revolt in England. Robinson speculates that a shadowy "Great Society" behind the revolt was a conspiracy hatched by clandestine Templars seeking revenge against Church and Crown. His evidence, while intriguing, is ultimately thin. However, Robinson raises valid points: medieval Mason guilds were not trade unions but employer associations run by masters who treated lesser brethren as laborers and indentured servants. Thus, Robinson suggests, Crypto-Templars may not have taken over genuine guilds but formed their own pseudo-Masonic guilds as cover, explaining their obsession with secrecy—atypical for ordinary Masons. Notably, the Knights Templar maintained their own internal sect of "Brother Masons," and since most Templar organizations survived suppression, the Brother Masons likely did as well.

The narrator also notes parallels with the revolutionary Carbonari (charcoal burners) of early 19th-century Italy and France, many of them Freemasons, who co-opted the name and terminology as cover for revolutionary conspiracies.

British Freemasonry and imperial power

British Freemasonry became the secret society of the establishment. Membership grew from 300 lodges in the early 18th century to 1,000 by the 1860s, 2,000 by the 1880s, and 3,000 by the start of the 20th century. The poet laureate of British imperialism, Rudyard Kipling, a Mason, popularized the Brotherhood in his writing, notably in The Man Who Would Be King. By 1900, an estimated 250,000 British Freemasons existed, though they represented a small fraction of a population of 40 million and were insignificant in the larger imperial population of 400 million. Membership was heavily concentrated among the wealthy and powerful.

However, some found Freemasonry insufficiently exclusive or serious. Cecil Rhodes, the arch-imperialist behind British South Africa, the De Beers Diamond Cartel, and the Boer War, joined a Masonic lodge in the 1870s because he saw "the wealth and power they possess, and the influence they hold." Yet Rhodes became disillusioned with what he called "the lodge's most ridiculous and absurd rites" and thought the order was "without an object, and without an end." Rhodes' grand vision was to bring the entire uncivilized world under British rule, recover the United States, and make the Anglo-Saxon race one empire. He formed his own secret organization, the Society of the Elect, selectively recruiting statesmen, financiers, and industrialists. Part of his scheme was the Rhodes Scholarship, designed to turn worthy Americans into "right-thinking Englishmen." Freemasonry gave Rhodes his starting point, model, and inspiration.

Irregular Freemasonry: dissidents, occultists, and radicals

Not all British Freemasons were loyal to the empire. The Grand Lodge of England was selective about what it regarded as legitimate, branding bodies like the Scottish Rite as irregular or clandestine. Irregular Freemasonry tended to attract dissidents, both moral and political.

Alistair Crowley, the occultist once branded "the wickedest man in the world," boasted Masonic degrees from French, Mexican, and other lodges, though the Grand Lodge denied any association with him. The esoteric organizations Crowley was associated with—the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn and the Ordo Templi Orientis (OTO)—all had Masonic roots. The OTO was initially envisioned as a Masonic academy unifying Masonic rites with mystical orders to create a unified occult Freemasonry.

Co-Masonry, which initiated men and women alike, originated in some 18th-century French lodges and resurfaced in 1893 with the founding of the Parisian lodge Le Droit Humain (Human Rite). Co-Masonry was later hijacked by English occultists connected to the Theosophical Society, which sought to combine Western and Eastern mysticism into a universal occult doctrine and would inspire much of the modern New Age movement.

Annie Besant, an Englishwoman, was largely responsible for turning Co-Masonry into an appendage of Theosophy. Besant's politics were radical: she was a socialist, a militant proponent of Indian nationalism, and later flirted with communism. Her Freemasonry was decidedly different from Rudyard Kipling's.

Politics in the lodge

While politics was officially forbidden within the Lodge, it was practically impossible to keep out. Masonic lodges were selective; a single dissenting vote could blackball a candidate, resulting in a lodge full of men sharing more or less common views and all sworn to secrecy. Connections formed inside the lodge could take on a different dimension outside it. Simply put, Masonic lodges were "like petri dishes for political conspiracy."

This was most obvious in French Freemasonry and the continental lodges it spawned and influenced. Interestingly, even French Freemasonry was of English origin. The first lodges were set up by British merchants in Dunkirk in 1721. In 1733, a Grand Lodge of France was formed, initially an extension of the London Grand Lodge. However, three years later, French Masons formed a new Grand Lodge, the Grand Orient de France, strongly influenced by radical Enlightenment ideas. While English Freemasonry became the mainstay of the social order, the Grand Orient became an incubator of revolutionary sentiments, passing this radical flavor to lodges in Italy, Germany, Turkey, and Russia. The motto of the Grand Orient—"Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity"—became one of the mottos of the French Revolution.

The radical streak was strongest in the Parisian Nine Sisters Lodge, formed in 1776 (the same year as the Bavarian Illuminati and the American Revolution). American revolutionary Benjamin Franklin was an initiate of the Nine Sisters, as was the anti-religious French philosopher Voltaire, and many other men destined to play a part in France's coming upheaval.

The church's condemnation and the Taxil hoax

Fears that Freemasonry nurtured dangerous ideas, while exaggerated, were not baseless. The Papacy looked on the Freemasons with particular displeasure. In 1738, Pope Clement XII issued the first papal edict against Freemasonry, declaring that it had caused "the greatest suspicion in the mind of the faithful" and that "all prudent and upright men had judged them depraved and perverted." He condemned and prohibited Freemasonry "now and forever." As Clement saw it, the Freemason obsession with secrecy condemned them, for "if they were not doing evil, they would not have so great a hatred of the light."

The Catholic Church also figured in an anti-Masonic scandal that infected France in the 1890s: the Taxil hoax. A journalist, ex-Freemason, and sometimes pornographer named Joseph Joanny Boullan (writing under the names Leo Taxil and Dr. Bataille) exploited public anxiety over Freemasonry and Satanism by combining the two. He churned out sensational books such as The Mysteries of Freemasonry and The Devil in the 19th Century, claiming that Freemasonry was run by a satanic cult called the Palladians, based in Charleston, South Carolina, with American Freemason Albert Pike as the head devil worshipper. Taxil even conjured a Masonic high priestess, Diana Vaughn, who spun lurid tales of black masses and child sacrifice. For good measure, she was supposedly the descendant of the 17th-century British alchemist and Rosicrucian Thomas Vaughan. In 1897, Boullan shocked everyone, especially the Vatican, by revealing that the whole thing was a hoax and Diana Vaughn a figment of his imagination. Nevertheless, that revelation did not and has not stopped some from insisting the story was true.

Conclusion: many freemasonries, one mystery

The most important takeaway is that Freemasonry is not one thing but many, and it always has been. What was true in one's grandfather's lodge is not necessarily true for others. Much of this vagueness and variety stems from the fact that Freemason origins are lost in time. So what was not known was invented. As the narrator concludes, "people see what they want to see and believe what they want to believe."
