Paracetam and the racetam family: the original nootropics

Overview

Dave Asprey argues that paracetam—a molecule invented in the 1960s by Belgian scientist Corneliu E. Sorigué—represents the gold standard for cognitive enhancement and deserves far more recognition than it receives. Unlike stimulants or sedatives, paracetam works by tuning neural efficiency rather than flooding the brain with dopamine. Asprey has used racetams almost daily for 25 years and credits them as foundational to his success in Silicon Valley and business leadership. Key insights

  1. Paracetam is subtle, not obvious. Asprey's first experience illustrates why many people dismiss it: he felt nothing while taking it, but noticed cognitive decline when he stopped. The effect is a tuning of the engine, not a stimulant jolt. This makes it easy to miss unless you cultivate self-awareness.

  2. The term "nootropic" was coined to describe paracetam. When Sorigué discovered that paracetam enhanced learning and memory without sedating or stimulating, he invented the word "nootropic" (mind-bending or mind-shaping) to classify this new category. The original definition requires enhancement of learning or memory, increased brain resilience, neuroprotection, improved higher-order control, and low toxicity—a much stricter standard than modern marketing uses.

  3. Racetams work by improving membrane fluidity and signal efficiency, not receptor flooding. Unlike stimulants that create massive dopamine and norepinephrine shifts, racetams have weak affinity for conventional receptors. Instead, they improve the efficiency of signals already present—Asprey compares it to tuning an engine rather than stepping harder on the gas. This mechanism explains both their subtlety and their neuroprotective effects.

  4. The five main racetams serve different purposes. Paracetam (the original, most studied, 1.5–5 g/day) is the safest baseline; aniracetam (750–1500 mg/day) is Asprey's personal favorite, offering mood support and memory enhancement; oxiracetam (800 mg once or twice daily) suits analytical work; pramiracetam (400–1200 mg/day) specializes in memory recovery after brain injury; and phenylparacetam (100–200 mg once or twice daily) is the most stimulating, ideal for high-stakes cognitive tasks but prone to tolerance.

  5. Racetams are most effective for people with cognitive deficits, not necessarily for healthy young brains. Evidence is strongest for dementia, stroke recovery, aphasia, hypoxia, and brain injury. Healthy young neurons already have flexible membranes and good blood flow, so they may notice less benefit. However, Asprey speculates that early use might slow age-related cognitive decline by preventing membrane stiffening.

History and discovery

Corneliu E. Sorigué, working at UCB in Belgium in the 1960s, was researching GABA-related molecules for sedative development when he synthesized paracetam. The compound behaved unexpectedly: it affected learning and brain function without sedating or stimulating. This forced a rethinking of cognitive enhancement and led Sorigué to coin the term "nootropic." The original definition required:

  • Enhancement of learning or memory
  • Increased brain resistance to disruption
  • Neuroprotection from injury or chemical stressors
  • Improved higher-order control
  • Low toxicity and minimal psychoactive effects

Asprey notes that this definition is far stricter than how modern marketers use "nootropic"—caffeine and amphetamines, which flood dopamine, do not meet the original criteria.

Mechanisms of action

Racetams operate through several overlapping mechanisms:

Acetylcholine and attention. Racetams increase demand on the acetylcholine system, which governs attention, memory formation, and sensory processing. They do not create acetylcholine but make the brain lean harder on what it already has. People over 35 may benefit from additional choline donors (CDP-choline, alpha-GPC, or egg yolks) to meet this increased demand. Insufficient choline can cause headaches or muscle tension.

Glutamate and AMPA signaling. Racetams, especially aniracetam, improve the handoff of glutamate to AMPA receptors. Asprey uses the analogy of oiling hinges and locks so a key (glutamate) slides smoothly into a door (AMPA receptor). Better glutamate-AMPA coupling increases neuroplasticity and learning capacity without the "random noise" of stimulation.

Membrane fluidity and mitochondrial function. Aging and stress stiffen neuronal membranes, reducing receptor flexibility. Paracetam likely improves membrane fluidity, restoring resilience. A 2010 study by Luner et al. showed paracetam improves mitochondrial function in aging and Alzheimer's-affected tissue. A 2016 follow-up by Stockburger et al. linked this to measurable improvements in neuroplasticity and mitochondrial movement—the brain's ability to shuttle mitochondria to areas needing extra power, like a concert's generator trailers.

Microcirculation. Racetams improve small-vessel blood flow, allowing cells to move more easily through capillaries. Poor microcirculation contributes to brain fog and inflammation. Asprey compares stiff membranes and sluggish circulation to cold molasses—everything has to push harder to get anything done.

The five main racetams

Paracetam (1.5–5 g/day). The original and most studied. Asprey recommends starting low and increasing gradually. It is the safest option for conservative users and those over 50. Typical benefits include improved word recall and mental endurance, especially on long research or writing days. Clinical evidence is mixed: a 2002 meta-analysis of 19 double-blind trials found statistically significant improvement in cognitive impairment in older adults; a 2024 meta-analysis found inconsistent results on memory alone. A 2016 systematic review found paracetam specifically helped aphasia after stroke.

Aniracetam (750–1500 mg/day). Asprey's personal favorite. Fat-soluble and shorter-acting (about 4 hours), it touches glutamate, acetylcholine, dopamine, and serotonin, offering a gentle anti-stress effect and mood support. Asprey reports 25 years of aniracetam use has contributed to creativity, social ease, reduced anxiety, and big-picture thinking. Best taken with fat (e.g., butter in coffee) for absorption. Asprey takes 1500 mg in the morning and again at lunch if doing cognitively intensive work.

Oxiracetam (800 mg once or twice daily). The analytical racetam, more activating than paracetam. Favored in the nootropic community for logical, structured work like coding or chess. Less research than paracetam; animal studies suggest mood and anxiety benefits. Asprey has used it but prefers aniracetam or pramiracetam.

Pramiracetam (400–1200 mg/day). The memory specialist, more potent by weight than paracetam, with strong affinity for choline uptake. Asprey recounts taking it before a Silicon Valley VC think tank at Pebble Beach and experiencing remarkable mental clarity and speed. Cited in studies for memory recovery after brain injury or oxygen deprivation. Asprey always takes one of the racetam family when flying to protect against hypoxia.

Phenylparacetam (100–200 mg once or twice daily). The most stimulating, with a phenyl group that crosses the blood-brain barrier faster. Produces alertness, motivated focus, cold tolerance, and endurance. Most people feel this one. Tolerance builds quickly, so it is not a daily driver. Asprey uses it once or twice weekly. Critical warning: Do not combine with psychedelics (LSD or psilocybin). Phenylparacetam's focused alertness directly opposes the surrender needed for psychedelic experiences, making for a poor trip.

Clinical evidence and real-world use

Asprey emphasizes that racetams are most effective for people with cognitive deficits:

  • Stroke and aphasia: Strong evidence, especially for paracetam.
  • Dementia: Mixed picture in clinical trials, but Asprey would use it preventively and therapeutically.
  • Brain injury and hypoxia: Good evidence for pramiracetam and paracetam.
  • Healthy aging: Speculative but plausible; early use might slow membrane stiffening.

Asprey's personal protocol includes taking paracetam or aniracetam before any medical procedure involving sedation or anesthesia for neuroprotection. He advises informing your anesthesiologist but notes they will likely know nothing about racetams and may object. Asprey's stance: "Do what your anesthesiologist says or do what you think is best."

Stacking and combinations

With choline: Recommended, especially over age 35. Use a 6:1 racetam-to-choline ratio: 300–600 mg alpha-GPC or 250–500 mg CDP-choline (citicholine). CDP-choline is safer and better studied; alpha-GPC can build up in cell membranes with daily use. Asprey prefers citicholine first but does not avoid alpha-GPC.

Racetam with racetam: Possible but not necessary. Asprey has stacked paracetam with aniracetam or oxiracetam but finds it rarely worthwhile. Exception: if exhausted from travel, adding phenylparacetam later in the day can provide a boost.

With caffeine: Excellent combination. Asprey notes that paracetam amplifies caffeine's effects, so adjust doses accordingly. Phenylparacetam with high-dose caffeine can cause overstimulation.

With omega-3s (EPA/DHA): Theoretically synergistic for membrane fluidity, but not essential. Both are good independently.

With modafinil: Stacks well; one of Asprey's favorite combinations.

General principle: Avoid over-stacking. Asprey takes 150 supplements daily but carefully layers them by mechanism and genetics. Most people benefit more from simple stacks (racetam + choline) than from complex combinations.

Safety and side effects

Reported side effects: - Headaches (often due to choline depletion; add choline donors) - Insomnia or agitation - Irritability or anxiety - GI symptoms - Brain fog (rare; Asprey suspects underlying issues)

Cautions: - Kidney dysfunction: No direct nephrotoxicity, but use with caution and consult a doctor. - Seizure disorders, psychiatric medications, large pharmaceutical stacks: Consult a healthcare provider. - Pregnancy and breastfeeding: Insufficient data; avoid. - Competitive athletes: Phenylparacetam is WADA-banned; paracetam and others are not (as far as Asprey knows).

Asprey's safety philosophy: The absence of safety data does not mean something is dangerous, nor does it mean it is safe. Paracetam has 60 years of clinical use and millions of users worldwide. Asprey weighs the theoretical risk against documented benefits and chooses to use it. He emphasizes personal autonomy: "Be your own health daddy."

Regulatory landscape (US)

  • Legal status: Paracetam is not a scheduled drug; possession is not criminal.
  • FDA position: Not approved as a dietary supplement or pharmaceutical. The FDA has acted against companies marketing racetams with medical claims.
  • Sourcing challenges: Amazon stopped selling racetams. Gray-market purchases carry risk of contamination, mislabeling, or substitution (e.g., caffeine sold as paracetam).
  • Enforcement: Nootropics Depot, a reliable lab-tested vendor, faced FDA enforcement and large fines during the Biden administration, nearly putting them out of business.
  • International context: Most of the world allows racetam purchase and use; the US regulatory stance is unusual and, in Asprey's view, counterproductive.

Asprey criticizes the regulatory environment as corrupt and anti-human, noting that the inability to access pharmaceutical-grade racetams in the US is a significant public health failure.

Starter protocol for paracetam

Conservative approach (especially for those over 50):

  1. Start with paracetam alone (not multiple racetams).
  2. Over age 40, take choline with it: 3–4 eggs with yolks, or CDP-choline, or alpha-GPC.
  3. Try for three weeks; expect gradual effects, not acute ones.
  4. Track a concrete metric: How often do you forget words? Use a simple tally.
  5. After three weeks, evaluate. If no change, your brain may be doing very well, or you may lack self-awareness. Consider other interventions.
  6. Once familiar with paracetam, try aniracetam for better memory and less stress.
  7. Keep phenylparacetam on hand for high-demand situations (travel, jet lag, intense cognitive work).

Dosing strategy: Start low and increase gradually. A typical range is 1.5–5 g/day in divided doses. If headaches occur, increase choline intake.
