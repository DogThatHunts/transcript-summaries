Master Key | The Law of Success, Book 4 of 8 (1928) by Napoleon Hill - "Imagination & Enthusiasm"

Hill makes imagination the hub of his course and enthusiasm its engine, teaching that recombined ideas, telepathic suggestion and unshakable belief in what you sell lead to success.

Book 4 of 8 of Napoleon Hill's The Law of Success (1928), read by Dan John Miller for the Master Key Society. The ideas are Hill's, reported as he states them, not endorsed or verified here. Every timestamp opens the video a few seconds early.

Book 4 holds two lessons of the sixteen: Lesson Six, Imagination, and Lesson Seven, Enthusiasm. Hill calls imagination "the hub of this reading course" because every earlier lesson depends on it. The definite chief aim, self-confidence, initiative and leadership all have to be built in the imagination before they can exist anywhere else. Enthusiasm is the force that turns those plans into action and passes them on to other people. The book closes with "The Seven Deadly Horsemen", a self-examination essay, and a page of Hill's maxims.

Most of the book is about selling. Hill holds that imagination does not create new things. It rearranges "old ideas and established facts" into new combinations, and its most profitable use is understanding what moves other people. He shows this through a long run of business anecdotes: Piggly Wiggly, the Edison bulb, a dog sold to a farmer, a university president raising a million dollars, and his own campaign to win a prisoner's pardon. Lesson Seven adds the principle of suggestion, the outward-facing twin of auto-suggestion. Hill grounds both lessons in his belief that thought passes from mind to mind by telepathy.

Both lessons end on character. Hill says you cannot convincingly sell what you do not believe. Enthusiasm without conviction fails, and "reputation is that which people are believed to be; character is that which people are." His instructions return to the definite chief aim: write it out, read it aloud each night with enthusiasm, and see yourself already in possession of it.

Lesson 6: Imagination [00:36]

What imagination is [00:46]

Hill defines imagination as "the workshop of the human mind," then adds a dictionary definition of it as the constructive faculty. The student already knows, he says, that the materials of the definite chief aim were "assembled and combined in your imagination." Self-confidence, initiative and leadership must likewise be created there first [02:10]. That makes this lesson the hub of the course, like the telephone exchange all the wires lead to [02:38]. The sequence is always the same. First comes the thought, then the organization of that thought into ideas and plans, then the transformation of those plans into reality [03:25].

Interpretive imagination and telepathy [03:39]

Imagination is both interpretive and creative. Its interpretive side can register thought waves from other minds, as a radio picks up sound [03:53]. Hill calls this telepathy and says it explains "snap judgment and hunches" [04:45]. A buyer's imagination senses a salesman's lack of confidence "whether the prospective buyer is conscious of it or not" [05:59]. Through telepathy and "the law of attraction through which like attracts like," a negative mind fails to attract a positive one [06:28].

Imagination is the one thing over which a person has absolute control. Others may take your wealth or your liberty, but not that [07:55]. Hill prints Leigh Hunt's poem "Abou Ben Adhem," which he says was written in prison. He offers it as a reminder that one great use of imagination is to forgive those who wronged you [08:26]. Understood properly, he claims, imagination could "wipe out poverty and misery and injustice and persecution" in a single generation [10:23].

Hill asks the student to accept telepathy as established. "We need devote no time to proving" it, he says [11:13]. Mob psychology is telepathy at work [11:32]. His evidence is Armistice Day 1918, when he was woken at about three in the morning "as if someone had aroused me by physical force." He went into the streets of Chicago and found thousands of others who had felt it. He attributes this to a thought wave set off by the joy of millions of soldiers ordered to stop fighting [12:03]. At work, the same principle means one grouch disrupts a whole organization [14:17]. If every business is "the extended shadow of one man," as Emerson said, that man should cast confidence and good cheer [15:13].

Creative imagination: rearranging old ideas [15:50]

"There is nothing new under the Sun." Life is a kaleidoscope whose pieces can only be rearranged. Clarence Saunders, a $20-a-week grocery clerk, took the self-help idea from a cafeteria line and created Piggly Wiggly. Hill reads this as a definite purpose, self-confidence and initiative brought together in the imagination [16:30]. Edison combined two known principles. A heated wire gives light, and nothing burns without oxygen, so he sealed the wire in a glass globe [18:12]. Carnegie's only unusual ability, Hill says, was choosing men who would cooperate in harmony and plan for him [20:09]. Beginners can do the same. One stenographer met Charles M. Schwab's private car unasked [21:38]. A graduate sent Hill a $10 bill with his application [22:34]. Edwin C. Barnes rode a freight car to West Orange to get a job with Edison [23:55]. Theodore Roosevelt "started the steam shovels to work on the Panama Canal" [25:23]. James J. Hill, at forty a $30-a-month telegrapher, saw the Great Northern in his mind's eye first [26:07].

After the poem "I hold it true that thoughts are things" [27:50], Hill tells the reader to use imagination as a mirror in which to see "the mansion you intend to own, the factory you intend to manage" [28:31]. A cobbler at the Brooklyn Bridge said it could not be built, while its engineer saw it finished in advance [29:05]. Hill gives more examples of "mixing" imagination with materials. A California promoter blasted useless hills into fill for useless lowland and made a fortune "for mixing some useless dirt with imagination" [30:46]. Pig iron becomes watch springs, but "the one ingredient that is worth most is the one that has no material form" [33:37]. A $50 barn becomes a filling station [34:36]. A printer prospers by allying with an advertising writer [34:57].

Adversity, alliances and the Master Mind [36:37]

Imagination turns failures into assets. A mail carrier who lost a leg used his $5,000 settlement to become a leading engraver [37:10]. There are two ways to profit from imagination: develop your own, or ally yourself with those who have it. Carnegie did both. His Master Mind group held specialists in chemistry, finance and salesmanship, Schwab among them [38:10]. Alliances may be marriage, partnership, friendship or employment, and "it is no disgrace to serve in the capacity of employee" [39:11].

Imagination in salesmanship: the pardon of "B" [40:08]

The master salesman first sees the merits of his goods in his own imagination. Hill's main example is his own campaign to free "B" from the Ohio Penitentiary [40:26]. B was serving twenty years for forgery and said twenty influential men had failed to free him. Hill moved to Columbus and told the governor that B had got 1,729 of the 2,518 prisoners into correspondence courses at no cost to the state. He asked for B's release so B could run a prison school for the 160,000 inmates of other American prisons [43:09]. Governor Vic Donahey agreed, provided the clemency board, warden and chaplain recommended it. The pardon was signed three days later [44:45].

The others, Hill says, failed "because they used no imagination." They pleaded B's family and gave the governor no motive, while Hill had first pictured himself in the governor's place [46:10]. The governor sensed his conviction by telepathy, or at least by "the look of self-confidence in my eyes" [48:57]. Hill draws three rules. There is "only one best approach" to every problem [50:29]. People will grant a favor for a third person that they would refuse you [50:54]. And "men are selfish," so make the buyer's advantage plain [51:52].

In a shepherd-dog story, a man trying "to get rid of" a dog found no taker. A man of imagination sold the same dog to the same crippled farmer for $100 by presenting it as a helper that would herd sheep and bring home the cows [52:17]. Paul Harris founded Rotary to build a law practice without advertising [55:09]. "A kite rises against the wind not with it" [55:47]. Frank Crane, Bud Fisher of Mutt and Jeff and F. W. Woolworth all sold imagination [55:53].

Misapplied imagination, and the other man's place [58:34]

Hill warns against imagining you can "get something for nothing" or ignore others' rights. He says nearly all of the 160,000 American prisoners are there for that reason [58:51]. The constructive rule is to study yourself to understand your customer, because "all men are much alike" [1:00:03]. A detective looks for the motive first [1:00:29]. A "weak-minded" boy found a lost horse by thinking "where I would have gone if I'd been a horse" [1:00:50]. Every business needs dreamers, "but the dreamer must be also a doer" [1:01:41].

The chief aim, decision and emergency [1:02:15]

The most important thing imagination creates is a definite chief aim. Hill points back to Lesson Two: the battle is half won when one knows what is wanted [1:02:28]. Choosing an aim takes imagination and decision, and "the power of decision grows with use" [1:03:05]. Adversity forces both, so men fight best with their backs to the wall, and "many a genius has been born" from emergency [1:03:26].

Welshimer and Harper [1:04:30]

The Rev. P. H. Welshimer of Canton, Ohio, built what Hill calls the country's largest Sunday school, over 5,000 members. He gives every new member a task, leaving "no time left for kicking," and runs committees, a church paper, a choir and an orchestra [1:05:55]. Dr. William Rainey Harper of the University of Chicago walked unannounced into a streetcar magnate's office at noon. He offered the man a hall bearing his name, mentioned that a board member favored the man's rival, and left without asking for anything. The check for a million dollars came the next morning [1:09:09]. Harper had put the man on the defensive, used the rivalry, and appealed to the wish to be remembered [1:13:59].

Ideas "here sown" [1:15:26]

An Illinois insurance salesman who heard Hill lecture on allied effort partnered with a grocer, a filling station, a restaurant and a builder to give their customers free policies. He became a general agent earning over $25,000 a year [1:15:53]. Hill offers more ideas for the taking: vending-machine five-and-tens [1:18:59], a weight-triggered railroad-crossing gate [1:19:40], a radio burglar alarm [1:20:45], and farm produce sold by contract to motorists [1:21:09]. "The more simple and easily adapted to a need an idea is the greater is its value" [1:22:41].

Reading the customer, and selling appearance [1:23:30]

A run of shopping anecdotes follows. A Philadelphia clerk pushed a yellow tie on Hill after he had plainly favored a blue one. Another clerk said "they are not wearing that style this season" and then offered suspenders. A small shop sold him three shirts and three ties in five minutes by watching which shirt he held longest [1:27:47]. Hill estimates "fully 75 percent of the walkouts" are due to tactless showing of goods [1:31:46]. His point is that people pay for atmosphere. Examples are Marshall Field [1:37:54], a jeweler's lavishly wrapped repair [1:39:45], a Broad Street fruit shop charging $10 to $25 for baskets sold for $3 to $7.50 nearby [1:43:12], and the same ham sandwich at 15 cents or a quarter [1:46:00]. "People do not know what they are buying." Even Henry Ford, Hill says, took nearly twenty years to learn that buyers want style [1:48:15].

Lesson 7: Enthusiasm [1:49:22]

What enthusiasm is and does [1:49:30]

Enthusiasm is "a state of mind that inspires and arouses one to put action into the task at hand," and it is contagious. It is to a person what steam is to a locomotive, and the most important factor in salesmanship and public speaking. Compare Billy Sunday with the average preacher [1:49:43]. Hill says it lets you work on less than half the usual sleep and do two or three times the work. He once wrote through two nights and a day [1:50:39].

Developing it through the chief aim [1:52:14]

Enthusiasm begins with the work you like best. If you cannot do that work yet, adopt a definite chief aim that includes it, since no one can stop you from planning, or from "mixing enthusiasm with your plans" [1:52:38]. Happiness "lies always in the future and never in the past" [1:53:09]. Hill's example is his twenty-year dream of a Golden Rule magazine. On Armistice Day 1918 he wrote its first editorial and read it with enthusiasm to an acquaintance, who offered on the spot to supply the money [1:53:57].

Suggestion and its three mediums [1:56:42]

Hill now turns to suggestion. Auto-suggestion, from Lesson Three, is self-suggestion. Suggestion is how "your words and your acts and even your state of mind influence others" [1:56:56]. Through the telepathy of the introductory lesson, an enthusiastic mind registers in the minds around it. A speaker feels this as rapport, and a salesman feels it as the moment to close [1:57:21]. Suggestion has three mediums: what you say, what you do and what you think [1:58:31]. Tone carries conviction more than words, and "words are but devitalized sounds unless colored with feeling" [1:58:55]. Acts count for more than words. A Ford salesman who arrives in a Buick, or a Dictaphone salesman whose stenographer takes shorthand beside him, will not convince [2:00:53]. Thought matters most, because it controls both [2:01:33]. Before suggestion can work, the other mind must be "in a state of neutrality," and "here is where most salesmen fail" [2:02:18].

The two letters [2:04:30]

Hill prints two letters he sent asking prominent men to endorse How to Sell Your Services. The first, to "Mr. Ford," asked a personal favor, ended "thanking you in advance," and got no replies [2:05:59]. The second, to Vice President Thomas R. Marshall, opened with a question that can be answered only one way. It offered a chance to advise "a few hundred thousand of your fellow men" and noted that a reply would cost a two-cent stamp but might change lives [2:06:45]. Hill says the first letter invites refusal [2:08:23]. The second neutralizes the reader and turns "his own conscience into an ally of the writer" [2:26:24]. It drew replies from all, including Roosevelt, John Wanamaker, Frank A. Vanderlip, Carnegie, William Jennings Bryan and Lord Northcliffe [2:31:08]. Between the analyses, a canvasser asks "You wouldn't subscribe for the Post to help me out, would you?" and fails [2:11:39]. A saleswoman opens with "Oh, I see you are a lover of books and magazines," talks about Emerson's "Compensation," and leaves with Hill's order plus five from his staff [2:12:36].

Negative suggestion [2:20:12]

Hill says that "science has established the fact" that negative suggestion can kill. A blindfolded French convict died in seven minutes after a plank struck his neck and warm water ran down his spine [2:20:37]. A woman who feared cancer of the left breast for twenty years died of it there [2:21:29]. He says he has seen warts removed by suggestion in 48 hours [2:22:38]. A life insurance agent hinted at a tubercular finding and so sold Hill a $20,000 policy. Hill calls this a "trick" that was "all right," because he needed the insurance [2:23:09]. The mind files like impressions together, so one doubt summons all doubts. Never knock a competitor, and remember that fear and courage "make poor roommates" [2:32:32]. A suggestion planted deeply and mixed with enthusiasm, "the fertilizer," calls up latent ability [2:35:28].

Sell yourself first: conscience and character [2:37:24]

"Whatever you successfully sell to others you must first sell to yourself." Hill refused a fortune to write propaganda for the Mexican government because he did not believe in its cause [2:37:24]. He asks the reader three times to read the surrounding paragraphs aloud [2:39:25]. Their core is this: "you cannot afford to suggest to another person by word of mouth or by an act of yours that which you do not believe." A conscience you compromise stops guiding you, "just as an alarm clock will fail to awaken you if you do not heed it" [2:43:29]. Real power comes from character, which no one can buy and anyone can build by auto-suggestion [2:44:47].

Enthusiasm in sales organizations [2:48:55]

Hill says he went through three campaigns with Billy Sunday to study his methods [2:48:55]. Sales meetings are revival meetings [2:49:48]. When National Cash Register salesmen lost heart over rumors about the company's finances, Hugh Chalmers had a Black shoeshine boy shine his shoes on a table. He contrasted the boy's earnings with his subsidized white predecessor's, and the salesmen roared that the fault was their own. They pledged five orders each, "Hugh Chalmers million dollar shoeshine" [2:50:19].

Seven stimuli and the psychology of clothes [2:55:02]

Enthusiasm "is never a matter of chance." Its stimuli are the work one loves; enthusiastic company; financial success; mastery of the fifteen laws; good health; knowing one has served others; and good clothes [2:55:02]. Broke after the war, Hill spent $675 on credit for clothes. He walked Michigan Boulevard daily at the hour a wealthy publisher passed until the man asked "who makes your clothes." Over lunch the publisher agreed to print and finance Hill's Golden Rule, more than $30,000 "raised on a front created mostly by good clothes" [2:56:11]. Edwin C. Barnes owned 31 suits "mostly for the impression they have on me" [3:03:28]. Of 3,000 salespeople Hill trained, every star producer understood clothes [3:06:43], and he prescribes at least ten suits [3:07:33]. Success, he says, attracts success [3:08:40]. He adds that nearly every achiever has some stimulus, mentioning James Whitcomb Riley's alcohol (which Hill says he does not recommend), Poe, Wilde and Ford's wife. He links this to the Master Mind, whose purpose is "stimulating the minds of all who participate" [3:09:01]. Self-Control, next, is "the balance wheel of this entire philosophy" [3:15:56].

The Seven Deadly Horsemen [3:16:23]

The essay names intolerance, greed, revenge, egotism, suspicion, jealousy and a seventh rider the reader must name. "The worst enemy that any man has is the one that walks around under his own hat" [3:16:32]. Intolerance has caused more wars than the other six together [3:18:05]. Greed and revenge ride as twins, and Hill points to Mexico and Spain [3:18:48]. So do egotism and suspicion, and the "it can't be done club" has "approximately 99 million members" [3:21:13]. Jealousy is called "dementia praecox." The seventh rider may be dishonesty, procrastination or "uncontrolled sex desire" [3:23:29]. Hill tells the reader to return in a month [3:27:19].
