Master Key: the Law of Success, Book 4

This text is a reading of Lessons 6 and 7 of Napoleon Hill's pioneering self-improvement work, The Law of Success (originally published in 1928). Read by Dan John Miller, this volume focuses on the twin principles of "Imagination" and "Enthusiasm," detailing how these mental faculties serve as the primary engines for transforming thoughts into material success.

Lesson 6: the power of constructive imagination

Napoleon Hill defines imagination as the "workshop of the human mind," where established facts and old ideas are reassembled into new combinations and put to new uses. Rather than viewing imagination merely as a tool for creating fiction, Hill argues it is the central hub of personal success, connecting a definite chief aim with self-confidence, initiative, and leadership.

Reassembling old ideas into new combinations

Hill emphasizes that "there is nothing new under the sun." Creative achievement relies on taking existing concepts and shifting them into new contexts: * Clarence Saunders: Created the Piggly-Wiggly self-help grocery store system simply by taking the self-help concept of a cafeteria and applying it to retail grocery stores. * Thomas A. Edison: Invented the incandescent light bulb by combining the known principle of electric resistance heating with the physical law that combustion cannot occur without oxygen, placing the filament inside an oxygen-free glass globe. * Paul Harris: Founded the Rotary Club as an imaginative, ethical way to extend his legal practice without violating professional advertising restrictions.

The interpretive capacity and telepathy

Hill asserts that the imagination possesses an interpretive capacity that acts like a radio receiver, registering thought waves and vibrations from outside sources. He attributes phenomena like "hunches" or "snap judgments" to stray telepathic vibrations.

To illustrate, Hill recalls waking up at 3:00 AM on Armistice Day in 1918 with an overwhelming, inexplicable feeling of peace and joy, which he claims was a result of millions of soldiers simultaneously releasing thoughts of joy, creating a worldwide telepathic "mob psychology."

Case studies in imaginative salesmanship

  • Securing B's prison release: Hill details how he secured a pardon for a former businessman, B, from the Governor of Ohio. While others failed by asking for favors based on B's prominent family, Hill used his imagination to put himself in the Governor's position. He presented B's release as a benefit to 160,000 other prisoners who could profit from the prison correspondence school system B had created.
  • The shepherd dog: A farmer failed to sell a shepherd dog by asking neighbors if they wanted a dog he wished to "get rid of." A man of imagination successfully sold the same dog for $100 to a crippled farmer by framing the dog as a valuable helper that could herd sheep and run errands.
  • Dr. Harper's million-dollar appeal: The former president of the University of Chicago, Dr. Harper, secured a million-dollar donation from a streetcar magnate by appealing to the man's desire to perpetuate his name and playing on his rivalry with a bitter enemy, rather than simply begging for university funds.
  • The life insurance alliance: A young insurance salesman built a $25,000-a-year business by partnering with local merchants (grocers, gas stations, builders) to offer free $1,000 life insurance policies as an incentive for customers who spent a certain amount with those businesses.

Lesson 7: the driving force of enthusiasm

Enthusiasm is described by Hill as a state of mind that inspires and arouses one to put action into the task at hand. He compares it to the steam that impels a locomotive. It is highly contagious, directly influencing both the person who possesses it and everyone within their radius.

The principle of suggestion and neutralizing the mind

Hill explains that "suggestion" is the principle through which your words, acts, and thoughts influence others. To successfully apply suggestion, a salesman or leader must first "neutralize" the prospect's mind—bringing it to a state of open, receptive confidence.

  • The two endorsement letters: When seeking book endorsements, Hill's first letter failed because it focused entirely on his own self-interest. His second letter succeeded because it appealed to the recipients' desire to help others, neutralizing their minds and turning their own consciences into his allies.
  • The magazine agents: A salesman who asked, "You wouldn't subscribe to help me out, would you?" invited a negative response. Conversely, a successful saleswoman neutralized Hill's mind by complimenting his library, discussing Emerson's essays, and making him feel that buying her magazines was a favor to himself.
  • The danger of negative suggestion: Hill cites an experiment in France where a condemned criminal was blindfolded and led to believe he was being guillotined; the physical shock of a cold plank and trickling warm water stopped his heart, proving that negative suggestion can kill.

The psychology of clothes and personal stimuli

Hill argues that successful people must discover personal stimuli to keep themselves in a high state of enthusiasm. Among these stimuli, Hill places a strong emphasis on the psychology of good clothes.

  • Hill's post-war wardrobe: Broke and in debt after WWI, Hill bought $675 worth of high-end clothes on credit. He argues that this appearance of prosperity not only gave him vital self-reliance but also attracted a wealthy publisher who financed his magazine, Hill's Golden Rule, with over $30,000 in capital.
  • Edwin C. Barnes: Edison's multi-millionaire partner maintained a wardrobe of 31 expensive suits—one for each day of the month—primarily for the positive psychological effect it had on his own mind.
  • Other historical stimuli: Hill notes that different figures used various stimuli to induce creative enthusiasm, such as James Whitcomb Riley (alcohol), Henry Ford (the love of his wife), and a prominent author who hired a female orchestra to play music while he wrote.

Hugh Chalmers' million-dollar shoeshine

When the National Cash Register Company faced financial trouble, its salesmen lost their enthusiasm and sales plummeted. Sales manager Hugh Chalmers called a convention and had a young boy shine his shoes while standing on top of a table in front of the crowd.

Chalmers pointed out that the boy's predecessor, a white boy subsidized with a salary, had failed to make a living, while this black boy made a great living and saved money under the exact same conditions. Chalmers used this demonstration to prove to his salesmen that their failure was due to their own lack of enthusiasm, not market conditions, saving the company from financial ruin.

The seven deadly horsemen of the mind

Hill warns that success is impossible if one harbors internal mental enemies. He visualizes these as seven unseen warriors riding through the human brain: 1. Intolerance: The most dangerous rider; it closes the mind to reason, facts, and logic. 2. Greed: Warps the brain to accumulate useless wealth at the expense of others. 3. Revenge: A deadly, double-edged sword that harms the wielder more than the foe. 4. Egotism: Leads to self-love and prevents genuine connection with others. 5. Suspicion: A prolific germ that destroys faith, which is necessary for enduring success. 6. Jealousy: A form of mental instability that ruins marriages, friendships, and businesses. 7. The Unnamed Rider: Left to the reader to name based on their own greatest weakness (e.g., dishonesty, procrastination, or uncontrolled desire).
