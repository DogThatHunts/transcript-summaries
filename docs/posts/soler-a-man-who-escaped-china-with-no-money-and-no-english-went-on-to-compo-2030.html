In this lecture at Columbia Business School, investor Li Lu shares his personal journey of escaping China with no money or English, only to build a highly successful career in value investing after accidentally attending a Warren Buffett lecture. He outlines the core tenets of value investing, explaining why true value investors represent a tiny, genetically distinct 5% minority of the market. Through detailed historical case studies of Timberland in 1998 and a South Korean company in the mid-2000s, Li Lu demonstrates how rigorous, investigative due diligence and the courage to stand alone can yield extraordinary, low-risk returns.

The genesis of a value investor

Li Lu reflects on his unexpected entry into the investment world. Having recently escaped China, he arrived in the United States deep in debt, with no connections, no money, and no familiarity with capitalist culture. His career trajectory changed entirely when he accidentally attended a Columbia Business School lecture by Warren Buffett.

Buffett's framework challenged Li Lu's preconceived notions of the stock market. He realized that investing was not about trading pieces of paper, but about understanding businesses. This realization provided a clear path for him to make a living and eventually launch his own fund.

The three pillars of value investing

Drawing on the teachings of Benjamin Graham, Li Lu defines the core attitudes that separate value investors from the rest of the market:

  • The owner's mentality: Viewing a stock not as a trading vehicle, but as fractional ownership of an underlying business.
  • Margin of safety: Demanding a significant discount to intrinsic value to protect against downside risk, particularly since minority shareholders lack control over the business.
  • The Mr. Market analogy: Recognizing that the stock market exists to serve the investor with liquidity, not to guide their valuation.

Li Lu notes that value investors manage less than 5% of all investment assets. The stock market is fundamentally designed for the other 95% of participants who trade frequently and focus on short-term performance. This structural discrepancy creates the mispricings that value investors exploit.

The evolutionary psychology of the herd

According to Li Lu, being a value investor requires a psychological "genetic mutation." Human evolution has hardwired people to seek safety in herds to survive threats. Standing alone in the market goes against tens of thousands of years of evolutionary biology.

To succeed, an investor must: * Be entirely comfortable being in the minority. * Rely strictly on cold reason and evidence rather than consensus. * Operate as an investigative journalist or academic researcher, driven by an insatiable curiosity about how the world works—spanning business, science, history, and literature.

Case study: Timberland (1998)

Li Lu illustrates his investment process using his 1998 investment in Timberland:

  • The discovery: He identified Timberland on ValueLine's "new low" list, trading around $28–$30 per share (a market cap of roughly $300 million).
  • The financials: The company had a clean book value of $230 million, with $275 million in working capital and $100 million in real estate. It generated $110 million in pre-tax earnings on $200 million of deployed capital—representing a 50% return on deployed capital.
  • The mispricing: The stock had collapsed due to the Asian financial crisis. Wall Street assumed all apparel and shoe brands with Asian exposure were doomed. However, Li Lu's research revealed that Timberland's Asian sales accounted for less than 10% of its business.
  • The due diligence: Because the company was 40% family-owned and controlled 98% of the voting rights, Wall Street analysts ignored it. Li Lu downloaded and read every court document from past shareholder lawsuits, discovering they stemmed from a single issue regarding missed earnings guidance. The owner had simply decided to stop talking to Wall Street altogether.
  • Verifying management: To assess the integrity of the family, Li Lu joined a board alongside the owner's son (the COO). He confirmed they were individuals of the highest integrity and brilliant operators.

Li Lu bought a massive position. Over the next two years, the stock rose sevenfold as earnings grew 30% annually and the valuation multiple expanded from 5x to 15x. He sold the position when Wall Street rediscovered the company and analyst meetings became overcrowded.

Case study: South Korean arbitrage (mid-2000s)

Li Lu presents a second case study of a South Korean company to test the students' analytical speed:

  • The valuation gap: The company traded at a market capitalization of roughly $60 million (5.5 million shares at $12).
  • Earnings and assets: It generated $25 million in net income and $31 million in pre-tax earnings, meaning it traded at just 2x pre-tax earnings. Its book value was $230 million, which included $180 million in tangible fixed assets.
  • The lesson: When assets and earnings are this heavily mispriced relative to market cap, the downside is heavily protected, presenting an obvious buying opportunity for an owner-minded investor.
