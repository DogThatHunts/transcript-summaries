The clash of narratives: Jackson Hole versus JOLTS

Last week's Federal Reserve gathering in Jackson Hole, Wyoming, jolted the markets with hawkish commentary from Kevin Warsh, who suggested that the Fed still has work to do on inflation. However, this hawkish narrative was quickly challenged by weak JOLTS (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey) data.

This tension highlights a growing divide between the Federal Reserve's perspective and the Treasury's outlook, represented by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. While Warsh and other Fed officials focus heavily on persistent inflation risks, Bessent argues that the economy has experienced a supply shock rather than demand-driven inflation. According to Bessent, raising rates into a supply shock is a mistake unless second- and third-order effects emerge—effects that have yet to materialize in the broader economy.

The Fed's forgotten third mandate

While the public is highly aware of the Fed's dual mandate—maximum employment and stable prices—Congress actually gave the Fed a third mandate during the reforms of the 1970s: maintaining moderate and stable interest rates.

The Fed rarely discusses this third mandate because officials assume that if they successfully manage employment and inflation, interest rates will naturally stabilize. However, the highly distorted, flattened, and inverted yield curves of recent years demonstrate that the Fed has failed to maintain interest rate stability, largely because it continues to misread the other two mandates.

The labor market illusion: why the official unemployment rate lies

The Federal Reserve remains hawkish because it takes the official unemployment rate at face value, viewing the labor market as highly resilient. However, a deeper look at the JOLTS data reveals a fragile labor market characterized by a severe lack of hiring:

  • Hiring rates are near historic lows: The hiring rate has fallen to levels comparable to the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis and the sluggish recovery of the early 2010s.
  • The participation rate trap: When the hiring rate drops below approximately 2%, workers realize there are few opportunities and stop looking for work. If a worker stops searching for more than four weeks, the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) removes them from the labor force.
  • Underestimating unemployment: By ignoring these labor force dropouts, the official unemployment rate artificially looks healthy, leading central bankers to believe the economy is strong when it is actually fragile.

This lack of hiring, rather than active layoffs, is the primary economic headwind. The JOLTS survey showed negative net turnover for the third consecutive month, suggesting that upcoming payroll reports may continue to surprise to the downside.

TIPS break-evens and the reality of demand destruction

Despite rising oil and gasoline prices, the market is not pricing in long-term, 1970s-style inflation. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) break-even rates remain remarkably low.

This market pricing supports Bessent's view: the energy spike is a temporary supply shock, not a structural inflationary spiral. In a fragile economic environment, higher energy costs lead to demand destruction—where consumers spend less on other goods and services to pay for fuel—ultimately exerting a disinflationary force on the economy.

This trend is also visible in Europe. Although the headline inflation rate in Europe recently hit a three-year high due to energy costs, core inflation unexpectedly decelerated to 2.4%, confirming that the price pressures are narrow and failing to spill over into the wider economy.

Yield curve mechanics: why long-term rates aren't actually soaring

While mainstream media outlets frequently report that long-term bond yields are "soaring" to multi-decade highs, the real question is why they are not much higher given massive government deficit spending and high short-term Fed rates.

The data shows that the bond market is not rejecting U.S. debt:

  • Shrinking spreads: The spread between the 30-year long bond and the 3-month Treasury bill has shrunk from 379 basis points in 2011 (when national debt was $14.3 trillion) to just 134 basis points today (with debt over $40 trillion).
  • No term premium rise: The market is not demanding extra compensation to hold long-term U.S. debt, and TIC data shows that foreign private buyers continue to purchase Treasuries.

The modest rise in long-term yields is not driven by inflation fears or Treasury rejection, but by yield curve mechanics. Normally, during a rate-cutting cycle, the yield curve un-inverts as the Fed rapidly cuts short-term rates. However, because the Fed is stubbornly keeping short-term rates high due to its inflation fixation, the un-inversion process is being forced from the long end, gently pushing long-term rates up. If the Fed continues to push against market realities, it risks forcing the yield curve to reinvert entirely.
