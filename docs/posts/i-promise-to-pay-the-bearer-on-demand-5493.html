The three stages of the monetary cycle

Dave Silver outlines an overarching framework for understanding the trajectory of fiat regimes throughout history, dividing monetary development into three sequential stages:

  1. Convertible currency: Promissory paper notes are fully redeemable for fixed weights of precious metal, establishing initial public confidence.
  2. Suspended convertibility: Governments issue excess notes and break the metallic link—such as the 1971 decision by President Richard Nixon to halt dollar-to-gold redemption—leaving an unbacked fiat regime in place while the public remains habituated to using the notes.
  3. Systemic revaluation: Accelerating money printing forces a sharp crisis where confidence evaporates, causing physical gold and silver to revalue drastically upwards to balance the expanded note supply before returning to stage one.

Dave Silver predicts that the global economy is nearing the end of stage two, suggesting that by the late 2020s or 2030, economic dislocations will force a transition to stage three and ultimately return to sound, asset-backed units of exchange, potentially through initiatives like the BRICS currency alliances.

Contested claims on self-authored promissory notes and debt

Richard and Dave Silver discuss tactics popular within sovereignty and freeman-on-the-land movements regarding the settlement of personal liabilities, such as council tax or collection agency demands.

Dave Silver asserts that because Bank of England banknotes carry the phrase "I promise to pay the bearer on demand" and operate as legal tender cash, private promissory notes function identically under UK commercial statutes. He cites the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 and a 1969 statement from Lord Denning that bills of exchange should be treated as cash. Under Dave Silver's method, a debtor facing a bailiff creates a handwritten promise for the debt amount minus £5, laminates a physical £5 note to the paper, and delivers it to the collector. He contends that if the collector takes the paper or refuses it, the debt is legally discharged under commercial law, and subsequent credit reporting disputes can be filed via official fraud portals. Mainstream legal systems and consumer finance regulators treat these theories as legally meritless, but Dave Silver insists the mechanism operates within statutory definitions provided no common-law harm occurs.

Industrial demand, depletion, and alleged silver manipulation

Examining the physical silver market, Dave Silver argues that silver's availability is fundamentally diverging from that of gold due to unrecoverable industrial consumption:

  • Photovoltaic consumption: Newer high-efficiency solar designs, such as heterojunction panels, require significantly higher quantities of silver per watt than earlier PERC technology, placing enormous strain on annual silver output.
  • Declining ore quality: Mining operations are extracting ores with sharply reduced silver concentrations compared to historical standards, rendering future mine production more energy-intensive and expensive.
  • Paper shorting and market manipulation: Dave Silver argues that institutions trade derivative silver contracts representing multiples of the entire global annual mine supply, creating artificial downward pressure on spot prices. He highlights the US Department of Justice's 2020 settlement against JPMorgan Chase over precious metals spoofing as evidence that institutional market suppression is an established fact rather than mere speculation.
