How a rigid medieval legal system forced citizens to appeal directly to the king's conscience, giving rise to the parallel legal framework of equity.

Historically, English common law was incredibly rigid, turning away anyone whose grievance did not fit a pre-printed form. To find justice, citizens petitioned the king directly, leading the Lord Chancellor to judge cases based on conscience rather than strict rules. This alternative path to justice evolved into "equity," a parallel system designed to step in whenever strict legal rules produce an unfair result.

Key takeaways

  • The rigidity of common law: Early English courts required a specific "writ" for every case; if a dispute did not fit an existing template, the court could not offer a remedy.
  • The rise of petitions: Citizens bypassed the rigid court system by appealing directly to the king's sense of fairness.
  • The Lord Chancellor's role: The king delegated these petitions to the Lord Chancellor, who acted as the "keeper of the king's conscience" and focused on doing the right thing rather than following rigid forms.
  • A parallel system: Equity developed alongside common law, acting as a corrective mechanism when strict legal rules would produce an unjust result.
  • The birth of the trust: This focus on fairness eventually led to the creation of the trust, one of equity's most significant legal innovations.
