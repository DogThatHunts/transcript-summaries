Michael Burry releases biggest warning yet: "it's like the final months of 1999"

RE-V reports on hedge fund manager Michael Burry's stark comparison between current market conditions and the lead-up to the 2008 housing crash and the 1999–2000 dot-com bubble. Burry argues that stock prices have disconnected from economic fundamentals, driven by a two-letter thesis—AI—that everyone believes they understand. The analysis reveals troubling parallels: the Shiller P/E ratio stands at 42, matching only the 1999–2000 peak; the U.S. personal savings rate has fallen to 3.6%, near record lows; and consumers are increasingly leveraging stock portfolios to fund spending rather than saving. Yet the video also notes a crucial counterpoint: corporate earnings remain robust, growing 25% year-over-year in Q1 2026, and historically, major stock crashes have not occurred while earnings were rising.

Key insights

  1. Stock valuations at historic extremes. The Shiller P/E ratio of 42 is comparable only to 1999–2000, while the historical average over 130 years is 16–17, signaling rare overvaluation relative to inflation-adjusted earnings.

  2. Personal savings collapse mirrors late-1990s behavior. At 3.6%, the U.S. savings rate is near record lows, driven by consumers spending against rising stock portfolios rather than income—the same pattern that preceded the dot-com crash.

  3. Earnings growth may be a stabilizing force. Unlike in 1999, companies are posting strong revenue and earnings growth (25% year-over-year), partly due to AI-driven cost cuts. Historically, major crashes require earnings to roll over first.

  4. Stock market correction would cascade to housing. A significant stock decline would force baby boomers and other portfolio holders to reassess their real estate holdings, potentially flooding the market with sellers and deflating inflated home prices.

  5. Regional housing markets already showing stress. Chattanooga, Tennessee exhibits signs of correction: inventory is 2.4× the long-term average, prices have declined, and valuations are disconnected from local incomes (e.g., $495,000 one-bedroom condos with $3,700/month payments).

The AI bubble and disconnect from fundamentals

RE-V emphasizes Burry's core claim: stock movements no longer respond logically to economic data like jobs or consumer sentiment. Instead, the market is driven by a single narrative—artificial intelligence—that has become detached from underlying business performance. This mirrors the late-1990s obsession with ".com" companies, many of which had no path to profitability.

The savings rate signal

The personal savings rate is presented as a leading indicator of market stress. In the late 1990s, as savings collapsed, consumers were spending based on inflated stock portfolios rather than earned income. Today's 3.6% rate suggests the same dynamic is at play, with the stock market's continued rise masking underlying household fragility.

Earnings growth: the counterargument

The video notes that Burry may be overstating the disconnect. Q1 2026 earnings grew 25% year-over-year, one of the strongest seasons in five years. Companies are genuinely growing revenue and cutting costs through automation. Historically, stock prices have not crashed while earnings were expanding, suggesting an imminent correction is not guaranteed.

Potential paradox: buyer demand after a crash

Counterintuitively, RE-V suggests that a stock market correction could increase real estate buyer demand. After the 2001 NASDAQ collapse (70% drawdown), home sales surged as investors rotated out of equities into property. However, the narrator notes a critical difference: the housing market in 2000–2001 was fairly valued, whereas today it is significantly overvalued. A crash would thus bring both increased buyer interest and a flood of panicked sellers, likely resulting in net price declines.

Uncertainty and caveats

RE-V acknowledges that bubbles can persist longer than expected and that no one can predict the exact timing of a correction. The video emphasizes that while the data suggests bubble conditions, the resilience of corporate earnings and consumer spending means the market could continue rising in the near term. The key takeaway is that real estate buyers and sellers should understand these macro dynamics when making decisions.
