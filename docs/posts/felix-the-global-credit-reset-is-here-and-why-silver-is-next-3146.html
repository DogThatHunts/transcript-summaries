Felix | The Global Credit Reset Is Here (and Why Silver is Next)

Economist Felix Preen explains how rising borrowing costs are quietly fracturing corporate debt and why a leverage shakeout in silver presents a major contrarian opportunity.

The mechanics of the credit reset

Felix argues that the fifteen-year era of cheap credit is officially over, marking a fundamental "credit reset" where the price of money is returning to historical norms. In the summer of 2020, the US government could borrow for ten years at roughly 0.5%. Recently, the US auctioned $39 billion in ten-year notes at 5.3%, marking the highest borrowing cost since 2000.

With US national debt rising from $23 trillion in 2020 to $40 trillion today, cheap debt must continually be refinanced at today's much higher rates. This rapidly increases the government's interest bill, which already exceeds Pentagon spending. Felix asserts that governments facing massive interest bills historically choose between cutting spending, raising taxes, or printing money. He argues the US has chosen the third option, pointing to a 51% increase in the M2 money supply since the pandemic and a 30% rise in official consumer prices.

Cracks in the corporate bond market

While the mainstream media focuses on all-time highs in the stock market, Felix points to severe distress in the corporate bond market as the first sign of systemic trouble. He highlights four key bond developments showing unprecedented stress:

  • Oracle: Bonds issued to fund AI data centers have fallen to 82 cents on the dollar, pushing yields over 8%. The cost to insure Oracle's debt against default has quadrupled over the past year.
  • SpaceX: Recently issued bonds paying nearly 7% interest have collapsed to an all-time low of 85 cents.
  • Meta: Bonds financing Meta's Hyperion data center (Project Bainais) have dropped from 110 cents to 91 cents over the past year.
  • Paramount: The cost of insuring Paramount's debt against default has surged to levels not seen since the 2008 financial crisis.

The stock market's AI illusion

Felix warns that the apparent strength of the stock market is an illusion driven by extreme concentration. According to JP Morgan data cited by Felix, AI-related stocks now make up 49% of the S&P 500 (up from 26% at the start of 2023) and account for 70% of the market's gains this year.

Underneath this giant-led surface, Morgan Stanley data reveals a quiet bear market. Since June, 89% of stocks in the Russell 3000 have fallen more than 10% from their peaks, with over half down by more than 20%. Felix argues that the index looks calm only because a handful of tech giants are holding it up, while the broader market is already experiencing a credit-driven downturn.

Why the silver sell-off is a leverage flush

Addressing the recent $1.6 billion institutional exit from silver, Felix argues this sell-off is not a sign of declining fundamental demand, but rather a forced liquidation of leveraged positions. He explains that trend-following funds (CTAs) and highly leveraged traders are forced to sell automatically when prices dip to meet margin calls. For example, a trader using 10x leverage loses half their capital on a mere 5% price drop, triggering automatic broker liquidations that push prices down further.

Once these "weak hands" are shaken out, Felix expects silver and gold to perform well. Because precious metals are priced in dollars, they stand to benefit when the Federal Reserve is ultimately forced to print more money to prevent a broader credit collapse.

The contrarian case for long-term bonds

Despite bonds being widely unloved—highlighted by a recent Economist cover asking if bonds will blow up—Felix views them as a compelling contrarian opportunity. With 30-year government bonds yielding 5.6% (the highest in over 20 years), investors can lock in historically high yields from a reliable borrower.

Furthermore, because bond prices and yields move in opposite directions, any future interest rate cuts by the Fed to alleviate credit stress would cause long-term bond prices to rise significantly. Felix notes that a 1% drop in the 30-year yield would result in an approximate 16% capital gain for the bond, pointing to the TLT ETF as a common vehicle to access this trade.
