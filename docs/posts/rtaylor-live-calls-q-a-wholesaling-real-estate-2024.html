Key insights

  • Wholesaling is a practice, not a permanent business model: Taylor views wholesaling as an apprenticeship to learn what active flippers want and build working capital, rather than a stable long-term operation, due to its vulnerability to buyer dropouts, hidden inspection defects, and third-party delays.
  • Securing private capital requires skin in the game: While personal guarantees are impractical when assigning wholesale contracts, Taylor readily offers personal guarantees on his own fix-and-flips because he controls the contractor, the rehab scope, and the underwriting.
  • Percentage discounts matter far more than absolute dollar discounts: A $30,000 price drop on a $100,000 home represents an actionable 30% wholesaling spread, whereas that same $30,000 discount on a $450,000 asset is barely 6.7%, offering insufficient margin for investors.
  • Reverse-engineer deals around verified buyers: Securing properties under contract without an existing buyer network turns wholesaling into low-probability speculation; investors must first identify active local buyers, their criteria, and their capital limits before hunting for inventory.
  • Concessions bridge inspection disconnects: When older properties (especially century-old Midwest homes) fail to meet rigid investor expectations, offering a negotiated seller concession (e.g., 5%) often rescues deals that would otherwise collapse over deferred maintenance.
