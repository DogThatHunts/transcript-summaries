Wall Street is quietly selling AI (and nobody's told you)

The core mechanism: fees without risk

Felix Niklaus Breen, an investment banker and founder of the Prune Institute, argues that the current AI lending boom is not primarily a technology story—it's a money story about who pays, who takes risk, and who walks away with fees.

The business model is straightforward: when Goldman Sachs and JP Morgan arranged a $5 billion loan for Volta Infrastructure (a company buying 36,000 Nvidia GPUs), they earned 2–3% upfront—roughly $100–150 million per deal. The moment the paperwork was signed, the fees went into their accounts. They then sold most of the loan ($3.7 billion of $5 billion) to pension funds and insurance companies on a "best efforts" basis, meaning the banks had no obligation to guarantee buyers. The risk migrated to institutional investors managing retirement savings; the fee stayed with the banks.

Breen compares this to a car salesman earning commission regardless of whether the buyer loves or regrets the purchase. The transaction is the product, not the outcome.

The information environment: conflicting signals from one institution

Goldman Sachs' research analysts published a strong buy recommendation on Meta at $885 per share (16% upside from current levels). Simultaneously, Goldman's own trading desk sent a note to institutional clients saying investors were "de-risking from AI capex" and "AI financing exposure"—in effect, selling.

This is not a contradiction; it reflects different audiences and incentives:

  • Research analysts generate trading fees and support relationships with companies seeking future deals (bond offerings, share placements). Their price targets create sentiment and justify new transactions.
  • Trading desks manage real money and watch actual capital flows. They signal to sophisticated clients who are already nervous about AI exposure.
  • Investment bankers arrange deals and collect fees regardless of outcomes.

Breen argues that price targets function as "infrastructure for the transaction machine," keeping sentiment warm and justifying new deals. The analyst may not be lying—Meta could reach $885—but the recommendation serves a commercial function inside a fee-generating system.

The Volta Infrastructure deal: a case study in risk transfer

Volta Infrastructure, founded in 2026 with origins in Bitcoin mining and backed by BitDeer, is pivoting to AI. It signed a six-year computing contract with Anthropic and is buying 36,000 Nvidia GPUs.

Key details revealing market skepticism:

  • The loan carries an 11% interest rate, compared to 8% for typical junk bonds. The 3% premium signals the market views this as high-risk.
  • Goldman and JP Morgan kept only $1.3 billion (backed by leases and cash) and put $3.7 billion on a "best efforts" basis—meaning they did not guarantee placement.
  • Nvidia is an investor in Volta, so the chipmaker is a part-owner of the company borrowing money at 11% to buy its own chips. Breen notes this is "not a normal arrangement."

Broader signals of stress in AI financing

Oracle's data center debt: - Oracle took a $38 billion construction loan for data centers in Texas and Wisconsin. - Six months in, $1 billion remains unsold. - Original lenders are selling their portion at a discount to exit the position. - Oracle's debt is trading below face value, and bankruptcy insurance costs are at their highest level since 2009.

Bank behavior: - JP Morgan and Morgan Stanley are moving AI-related loans off their balance sheets faster than they can justify. - Both are approaching limits on how much AI financing they will hold. - Meta moved billions of dollars of AI investment off its main balance sheet into special purpose vehicles (SPVs)—essentially hiding the debt.

Market conditions: - Interest rates on AI lending are rising. - AI borrowing is driving up mortgage rates and other consumer interest rates. - Buyers of AI debt are becoming less sophisticated—now including life insurance companies rather than seasoned institutional investors.

The pattern: risk migration in credit cycles

Breen argues this pattern repeats in every major credit cycle: 2006–2007 (mortgage-backed securities), 2014 (energy lending), and now 2026 (AI infrastructure).

The mechanism is consistent: 1. Early-stage lenders (Goldman, JP Morgan) earn fees and distribute risk to later buyers. 2. Sophisticated institutional clients (traders, early investors) recognize the slowdown and de-risk. 3. Less sophisticated buyers (pension funds, insurance companies, life insurers) enter late in the cycle. 4. When the cycle turns, losses concentrate among those least prepared.

Breen emphasizes this is not a prediction of imminent collapse, but rather a recognition that "the music will slow" before it stops. By the time obvious problems appear in news headlines, sophisticated money has already exited.

The energy connection: AI and the petrodollar

Breen argues that the AI story and the oil story are interconnected. Data centers require massive amounts of electricity. If AI financing slows, energy projects that depend on computing power will stall. When those projects stall, oil prices respond in ways that traders watch carefully. He frames this as a skill most people were never taught but can learn.

What to watch for

The information is public but requires understanding the vocabulary: - "Best efforts" clauses in loan agreements - Credit default swap pricing (insurance on bankruptcy risk) - Divergence between research analysts' recommendations and trading desk signals - Interest rate levels on AI-specific debt - Who is holding the risk on balance sheets

Breen argues that Wall Street is "not out to get you" but is "there for the fee." When fees stop justifying the risk, banks move on and the risk remains in the system, migrating toward the least prepared participants.
