The migration collapse and affordability crisis

RE-V emphasizes that Florida's housing crisis stems from a fundamental mismatch: prices were inflated by out-of-state migration during 2020–2022, but that migration has reversed sharply. Domestic migration into Florida is now at its fourth-lowest level in 35 years, down 93% from the 2022 peak. With local median incomes around $70,000–$80,000, buyers cannot afford homes still priced at $400,000–$500,000, even after 16% declines.

The rental market reflects this stress. RE-V documents a three-bedroom, 2,200-square-foot home renting for $1,900/month—only $200 more than it rented for in 2017, nearly a decade earlier. Landlords are offering one month free to fill vacancies. Vacancy rates across Florida's housing market are "through the roof," with newly built homes sitting empty.

Cape Coral: the epicenter

RE-V focuses on Cape Coral as the hardest-hit market. Home values there are down 16% from the 2022 peak—the third-largest metro-level downturn in the US, behind Austin (–26.7%) and Punta Gorda (–22%). However, individual neighborhoods within Cape Coral range from –15% to –25%.

The visual landscape reflects distress: half-finished homes, vacant lots, short sales, and new construction with "100% financing available" signs. RE-V argues that this mirrors 2008 conditions, though analysts have repeatedly claimed such a crash was impossible in this cycle.

The builder problem: easy money and abandoned projects

During the 2020–2022 boom, Cape Coral experienced a construction surge. While building has slowed, it has not stopped—construction levels remain higher than pre-pandemic. However, many projects are stalled or abandoned.

RE-V cites a Bloomberg article on "easy money lending crushing rookie home flippers" in Cape Coral. Amateur real estate investors bought vacant lots for $30,000–$50,000, then financed construction with hard-money loans at 8% interest and 90% leverage. This strategy worked when values were rising, but now that prices are falling, builders cannot sell completed homes for enough to cover construction costs. A builder interviewed in the Bloomberg piece reported losses of ~$75,000 per property. RE-V observes multiple half-built homes on his drive through Cape Coral, questioning whether some will ever be completed.

Foreclosures and short sales

Short sales—where owners sell below the mortgage balance to avoid foreclosure—are spiking. RE-V explains the distinction between lender-approved short sales (faster closing, lender has pre-approved the price) and non-lender-approved short sales (lender may negotiate after the buyer goes under contract, potentially extending the timeline to 3–4 months).

Examples include: - A short sale purchased in 2024 for $260,000, now listed at $185,000 (a $75,000 loss). - A condo purchased in 2023 for $800,000, now listed at $440,000 (a $360,000 loss).

RE-V notes that such pricing—$185,000 for a home in Cape Coral—has not been seen since before the pandemic, reflecting the magnitude of the correction.

Regional variation and the role of hurricanes

While the entire state of Florida is experiencing year-over-year value declines, the west coast has been hit hardest. Hurricane Ian (2022) and Hurricane Milton (2024) "served as an accelerator" of the downturn, lowering demand and migration further. Southwest Florida metros (Sarasota, Punta Gorda, Cape Coral, Fort Myers) show 15–25% metro-level declines.

Miami-Dade and Palm Beach have been more insulated. Miami-Dade values remain up double digits since mid-2022, and Palm Beach has begun recovering over the last six months. However, RE-V notes that even within these counties, zip-code-level variation is significant: some neighborhoods still have bidding wars, while others show price cuts and negotiation room.

Home sales volume and demand collapse

Home sales in Florida in July 2026 totaled 32,100—about 30% below the pandemic peak, 20% below 2019, and 11% below the long-term average. This low demand, intersecting with foreclosures, mortgage distress, and declining values, creates a "bad situation" in the housing market.

Florida's property tax reform (Amendment 3)

On November 3rd, Florida voters will decide on Amendment 3, a property tax relief measure. If passed, it would increase the homestead exemption to $250,000 for non-school property taxes by 2028, indexed for inflation. For a home purchased at $185,000, this would eliminate non-school property taxes entirely.

RE-V calculates that for a typical Cape Coral home, this would reduce tax bills by 40%, saving owners ~$1,700 per year. However, school taxes would remain unchanged. He expects a modest uptick in demand in early 2027 if the amendment passes, but doubts it will fundamentally alter the market, given affordability constraints and continued low migration.

Importantly, the exemption is restricted to residents who have lived in Florida for at least five years, limiting its immediate impact on new migrants.

The 2008 comparison and future risks

RE-V argues that current conditions mirror 2008: 30–40% value declines, short sales, foreclosures, abandoned construction, and 100% financing offers. However, he notes a critical difference: the stock market remains at all-time highs, and there has been no full-scale economic recession.

He expresses concern about tail risks. If the stock market corrects 30% in the next two years, or if unemployment rises two percentage points and triggers a recession, further downside is likely—especially given the "crazy stuff" in home building. Cape Coral, as a major retirement destination with many residents on fixed incomes and stock portfolios, would be particularly vulnerable.
