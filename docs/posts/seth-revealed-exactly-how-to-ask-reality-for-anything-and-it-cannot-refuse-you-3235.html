The woman they called crazy

Yet decades later, experiments in quantum physics, neuroscience, and consciousness studies would begin validating the very claims she'd been ridiculed for making.

Roberts never claimed to be a guru. She remained skeptical of her own experience, questioned the material openly, and refused to profit from her role as Seth's channel. The foundation: consciousness comes first

Seth's core claim contradicts mainstream neuroscience: your brain does not produce consciousness; it receives it. Think of a radio. If you'd never seen one before, you'd swear the music lives inside the box. Smash it and the music stops—but the signal was always in the air. The radio was only translating it. Seth said the brain works the same way: consciousness is the broadcast, the brain is the receiver. Death, in this model, is a radio going quiet, not a station going off the air.

This idea wasn't new. In 1898, Harvard psychologist William James suggested the brain might transmit consciousness rather than create it. Aldous Huxley later described the brain as a "reducing valve," narrowing a much larger awareness down to what you need to survive. But no one had mapped it in this much detail, and no one had a poet in upstate New York doing it twice a week for twenty years.

Science still cannot explain why there is anything it feels like to see the color red—a problem philosopher David Chalmers named "the hard problem of consciousness" in 1995. Three decades later, there is still no accepted answer. Seth's answer: we've been looking in the wrong place.

Terminal lucidity offers a strange parallel. Patients with severe dementia—people who haven't recognized their own children in years—suddenly wake up with clear speech, names, and memories, often right before death. The National Institute on Aging held an expert workshop on it in 2018, yet nobody knows why it happens. If the brain is a failing receiver, a sudden clearing is exactly what you'd expect.

Near-death experiences push on the same bruise. Critics argue the brain may be active in ways monitors miss—a fair point. But the question remains: if the brain is the source, why does awareness show up when the source looks offline?

The double-slit experiment and the observer

In the double-slit experiment, particles fired one at a time at a barrier with two slits don't land in two neat piles. They build up a striped wave pattern, as if each particle went through both slits at once. But put a detector at the slits to see which way each one goes, and the stripes vanish. The particles start behaving like tiny bullets.

Watch them and they behave one way. Don't watch them and they behave another.

Textbooks say the word "observer" in physics doesn't mean a person—it means any measurement, any interaction that records which way the particle went. A detector counts. But here's what they say far less loudly: nobody agrees on where the measurement stops and the mind begins. It's called the measurement problem, and after about a hundred years, it remains unsolved.

Some serious physicists—John von Neumann and Eugene Wigner among them—took seriously the idea that consciousness is part of the chain. Most physicists today reject that. It remains a live argument, not a settled fact.

The timeline that should bother you

Entanglement. In 1935, Einstein and two colleagues argued that quantum mechanics must be incomplete because it implied two particles could stay linked across any distance. Einstein later called it "spooky action at a distance." In 1964, physicist John Bell showed the question could be tested. In 1972, John Clauser ran the first test. In 1982, Alain Aspect ran the version that made physicists take it seriously. The result? Einstein was wrong. The particles stay correlated in a way that no ordinary, local explanation can account for. In 2022, Aspect, Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for it.

You can't use entanglement to send a message faster than light. What it shows is that separation is not what we thought it was. Seth described a universe where separation is a surface appearance—published a decade before Aspect confirmed that separation doesn't work the way anyone believed.

The delayed choice experiment. In 1978, physicist John Wheeler proposed whether a choice made after a particle has passed through the apparatus could change how it behaved. In 2007, a team in France ran a version in the lab, and the results were consistent with quantum prediction. Wheeler concluded that we live in what he called a "participatory universe"—not a stage you stand in front of, but a system you're part of. That was one of the most respected physicists of the century, saying out loud what Jane Roberts had been dismissed for saying in a living room.

The practices: how to use this

Seth left a set of practices. Everything in this section comes from Seth's own instructions, not from wellness trends. Here's the system:

The night suggestion

Before you fall asleep at night, give yourself a suggestion. In Seth's words: "I can remember my dreams and write them down in the morning." That's it, one sentence.

Then imagine your dream notebook lying open in the morning with the dreams already written in it. Not the dream itself—the notebook. You're not forcing yourself to dream anything; you're rehearsing the act of remembering.

If you're a heavy sleeper, add a second suggestion: "I'll wake up in order to remember."

Why did Seth care so much about this? Because he taught that suggestions given before sleep improve your chances of becoming aware inside the dream itself. He told Rob to instruct a part of himself to stay alert during sleep, and he gave students a simple anchor for it: hold the image of a candle as you go to bed.

How to use it: Put a notebook and pen beside your bed. As the lights go out, lie quietly and say Seth's sentence slowly, a few times. See the open notebook in your mind. If you sleep deep, add the second line. Then let go. For the first few days, you may get nothing—that's normal. Even a fragment counts. Give it two weeks.

The morning catch

In the morning, before you move, before you reach for anything, write whatever's there. An image, a feeling, a single word. Don't interpret it; don't make it make sense. Just catch it.

Over a couple of weeks, something happens: you begin to see the same images, the same problems, the same fears showing up in different costumes. Students of the Seth material describe the dream state as the place where your beliefs run without supervision—which is exactly what we're about to go hunting for.

The belief catch

Seth's biggest idea about changing your life is about beliefs, and it's the heart of his book The Nature of Personal Reality. Your beliefs aren't buried in some vault in your unconscious; they're sitting in plain sight, like furniture you stopped noticing years ago. You've just never written them down.

Take a sheet of paper and list what you believe: about yourself, about money, about work, about love, about your body. Start wherever your life keeps repeating itself. Don't judge them; just get them out of your head and onto the page. Seth said that simply listing them can begin to dissolve barriers you didn't know you'd built.

Then follow your feelings. Seth taught that emotions grow out of beliefs. So when a familiar feeling shows up—that tight chest, that flash of shame—treat it like a trail. Ask what you'd have to believe for that feeling to make sense. Whatever answer comes, write it on the list.

The belief swap

Choose one limiting belief from your list. Then write one simple sentence that captures the opposite. For no more than 10 minutes a day, close your eyes and repeat that sentence aloud, slowly, feeling what it means. When your mind wanders, bring it back.

Seth was firm about this: don't strain, don't push. Ten minutes, every day.

Here's a warning Seth gave that most people skip: positive thinking fails when it ignores the habitual negative feelings underneath it. If the sentence makes you flinch, it's the wrong sentence. Experiment with the wording until it feels simple and true enough to say. Many students point to one belief as the foundation: "I am worthy and deserving." Start there if it fits.

The symbolic action

A belief you only think is a wish. A belief you act on is a decision. So take one small action today that you would not have taken under the old belief. Make the call, say the thing, spend the money you'd normally hold back. Keep it small and keep it sane. The point is to show yourself, in the physical world, that the new belief is already running.

Why today? Because of the lines Seth repeated more than any other in that book: "The point of power is in the present." You can't change the past; you can't act in the future. The only place any belief has ever been installed or removed is right now.

The seven-day test

Run a seven-day test and make it Seth's own: - Each night, give yourself the suggestion. - Each morning, write down whatever dream fragment you have. - Each day, spend your 10 minutes with your belief sentence and take one small action that proves it. - Keep a log: three lines a day—what you intended, what you noticed, and what didn't happen. The misses count exactly as much as the hits.

Why the misses matter: People who try Seth's methods report coincidences that echo their intentions, people responding to them differently, and opportunities that seem to come from nowhere. Maybe. But the moment you start looking for something, your attention finds it. That's how attention works. Seth would say, that's the point. A log with the misses in it lets you tell the difference between a pattern and a wish.

You're not looking for a miracle on day two. You're looking for a shift over a week.

Expect pushback. Old habits argue back. Don't take that as a sign it's working. Take it as information and adjust your sentence. Seth said finding the right wording takes experimenting, and that's allowed.

The trap: desperation. Seth capped the belief exercise at 10 minutes a day, and he told people not to strain. Working harder is just another way of telling yourself the problem is enormous. Care enough to do it. Relax enough not to grip it.

The pause scan

Before any conversation that matters, take a single breath and check your own body first. A tight chest, a clenched jaw, a held breath. Then notice how that changes the moment the other person enters. Treat what you notice as information, not a verdict. It's your body reading the room.

After an encounter that leaves you drained, spend 60 seconds on a slow exhale, feel your feet on the floor, and name one thing that belongs only to you.

The view from the end

This is one you do tonight, once. Lie down before sleep and take a few slow breaths. Then picture yourself at the end of a very long life—safe, old, looking back. Not the dying part; the looking back.

Ask yourself three things: - What did I spend too long worrying about? - Who did I love and not tell? - What did I come here to do?

Keep a notebook beside the bed and write one or two sentences. Don't edit them. Whatever shows up, that's your inner self talking.

If you're in a very dark place right now, skip this one tonight and talk to someone you trust. That's not weakness; that's the smartest move you can make.

The unsent letter

If you've lost someone, write them a letter—the one you never got to send. Tell them what you didn't say. Then put the pen down and sit quietly for a few minutes and see what comes.

Seth said they can hear you. Whether or not that's true, grief researchers have found that for many people, keeping an inner relationship with the person who died helps more than cutting it off. It's called continuing bonds.

The authority check

Pick one belief about your own life that you've never questioned—like "I'm not a numbers person" or "people like me don't get that" or "that's just how I am."

Then ask where it came from, not what it is. Who told you? A teacher? A parent? A doctor? A bad year?

Then ask the question Semmelweis' critics never did: "What would change my mind? What evidence would I accept?" Give it five minutes, with a pen, once this week.

Note: This is for beliefs about yourself and your limits, not for your medical care. Questioning a belief is not the same as ignoring your doctor.

The expectation column

If you started the 7-day log from earlier, add one thing: take two columns.

In the first, write down what you expected to happen in a moment that mattered—a meeting, a conversation, a drive in traffic.

In the second, write what actually happened.

After a week, look for the gap. Not to prove a theory, but to see how much of your experience was handed to you by your own prediction. Call it the expectation column.
