Roger Sayles outlines his legal theory claiming individuals can escape federal administrative jurisdiction and debt obligations by declaring status as American nationals rather than Fourteenth Amendment citizens.

Host Deanna Spingola interviews author and researcher Roger Sayles on the concepts behind his book From Sovereign to Serf. Sayles presents his sovereign citizen–adjacent framework, asserting that standard "U.S. citizenship" under the Fourteenth Amendment is an ongoing system of voluntary feudal servitude. He maintains that through linguistic manipulation ("equivocation") in birth certificates, residency forms, and tax statutes, the federal government tricks people into consenting to administrative jurisdiction. He claims that individuals can opt out of this system by executing a formal affidavit declaring their status as a non-citizen "national" rather than a citizen of the United States.

Key insights

  • The binary of political status: Sayles asserts that all political conditions reduce to being either completely free or in voluntary bondage, comparing modern statutory citizenship to feudal serfdom.
  • Linguistic "equivocation" in law: According to Sayles, the legal system relies on using everyday colloquial words (such as "resident" or "property") with narrow, technical statutory definitions to establish administrative power without the public realizing it.
  • The Fourteenth Amendment argument: Sayles argues that the Fourteenth Amendment created a specialized federal citizenship intended originally for freed slaves, which modern Americans inadvertently assume by checking boxes on official paperwork.
  • Securitization of birth certificates: Sayles claims that birth certificates function as commercial "warehouse receipts," collateralizing future citizen labor to back the issuance of government bonds and paper currency.
  • Rebutting the presumption of citizenship: Sayles contends that filing an affidavit declaring intention to be an "American national, but not a citizen of the United States" severs federal administrative jurisdiction and exempts individuals from most domestic tax and regulatory codes.

The feudal model and modern citizenship

Sayles traces the development of modern Anglo-American jurisdiction back to the fall of Rome and the establishment of European feudalism after the Norman Conquest in 1066. In this system, serfs offered allegiance in exchange for protection from the lord of the manor under an "oath of fealty." Sayles claims this reciprocal formula (allegiance for protection, protection for allegiance) remains the foundational principle of statutory jurisdiction today.

He argues that the United States transitioned into an administrative feudal structure specifically around the Civil War and the 1933 gold confiscation. Under this interpretation, individuals born in the United States inherit the status of a "voluntary serf" unless they take affirmative steps to revoke that relationship. Consequently, people do not hold absolute legal title to their homes or cars because, in feudal law, a serf is considered property and property cannot own property.

Linguistic manipulation and administrative jurisdiction

Central to Sayles's argument is the concept of "equivocation"—using words that carry ordinary meanings colloquially while executing restrictive meanings legally:

  • Resident: Sayles states that in statutory law, "resident" does not mean where a person physically dwells; instead, it is a technical status indicating submission to a sovereign jurisdiction in exchange for state-provided benefits.
  • Citizen of the United States: He contrasts "citizen of the United States of America" (which he associates with original, national rights under the organic Constitution) with "citizen of the United States," which he asserts was created by the Fourteenth Amendment to denote federal civil rights subject to Congressional power.
  • Non-resident alien: Under Title 26 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Sayles asserts that "non-resident alien" is the internal code designation applied to individuals who operate outside the Fourteenth Amendment framework.

Birth certificates, bonds, and commercial paper

Sayles presents a theory regarding the commercial monetization of citizens. He states that the "verification of facts" filled out at birth hospitals is left unsigned by parents and sent to the Bureau of Vital Statistics to generate a birth certificate. In his view, this document acts as a commercial "warehouse receipt" under the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC).

By his accounting, these documents collateralize future labor and are attached to federal debt instruments sold to bondholders. Because domestic currency consists of debt instruments issued by the Federal Reserve rather than hard commodity money, he asserts that citizens merely "discharge" debts rather than truly paying them off.

The process of claiming national status

To separate oneself from this administrative state, Sayles advocates rebutting the presumption of Fourteenth Amendment citizenship:

  • The statutory exit phrase: Sayles directs listeners to State Department travel guidance involving American Samoa, citing the phrase: "I, [Name], being duly sworn, hereby declare my intention to be a national, but not a citizen of the United States."
  • Filing an affidavit: He recommends submitting this declaration in a notarized affidavit directly to the U.S. Department of State—either independently or attached to a DS-11 passport application.
  • Weaponizing notice: Once filed, Sayles advises sending formal copies with a cover letter stating "Legal, Lawful Notice — Not to Be Construed as a Filing" to the state Attorney General, county sheriffs, local police chiefs, and the IRS, arguing that this establishes personal liability for any official who attempts to exercise administrative authority over the individual.
