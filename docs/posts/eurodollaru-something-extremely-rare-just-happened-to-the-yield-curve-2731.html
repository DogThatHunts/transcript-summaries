The anatomy of a rare yield curve anomaly

The 5.4% market footprint: steep front, flat back

The US Treasury yield curve has adopted a structure observed on only 5.4% of trading days since January 1990. This configuration is defined by two simultaneous extremes:

  • A steep front end: The spread between the 3-month Treasury bill and the 2-year Treasury note exceeds 60 basis points (recently around 65 basis points), with the 2-year yield surging toward 4.9%.
  • A compressed back end: The spread between the 2-year note and the 10-year note has collapsed to roughly 20 to 30 basis points, while the 5-to-10-year segment sits at a modest 15 basis points.

Individually, these conditions are uncommon—front-end steepness of this magnitude occurs roughly one-third of the time, and back-end compression about one-quarter of the time. Together, this combination clusters around specific historical inflection points where central bank policy expectations diverge sharply from fundamental economic reality.

Deconstructing yields: eliminating inflation and growth

Long-term interest rates break down into three primary components: the expected path of short-term policy rates, inflation expectations, and real economic growth prospects. Analyzing the current curve eliminates two of these drivers:

  1. Subdued inflation expectations: Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) break-even rates have failed to break out despite rising oil and fuel prices. Investors are not pricing significant broad-based inflation risk into the nominal Treasury market.
  2. Absent growth optimism: If rising yields reflected expectations of robust economic expansion, investors would demand higher returns to lend long-term into a strong economy. Genuine growth surges cause the long end to steepen rather than compress.

With inflation compensation subdued and growth sentiment weak, the front-end rise is purely an expectation of Federal Reserve interest rate policy, while the flat back end demonstrates that the market does not expect the underlying economy to support those rates.

Historical counterexamples: how real reflation behaves

To demonstrate what authentic growth expectations look like on the yield curve, EurodollarU highlights two reflationary periods:

  • The 2013 "taper celebration": Often mischaracterized as a panic, the 2013 sell-off saw intermediate and long-term Treasury yields surge while short-term rates remained anchored. The back end of the curve steepened dramatically as investors priced in improving post-crisis conditions.
  • The late-2016 Trump election reflation: Amid hopes of globally synchronized growth and regulatory shifts, longer-dated Treasury yields leaped higher, once again driving a steep back-end move while the short end remained relatively restrained.

In both instances, rising long yields and a steepening curve signaled growing demand for capital and reduced urgency to hold sovereign debt. The current curve displays the polar opposite: aggressive short-rate escalation paired with a heavily restrained back end.

Precedents in policy mistakes: 2000, 2006, and 2018

Historical instances of this steep-front, flat-back pattern reliably emerge ahead of macro turning points:

  • 2000: The Federal Reserve held short-term rates higher even as long maturities signaled that the technology and dot-com investment boom had run its course, culminating in the 2001 recession.
  • 2004–2006: The Fed raised rates while the long end resisted—an anomaly Alan Greenspan labeled a "conundrum"—shortly before the housing bubble and broader financial system began unraveling.
  • Summer to late 2018: The closest parallel to the current environment. Fed officials repeatedly pointed to headline resilience and warned of labor shortages, hiking rates into December 2018. The bond curve flattened sharply in protest; by mid-2019, economic weakness forced the Fed to abort hikes and initiate rate cuts.

The diagnosis: hawkish policy colliding with fragility

The yield curve is not an exact calendar for recession, but it functions as a diagnostic tool. EurodollarU argues that Fed officials are motivated by reputational damage from the 2021–2022 "transitory" inflation episode, making them prone to overtighten in response to supply-driven commodity spikes such as diesel and gasoline.

The market's pricing is unambiguous: the steep front end reflects the risk of further near-term rate hikes, while the flat back end reflects the judgment that an already fragile economy cannot absorb this tightening. History suggests that when the yield curve takes this rare shape, the hawks eventually retreat as growth buckles.
