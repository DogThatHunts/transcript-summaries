The triarchic theory of successful intelligence

Traditional education primarily measures and teaches to analytical intelligence (IQ), which prepares individuals to perform well within closed systems like standardized tests. However, psychologist Robert Sternberg argues in Teaching for Successful Intelligence that true intellectual performance in open systems—such as careers, businesses, and creative fields—requires three distinct cognitive abilities working in unison:

  • Analytical thinking: The ability to analyze, evaluate, compare, and critique.
  • Creative thinking: The ability to generate new ideas, reframe problems, and question assumptions.
  • Practical thinking: The ability to translate knowledge into real-world action.

Sternberg's research demonstrates that students taught using all three modes simultaneously significantly outperform those taught through memory or single-mode methods on exams, homework, and independent projects.

Michael Faraday: organizing and representing information

Despite having virtually no formal scientific education, Michael Faraday became one of history's most consequential scientists. His success relied on a disciplined combination of analytical and creative thinking:

  • Analytical discipline: Faraday did not just record experimental outcomes; he meticulously documented what he expected to happen, why it did not, and what the discrepancy implied. This allowed him to organize and represent information to reveal underlying patterns.
  • Creative reframing: Instead of asking how magnets attract metal, Faraday redefined the problem by asking about the structure of the space between them, leading to his revolutionary concept of electromagnetic field lines.

The Faraday exercise

To practice representing information: 1. Write a concept or problem as a sequence of causes and effects. 2. Draw it as a diagram showing the relationships and dependencies between its parts. 3. Compress it into a single sentence that captures its core tension or fundamental question.

Frederick Douglass: reframing assumptions and understanding audiences

Born into slavery, Frederick Douglass taught himself to read and became an exceptionally powerful orator and political strategist by mastering all three cognitive modes:

  • Analytical precision: Douglass dismantled pro-slavery arguments by identifying hidden premises and unexamined assumptions rather than relying solely on emotional appeals.
  • Creative reframing: When opponents argued using scripture, Douglass did not debate their interpretation; instead, he questioned the very legitimacy of using scripture to justify human bondage.
  • Practical intelligence: He possessed a keen ability to "sell his ideas" by adjusting his register, tone, and emphasis depending on whether he was addressing abolitionists, religious groups, or politicians.

The Douglass exercise

To practice questioning assumptions: 1. Take a position you hold and write the strongest possible argument against it. 2. Identify what you would need to reframe, rather than simply rebut, to genuinely persuade someone holding the opposing view.

Nikola Tesla: the warning of neglected practical intelligence

Nikola Tesla possessed extraordinary analytical and creative intelligence. He could visualize complete, working machines in his mind, run them through mental testing cycles, and identify failure points before building a physical prototype. However, his life serves as a warning about neglecting practical intelligence:

  • The practical gap: Tesla struggled with the cognitive skill of selling his ideas. He could not translate his complex visions into terms that persuaded investors, institutions, or the public, which ultimately cost him credit and financial reward for some of his most important inventions.

The Tesla exercise

To test your practical intelligence, write three versions of an explanation for an idea: 1. For a deep expert (technically precise, no simplification). 2. For a curious non-expert (conceptually clear, no jargon). 3. For someone with an immediate, practical reason to care (concrete, actionable, and relevant to their situation).

The area where the third explanation falters indicates where your practical intelligence needs development.

Temple Grandin: synthesizing all three modes

Temple Grandin, a professor of animal science and designer of livestock handling systems, exemplifies the ideal integration of Sternberg's three modes:

  • Analytical observation: Grandin systematically records and analyzes facility designs to identify the precise causes of behavioral issues in livestock.
  • Creative perspective: By recognizing the "two faces of knowledge"—how expertise can both illuminate a problem and blind you to its solution—she bypassed traditional design blind spots. Her unique perceptual differences allowed her to visualize facilities from the animals' perspective.
  • Practical implementation: Grandin focuses entirely on translating insights into buildable, testable designs while showing the practical perseverance required to overcome institutional resistance.
