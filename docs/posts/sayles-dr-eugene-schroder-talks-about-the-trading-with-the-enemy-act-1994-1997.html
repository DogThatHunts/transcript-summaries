The doctrine of national emergency

The presentation opens with introductory remarks questioning government secrecy and unchecked federal growth, before turning to Dr. Eugene Schroeder's historical analysis of war and emergency powers. Schroeder contends that emergency governance inherently undermines constitutional restraints. Citing former Supreme Court Justice Charles Evans Hughes's warning after World War I—wondering whether constitutional government could survive another major war—and Congressman James M. Beck’s 1933 floor speech comparing emergency rule to the Weimar Republic’s collapse into autocracy, Schroeder argues that declaring an emergency effectively supplants regular constitutional limitations with executive decrees.

Schroeder highlights Senate Report 93-549, published in 1973 by the Special Committee on the Termination of the National Emergency. The report acknowledged that the United States had operated under continuous states of declared national emergency since March 9, 1933. Under these delegated statutory authorities, Schroeder observes, the executive branch amassed vast latent powers to regulate commerce, seize private property, restrict travel, and impose martial controls during crises.

The 1917 Trading with the Enemy Act and the 1933 revision

Schroeder traces the legal mechanics of modern emergency rule to the Trading with the Enemy Act of 1917 (TWEA). Enacted under Article I, Section 8 war powers during World War I, the original statute gave the executive broad control over hostile alien property and international financial transactions. Schroeder emphasizes that Section 2 and Section 5(b) of the original 1917 Act explicitly excluded domestic transactions conducted by American citizens within the borders of the United States.

According to Schroeder, this fundamental boundary was altered on March 9, 1933, when President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Emergency Banking Relief Act (48 Stat. 1), codified in parts of Title 12 of the U.S. Code. Roosevelt had issued Proclamation 2039 declaring a national banking holiday on March 6, relying on TWEA authorities. Congress retroactively confirmed this action and amended Section 5(b) by removing the domestic exemption. Schroeder asserts that by expanding the language to cover "any person within the United States or any place subject to the jurisdiction thereof," Congress legally folded American citizens and their domestic transactions into the statutory framework governing enemies of the state.

Prize law, property rights, and the licensing power

Drawing on nineteenth-century maritime prize law and federal precedents—such as The Rapid, The Julia, and The Sally—Schroeder argues that the legal consequence of placing domestic transactions under war powers is severe. Under traditional prize doctrine, any commercial intercourse with an entity designated as an enemy is strictly unlawful unless conducted under government license. Furthermore, prize law permits the appropriation of enemy property to support the sovereign's war effort.

Schroeder asserts that the 1933 statutory changes converted the American economic system into an all-encompassing licensing regime. He points to the confiscation of private gold reserves under Executive Order 6102 and the Agricultural Adjustment Act's licensing requirements as direct manifestations of war powers. Citing government records such as Senate Document 43 (Contracts Payable in Gold), Schroeder claims that the legal relationship between the individual and private property shifted: private ownership was reduced to mere state-permitted usufruct, while the population and its productive capacity became collateral backing the issuance of new unbacked Federal Reserve banknotes.

The expansion of federal administrative power

Schroeder argues that the post-1933 emergency apparatus permanently altered federalism and the separation of powers. Although the Supreme Court initially struck down major New Deal programs such as the Agricultural Adjustment Act in United States v. Butler (1936), the legal battlefield shifted following Roosevelt’s court-packing crisis in 1937. Thereafter, the judiciary increasingly treated emergency and war measures as non-justiciable political questions committed solely to Congress and the President.

Schroeder further maintains that the passage of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure in the 1930s extended federal prize and statutory jurisdiction inland. He notes that while the National Emergencies Act of 1976 attempted to terminate long-standing emergency authorities, Section 502 specifically exempted Section 5(b) of the Trading with the Enemy Act. In Schroeder's reading, this legislative exemption ensured that the underlying legal mechanism established in 1933 remained permanently operational through subsequent administrations.

Call for constitutional restoration

Comparing the modern administrative state to the grievances voiced in the Declaration of Rights of the Continental Congress in October 1774, Schroeder concludes that federal governance has reverted to imperial forms of arbitrary rule by executive proclamation and bureaucratic fiat. While he raises the question of whether the continuous use of emergency war powers constitutes treason, he asserts that such legal determinations can only be resolved once constitutional limitations are properly restored.

Alvin Jenkins follows Schroeder’s presentation with closing remarks, urging listeners to review official congressional records directly. Jenkins argues that ten consecutive presidential administrations across both major political parties have maintained this emergency framework rather than dissolving it, calling on citizens to prioritize constitutional allegiance above partisan divisions.
