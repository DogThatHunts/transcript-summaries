The illusion of the June CPI shock

The overall CPI fell by nearly 0.5% in June, marking the largest monthly decline since April 2020. More importantly, the core CPI rate—which excludes volatile food and energy prices—was fractionally negative for the first time since May 2020.

The fed's "Trichet" trap and historical echoes of 2008

Federal Reserve officials and commentators like Kevin Warsh continue to warn about "second-round effects," where rising energy costs supposedly spill over into broader services and goods prices. EurodollarU disputes this economic model, asserting that businesses cannot pass higher input costs onto cash-strapped consumers. Instead, businesses are forced to absorb these costs, squeeze their margins, and ultimately cut worker hours or initiate layoffs—a trend already visible in recent labor data.

This disconnect threatens to lead the Fed into what EurodollarU calls "tricheting"—a reference to former European Central Bank President Jean-Claude Trichet. In mid-2008, between the collapse of Bear Stearns and Lehman Brothers, Trichet famously hiked interest rates because soaring oil prices blinded the ECB to widespread economic deterioration. Similarly, the current rise in the two-year Treasury yield reflects the market pricing in the high probability of a Fed policy mistake, rather than actual inflation risk. The dramatic flattening of the yield curve serves as a warning that the market is preparing for a central bank error rather than economic growth.

TIPS break-evens signal zero long-term inflation risk

The Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) market has consistently rejected the "sticky inflation" narrative. TIPS break-even rates, which measure the relative demand for inflation protection, have plummeted since late May.

Comparing the five-year break-even rate to the ten-year rate reveals that the market expects any near-term, energy-driven CPI spikes to be highly temporary. Furthermore, the five-year, five-year forward rate—a measure of long-term structural inflation expectations—has remained stable since 2022. This stability suggests that the market has always viewed post-pandemic price spikes as temporary supply shocks rather than systemic, monetary inflation.

The twist on the oil futures curve

Geopolitical tensions in the Middle East have pushed near-term West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude prices back toward the $80 range, but the broader oil futures curve tells a very different story. While front-month contracts for August and September delivery have risen, contracts further out on the curve are selling off.

This "twist" on the curve, alongside recent periods of contango (where future prices are higher than spot prices, signaling oversupply), suggests that physical oil traders expect long-term demand to collapse. This outlook is reinforced globally; for example, China reported a 40% year-over-year drop in the volume of crude oil imported for the month of June. Ultimately, the oil market is aligning with the TIPS and CPI data to forecast a global economic slowdown.
