This is what consumers 'always' do before a recession

The contradiction that isn't

In August, American consumers exhibited seemingly contradictory behavior: they cut revolving credit (credit cards) by $4.8 billion—the largest decline since November 2024—while simultaneously adding $13.1 billion in non-revolving credit (auto loans, student loans, installment financing). EurodollarU argues these aren't contradictory at all, but rather two expressions of the same income insecurity.

The apparent paradox resolves when we understand what each type of borrowing signals:

  • Revolving credit decline: Households are nervous about jobs, incomes, and their ability to service open-ended debt at 20%+ interest rates. This is defensive behavior.
  • Installment credit surge: Households are panic-buying vehicles and durable goods before they believe prices or financing costs will rise further. This is urgent behavior.

Neither reflects confidence. Together, they suggest the economy may be closer to a turning point than headline spending numbers indicate.

Why revolving credit matters most

Revolving credit is especially sensitive to confidence in future income rather than interest rates. Unlike a car loan with a fixed schedule, credit cards are used to smooth ordinary consumption—groceries, fuel, medical bills. When households worry about job security or hours, carrying an open-ended balance becomes dangerous.

The August decline doesn't automatically prove a recession is coming, but the context is telling:

  • Prices have risen faster than wages for five consecutive months
  • Consumer confidence has deteriorated sharply
  • Hiring in the labor market remains "almost entirely absent"
  • Quits are near their lowest rate since 2020
  • Surveys show widespread concern about employment, income, prices, and household finances

Against this backdrop, the drop in revolving credit looks less like financial comfort and more like financial defense.

The affordability crisis is really an income crisis

The term "affordability" has become a buzzword for inflation, but it misses the core issue. A price is affordable or unaffordable relative to income. When we talk about affordability, we're really talking about the labor market—which EurodollarU argues is in a "prolonged slump" despite mainstream denials.

If wages and employment rose rapidly enough, households could absorb higher prices. But instead:

  • Income is stagnating
  • Hours are being reduced
  • Workers can't find better jobs

Research from Georgetown Law Center on Poverty and Inequality shows that 70% of Americans struggle with multiple affordability issues: 41% face healthcare burdens, one-third face housing burdens, and nearly one-third face food burdens. This struggle reaches well into the middle class, as evidenced by weak performance at retailers like Walmart, Dick's, and Kroger.

Labor market data confirms the weakness

  • August job openings fell by 256,000 to 7.1 million
  • Hiring remains around 5.2 million—essentially unchanged from July and at "recession level"
  • Quits are at 3.07 million (1.9% rate)—workers quit when they believe opportunities exist; they stay put when they don't trust the labor market
  • The September Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index fell to its lowest level since 2014, with the Expectations Index moving into "recession territory"

The installment credit surge: panic buying, not confidence

At first glance, strong auto financing looks bullish. But EurodollarU argues it reflects panic buying, not genuine optimism. Imagine a household already planning to replace a vehicle later in the year. It hears warnings about higher fuel prices, tariffs, supply disruptions, and potential Federal Reserve rate hikes. That household may decide to purchase now, pulling forward a purchase from November or December into August.

The loan is real, the vehicle is real, the spending is real—but the timing has been distorted. This creates a "payback period" where current consumption is elevated while future demand is reduced.

Unlike credit card borrowing, a fixed installment loan provides certainty: the household knows the monthly payment and payoff date. This can feel safer than carrying open-ended credit card debt. So the same household can "rashly take out an auto loan while cutting card use"—locking in one necessary purchase before conditions worsen while becoming cautious everywhere else.

Evidence that this is artificial, not genuine demand

Used vehicle prices fell in September, with the Mannheim Used Vehicle Value Index declining 1.1% from August. This isn't what a broad demand boom looks like. If households experienced a genuine increase in purchasing power, stronger demand should support vehicle values. Instead, prices are softening under the pressure of higher perceived financing costs and consumer anxieties.

Production data also tells a cautionary tale:

  • Mexican vehicle output fell 15.1% year-over-year in September
  • Auto exports declined almost 12%
  • General Motors and Nissan recorded production drops exceeding 25%

Producers facing uncertain demand and unpredictable trade rules aren't building as if August represented the start of a durable automotive boom. They're limiting exposure. Consumers are completing panic purchases; producers and financial markets are preparing for what happens after.

The "resilient consumer" narrative is misleading

Spending headlines have created a false narrative about an "endlessly resourceful" consumer. But resourcefulness is what consumers need when resilience is running out. Households are:

  • Using buy-now-pay-later services
  • Tapping home equity (many are doing this extensively)
  • Using AI to compare grocery prices
  • Relying on food banks to free up money for rent, fuel, and medical expenses

These strategies aren't evidence of financial strength. A consumer who must borrow against a home, split routine purchases into installments, or meticulously compare every grocery item isn't demonstrating abundant purchasing power. They're trying to reallocate scarcity.

While these tactics can keep spending elevated temporarily—home equity can be converted to cash, food assistance can free income for utilities—none of them create the income that's badly needed. They allow households to maintain consumption temporarily despite weak income growth, potentially weakening their future financial position.

Consumption did rise at its fastest pace in more than a year in August, including spending on cars, clothing, and restaurants. But the methods households use to sustain that spending tell a much less optimistic story.

The artificial high: how shocks create false resilience

EurodollarU identifies a historical pattern: energy shocks combined with Federal Reserve rate-hike warnings create an "artificial high" where everyone acts defensively, but the resulting data looks expansionary.

Manufacturers worried about diesel prices or freight costs place orders early. Retailers build inventory before expected wholesale increases. Consumers purchase vehicles or appliances on installment credit before anticipated price hikes reach showrooms. Everyone acts defensively, but the data can look expansionary:

  • Retail sales go up
  • Factories increase output
  • Freight activity improves
  • Inventory accumulates
  • Businesses add shifts or part-time employees

Economists then declare the economy resilient, but the shock may have manufactured the resilience. We saw this pattern during the 2025 tariff scare, when spending accelerated as households tried to get ahead of expected price increases that never materialized. The initial burst was followed by weaker activity because consumers had already bought goods they would have purchased later.

The Federal Reserve contributed to this urgency through repeated inflation warnings and willingness to consider additional rate hikes, encouraging households to believe that waiting means higher costs across the board.

Labor market: the foundation that isn't there

If installment borrowing reflected genuine optimism, we should see employers expanding, workers moving to better jobs, and incomes accelerating. We don't.

  • August JOLTS showed no meaningful rise in hiring
  • Job openings fell, especially in manufacturing
  • Quits have already declined, signaling workers lack confidence
  • Income data, while revised higher in recent benchmarks, remains nowhere near enough to pay for past price increases

Where labor data has seemed positive, it fits too well with the panic-buying narrative: increases in part-time employment and extra shifts at retailers, manufacturers, and transportation companies processing temporary waves of orders. These don't represent permanent full-time hiring commitments. The Household Survey registered employment gains concentrated in part-time work in September while the Payroll Survey remained weak.

Flexible staffing isn't the foundation of a new expansion—it's how businesses manage an activity burst they don't trust.

Resolving the apparent contradiction

The August credit report's two halves answer different questions:

  • Revolving credit asks: Do households trust their future income enough to carry open-ended debt today? August's answer was increasingly no.
  • Installment credit asks: Do households believe delaying a specific purchase could make it more expensive or impossible to afford later? August's answer was yes.

The first response reflects caution about jobs and income. The second reflects fear that weak income won't absorb future price increases or higher financing costs.

The key to the affordability crisis: The problem isn't simply that prices rose; it's that employment and income failed to keep pace, and consumers don't think that's going to change. Because households don't expect earnings to suddenly accelerate, anticipated price increases become reasons to act immediately.

Consumers aren't saying, "The future looks great, so let's borrow." They're saying, "The future may be worse, so complete this specific purchase now and cut back everywhere else."

What comes next: the payback period

Panic buying can extend for an extended period, but the real test is what comes afterward. Once consumers have purchased the vehicles, appliances, and other goods they were already planning to buy, what replaces that front-loaded demand?

Not rapidly rising income. Not stronger hiring. Not confident workers moving to better jobs or businesses preparing for sustained expansion. The credit report's two halves are already giving us the answer:

  • The installment binge is the artificial high
  • The revolving credit decline is the underlying baseline

One tells us consumers are afraid of what prices and financing might do next. The other tells us they're afraid their jobs and incomes won't be strong enough to handle it. Once that urgency fades, caution is likely to win.

The strangest part of the August credit report wasn't that consumers both cut back and splurged on borrowing at the same time—it was that both decisions delivered the same message: deep anxiety about the future.
