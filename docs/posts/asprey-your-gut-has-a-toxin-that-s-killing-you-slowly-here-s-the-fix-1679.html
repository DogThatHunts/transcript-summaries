The problem: endotoxemia and leaky gut

Asprey opens by noting that complex diseases like heart disease and autism have no single cause; they are multifactorial. The microbiome is a key driver of chronic disease risk because it provides enormous functionality to the body. However, the loss of microbial diversity is one of the biggest risk factors.

Krishnan explains that endotoxins are toxins generated within the body—specifically, lipopolysaccharides (LPS) released from the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria when they die. When the gut barrier becomes permeable (leaky gut), these endotoxins leak into the bloodstream and trigger a mini-sepsis response throughout the body. Asprey notes that he has a genetic predisposition to leaky gut and leaky blood vessels, making him acutely sensitive to endotoxin effects.

The shift toward gram-negative bacteria in modern Western guts is largely due to agricultural practices. Asprey and Krishnan argue that widespread use of antifungal sprays on crops (especially citrus) has favored gram-negative bacteria over gram-positive bacteria, which are more susceptible to fungal antibiotics. This shift has increased the prevalence of endotoxin-producing bacteria in the environment and in human guts.

Why conventional probiotics fail

Krishnan's first study in the probiotic space tested 45 top-shelf probiotics and found that 98% were decimated by stomach acid. The stomach's pH can be as low as 1.2–1.4, which is so acidic it would burn off fingertips instantly. This gastric barrier evolved to protect against pathogenic bacteria and parasites.

However, Krishnan notes that even dead probiotic debris can have benefits—a phenomenon called "parabiotic" activity. The immune system samples dead bacterial parts and learns to recognize and defend against those microbes. But live probiotics that survive the gastric barrier are far more powerful.

Spore-forming probiotics: nature's solution

Spore-forming bacteria like Bacillus subtilis have evolved a remarkable survival mechanism. When stressed, they coat themselves in a protein-hardened, calcified shell and enter dormancy. In this state, they can remain viable for hundreds of millions of years—Krishnan cites research by Raul Cano, who revived bacterial spores from a 50-million-year-old honeybee preserved in amber and used them to ferment kombucha.

Krishnan and his team at Just Thrive selected four spore-forming strains based on specific criteria:

  1. Natural survival of gastric acid. Unlike most probiotics, these strains naturally survive the stomach's pH.
  2. Biofilm production. Good gut bacteria live within biofilms—protective networks that allow instantaneous communication and mutual defense.
  3. Silent immune activation. Most probiotics upregulate inflammatory pathways (Th1, Th2, Th17). The Just Thrive strains instead activate regulatory T cells (Tregs) without triggering inflammation.
  4. Quorum sensing. These bacteria can read chemical signatures of other microbes, understand the microbial landscape, and modulate the microbiome intelligently.

The microbiome as a network: cross-feeding and butyrate

Krishnan emphasizes that the microbiome is not a collection of isolated organisms but a complex network with primary, secondary, and tertiary degraders. When you eat resistant starch or fiber, primary degraders like Ruminococcus break it down into lactate and acetate. Secondary degraders like Faecalibacterium prausnitzii then convert these into butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid with profound health effects.

Butyrate is directly anti-inflammatory and a powerful generator of ketones. It activates AMPK, the body's fat-burning system. In a published study, spore probiotics increased butyrate production by 50% in three weeks; when combined with resistant starch and prebiotics, the effect tripled to 150%.

Reducing endotoxins: the liver's role

When endotoxins leak through the gut barrier, 85% of them go to the liver first via the portal circulation. The liver has two mechanisms to neutralize LPS:

  1. HDL3 (high-density lipoprotein 3) binds LPS and delivers it to Kupffer cells, which neutralize it without triggering an immune response.
  2. LPS-binding protein (LBP) also binds LPS but triggers a massive inflammatory response.

Therefore, maintaining high HDL3 levels is crucial for controlling circulating endotoxins. This explains why people with higher HDL have lower all-cause mortality—they are effectively managing endotoxin exposure.

The gelding effect: endotoxins and testosterone

Krishnan introduces the "gelding effect"—a well-documented pathology in which elevated gut-associated endotoxins suppress testosterone production. When LPS enters circulation, it triggers inflammation that the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis detects. Instead of signaling the gonads to produce testosterone, the pituitary facilitates the cortisol pathway, shutting down testosterone production.

Asprey connects this to the global decline in sperm counts, suggesting that gram-negative gut bacteria may be a significant contributor. He notes that this mechanism explains why certain dietary choices—such as eating glyphosate-laden oatmeal—can have immediate effects on hormonal health.

Microbiome diversity and lost species

SIBO and gram-negative bacteria

Krishnan explains that small intestine bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) is characterized by an unusually high amount of gram-negative bacteria in the small bowel. Many of these gram-negative bacteria originate from the oral microbiome. Asprey notes that we are constantly breathing in fecal bacteria—toilet flushing aerosolizes fecal matter throughout homes.
