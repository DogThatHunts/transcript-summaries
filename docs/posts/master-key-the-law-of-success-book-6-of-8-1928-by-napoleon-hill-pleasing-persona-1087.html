Lesson 10: pleasing personality

Napoleon Hill defines personality as the sum total of an individual's physical, mental, and character traits. While outward appearances like clothing and handshakes form critical first impressions, Hill argues that the most vital aspect of personality is character—the invisible, thought-built foundation of personal magnetism.

The power of genuine interest in others

The most effective way to build an attractive personality is to take a keen, genuine interest in other people's lives and endeavors. Hill illustrates this with two key anecdotes: * The security saleswoman: An elderly woman visited Hill's office and spent 45 minutes praising his editorial work on Hill's Golden Rule magazine. Only in the final three minutes did she introduce the securities she was selling. Disarmed by her genuine interest, Hill did not buy directly but introduced her to a contact who purchased a massive block of securities. * The artist and the green salesman: A newly trained salesman was sent by his colleagues to pitch a wealthy, notoriously difficult artist as a practical joke. Instead of talking business, the salesman spent an hour discussing the artist's current painting. The artist, feeling appreciated, voluntarily purchased $2,000 in securities, noting that the salesman "presented the matter so differently" compared to others who only talked business.

Hill cautions that cheap flattery is easily detected and repels people; only sincere appreciation can build a magnetic personality.

Practical business plans for creative thinkers

To demonstrate how imagination, cooperation, and a pleasing personality blend together, Hill proposes three practical business ideas: 1. Toy manufacturing: Capitalize on the post-WWI decline in German toy imports by identifying high-demand toys, partnering with an inventor for models, and securing small manufacturers to produce them. 2. Independent advertising copywriting: Partner with an advertising agency to receive a 7% commission on accounts. Offer free copywriting and management services to businesses on the condition that they route their advertising budget through your partner agency. 3. Creative printing services: Partner with a printer (for a 10% commission), a commercial artist, and a copywriter. Pitch redesigned, highly improved marketing materials directly to businesses.

Hill notes that these plans rely on shortening the route between producer and consumer, citing Henry Ford's high-wage, low-cost model and John D. Rockefeller's oil distribution system as prime examples of service-oriented wealth accumulation.

Mark Antony's funeral oration as master salesmanship

Hill analyzes Mark Antony's famous speech from Shakespeare's Julius Caesar as a masterclass in psychology and salesmanship. Antony faced a hostile Roman mob that favored Caesar's assassins. Rather than attacking Brutus directly, Antony neutralized their minds by: * Aligning with their current beliefs ("I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him"). * Repeatedly calling Brutus and the conspirators "honorable men" to avoid immediate rejection. * Subtly introducing doubts by pointing out Caesar's generosity and refusal of the crown. * Appealing to their curiosity by teasing Caesar's will but refusing to read it initially. * Allowing the mob to reach the conclusion of murder on their own, thereby transforming their hostility into a mutinous frenzy.

Seven steps to cultivate a pleasing personality

Hill outlines seven core factors for developing personal magnetism: 1. Interest in others: Form the habit of finding and genuinely praising the good qualities in others. 2. Forceful speech: Cultivate a deep, rich, and rhythmic voice; practice speaking with conviction. 3. Appropriate dress: Clothe yourself in a style fitting your physical build and profession. 4. Positive character: Build character through rigid self-discipline and positive thoughts. 5. Warm handshake: Express enthusiasm and warmth through physical greetings. 6. Attractiveness: Attract others by first showing genuine attraction to them. 7. Mental limits: Remove self-imposed limitations from your own mind.

To build character, Hill recommends his "council table" visualization technique from Lesson 2, where the student mentally consults historical figures whose traits they wish to emulate, reinforcing these traits through daily auto-suggestion.

Lesson 11: accurate thought

Accurate thinking requires the strict mental discipline of separating facts from mere information, and then categorizing those facts into the important (relevant to one's definite chief aim) and the unimportant (irrelevant).

The "they say" chorus and the law of evidence

Hill warns against the "they say" chorus—the widespread human tendency to accept gossip, rumors, and biased newspaper reports without analysis. He points out that leaders are routinely slandered by competitors and opponents, citing historical rumors targeted at George Washington, Abraham Lincoln, Warren G. Harding, and Woodrow Wilson.

An accurate thinker must apply a personal "law of evidence," scrutinizing information closely—especially when it is negative or when the source has a vested interest in coloring the truth.

The five stages of human evolution

Hill traces the development of the human mind through five distinct evolutionary periods: 1. The mineral period: Life in its lowest, inert form. 2. The vegetable period: Active growth and reproduction, but fixed in place. 3. The animal period: High intelligence with the capacity for physical movement. 4. The human (thinking) period: The capacity for inductive and deductive reasoning. 5. The spiritual period: The ultimate stage where human thought connects with infinite intelligence.

The camera analogy of the subconscious mind

Hill explains the relationship between the conscious mind, the subconscious mind, and infinite intelligence using a camera analogy: * The object to be photographed represents your definite chief aim. * The shutter is the conscious mind, which controls what information is allowed to enter. * The lens represents auto-suggestion, the medium that transfers the image. * The sensitized plate is the subconscious mind, which records the image without choosing or judging it. * The developer is infinite intelligence, which translates the recorded mental image into physical reality through natural laws and opportunities.

Hill emphasizes that infinite intelligence does not perform magic; rather, it guides the individual to recognize and act upon natural opportunities to achieve their goals.

Mind power, hypochondria, and physical health

Hill asserts that the mind exerts absolute control over the physical body. He presents several claims regarding health and mental suggestion: * Hypochondria: Hill cites claims from physicians that roughly 75% of physical illnesses are hypochondriacal (imaginary), often triggered by auto-intoxication or a runaway imagination. * The power of suggestion: He shares an anecdote from Dr. Henry R. Rose, where a woman reportedly recovered from severe pneumonia after her minister inspired her with the faith that she would survive. * The limits of mental healing: Crucially, Hill clarifies that the mind cannot cure everything. He explicitly states there is no evidence that cancer can be cured by faith or mental processes, warning that cancer must be treated surgically at the very beginning.

Desire, persistence, and the will to win

A definite chief aim is useless without a deeply seated, passionate desire. Hill argues that elements in the universe respond to the law of attraction, bringing physical equivalents to those who maintain persistent, positive thoughts.

He contrasts the boxing matches between Gene Tunney and Jack Dempsey, claiming Tunney won because of his absolute self-reliance, while Dempsey was "whipped in his own mind" before entering the ring due to fear and self-doubt.

After-the-lesson visit: the value of failure

Hill concludes with an essay on the necessity of failure. He argues that failure is not a permanent curse but a temporary defeat designed by an all-wise Providence to test an individual's mettle and teach humility. Carrying the "cross of poverty" or experiencing hardship is often the very catalyst that prepares great leaders for their future responsibilities.
