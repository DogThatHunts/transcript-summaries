Master Key | The Law of Success, Book 6 of 8 (1928) by Napoleon Hill - "Pleasing Personality & Accurate Thought"

Hill teaches how a pleasing personality and sincere interest in others win people over, then how accurate thought, auto-suggestion and faith turn a definite chief aim into reality.

Book 6 of 8 of Napoleon Hill's The Law of Success (1928), read by Dan John Miller for the Master Key Society. The ideas are Hill's, reported as he states them, not endorsed or verified here. Every timestamp opens the video a few seconds early.

Book 6 holds Lesson Ten, "Pleasing Personality," and Lesson Eleven, "Accurate Thought." The first is the most outward-facing lesson in the course: the handshake, the voice, dress, the habit of praising, and above all a keen interest in "the other fellow's game in life." The second starts with plain reasoning (separate facts from mere information, then relevant facts from irrelevant ones) and climbs into Hill's most metaphysical territory: auto-suggestion, the subconscious mind, creative thought, infinite intelligence, and "thoughts are things."

Character joins the two lessons. A pleasing personality cannot be faked, Hill argues, because others sense your real character through telepathy and the law of attraction. Accurate thought demands "the staunchest and most unshakable character," because it means following facts at your own cost and keeping gossip, slander and hatred out of the subconscious mind.

Hill calls Lesson Eleven "the very keystone to the arch of this course" and asks the student to read it four times. He also marks a turn: earlier lessons measured success in money, while this one and those after it are for readers who want "to go higher up the ladder." The book ends with Hill's essay on failure and a set of his inspirational quotations.

Lesson 10: Pleasing Personality [00:40]

What personality is [00:51]

"Your personality is the sum total of your characteristics and appearances which distinguish you from all others." [01:03] That takes in clothes, face, voice, thoughts and the character those thoughts build. Character is the most important part, "the part that is not visible," though first impressions still come from appearance, the handshake and the eyes. Hill also counts bodily vitality, "sometimes called personal magnetism." [01:36] Any personality can attract, "even though you may be as homely as the circus fat woman," by "taking a keen interest in the other fellow's game in life." [02:36]

The old lady who sold Hill himself [02:56]

An elderly woman called at Hill's office and would not tell his secretaries her business. Expecting a book saleswoman, he went out meaning to buy. Her smile and handshake "disarmed me and made me a willing victim"; she had "neutralized my mind and made me want to listen." [05:18] It is useless, Hill says, to try to sell anyone until you have made him want to listen.

She told him he was "doing the most wonderful work of any man in the world to-day," and he gave her his easy chair for three-quarters of an hour. She never said "I." She sold him himself, reading marked passages from a year's file of his magazine, Hill's Golden Rule. [08:49] Only in the last three minutes did she describe the securities she sold. Hill bought nothing, but he introduced her to a man who bought five times what she had meant to sell him. Reverse the order, he says, and she would never have reached the chair. "We are all human, and we are all more or less vain." [09:41]

The green salesman and the artist [11:00]

In Chicago Hill ran a salesmanship school for a securities house of more than fifteen hundred salespeople, which hired six hundred new ones a week. As a joke, a star named Perkins sent one newcomer to a wealthy artist whom every star had failed to sell. The newcomer watched the artist paint, talked about the picture for nearly an hour, and answered questions about himself with "Oh, never mind my business or my name, I am more interested in you and your art." [14:54] The artist insisted on hearing his business and bought $2,000 worth, because other salesmen "talk nothing but business." What the newcomer really sold him was "his own picture." [16:23] He earned $7,900 in commissions his first month, more than double the next man, and no one asked why, which Hill says justifies his "biting reprimand" in Lesson Nine. [16:52]

Not flattery, and not new [17:23]

Carnegie, Rockefeller, James J. Hill and Marshall Field used principles open to everyone; "you will not find a single new principle" in the course. [18:00] This is not a license for flattery. The aim is to find "something about them or their work that you really admire"; cheap flattery "repels instead of attracting." [18:38] The lesson rests, Hill says, on the same principles as Lesson Six (Imagination) and Lesson Thirteen (Cooperation). [19:46]

Ideas that combine imagination, cooperation and personality [20:07]

Hill offers sample money-making plans:

  • Toys. With Germany's toy trade lost to the war and Japan's toys "of so poor a quality," find the fast sellers, advertise for an inventor, contract a small shop, and sell the output to a jobber on credit. Make toys educational or active, such as a one-child indoor baseball game. [20:49]
  • Advertising. Take 7% of the billings you bring an agency and serve its clients free from a desk on their premises: about $17,500 a year on $250,000 of business, $5,000 to $7,500 for the average person. [24:50]
  • Printing. Take a 10% commission from a printer and, with an artist and copywriter, show customers improved versions of their printed matter before quoting. [29:08]

Profit from organizing others is legitimate, Hill argues, because it raises their earnings. An employee who wants to be an employer should serve his firm "just as you would wish to be served" if he ran it. [32:04] Bring buyers and sellers together, shorten the route from farm to consumer, and make sure a plan "slices off a little of the cost to the consumer." Ford paid workers "as much as his profits will permit" and cut prices [35:32]; Rockefeller, Hill says, delivered lamp oil for little over half the 25 cents a gallon Hill once paid. [36:39] Ninety-nine in a hundred think only of getting money and "no thought to the service to be given in return for it." [37:14] "A pleasing personality is one that makes use of imagination and cooperation." [37:29]

Mark Antony's speech as salesmanship [37:55]

Hill reproduces Antony's funeral oration from Shakespeare's Julius Caesar, with his comments in parentheses, as "one of the greatest lessons on personality ever placed on paper." Brutus has persuaded a hostile mob that killing Caesar was noble. Antony's steps, as Hill reads them:

  • Neutralize. Open humbly and side with the listeners: "I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him." Had he begun by "knocking Brutus," Rome's history "would have been different." [39:56]
  • Pause and let the crowd talk, as a master salesman lets the prospect talk. [42:20]
  • Plant suggestions gradually: "honourable men," then "mutiny and rage," then "daggers." [43:52]
  • Use curiosity: refuse to read the will. "Human nature always wants that which is difficult to get, and that of which it is about to be deprived." [45:36]
  • Let them say it. The mob calls the conspirators "traitors" before Antony does. [47:22]
  • Save the climax. Many a speaker or salesman, Hill says, "tries to reach this point too soon." [52:55]

Brutus lost because he lacked "the personality and the good judgment" to argue from the mob's point of view. [54:39] Hill points back to the lesson on initiative and leadership and to Lesson Seven's two letters as further examples of the "you" appeal beating the "I" appeal. [55:40] He then adds a caveat: Antony's appeal was "of the crafty type," the method of "dishonest men," and rested on "flattery." [56:29]

Character first [57:41]

No one can have a pleasing personality without "a sound, positive character." Through telepathy, Hill says, you "telegraph the nature of your character" to others; a greedy or envious heart attracts only its like, and manners without "earnestness of purpose" repel. [57:59] "The first step in character building is rigid self-discipline." [59:18] His formula, repeated from Lessons Two and Eight:

  1. Each night, at an imaginary council table, sit with people whose qualities you want, holding a written statement of those qualities, and affirm aloud that you are developing them. [59:41]
  2. Keep the mind positive (Lesson Eight) and, a dozen times a day, feel "with a faith that knows no limitation" that you are growing like them. [1:00:32]
  3. Each day, sincerely praise a good quality in at least one person. Those you praise "will see in you the qualities that you see in them." [1:01:14]

Speaking affirmations aloud "sets into motion the vibration" that carries thought into the subconscious, and it trains forceful speech. [1:03:17] Hill advises a "deep, rich tone of voice," since "speech is the chief method of expressing your personality," and says every prominent leader he knows of speaks with force and conviction. [1:04:20]

The seven factors [1:06:10]

  1. Take an interest in others and praise their good qualities.
  2. Speak with force and conviction.
  3. Dress to suit your build and work.
  4. Build a positive character by the formula.
  5. Shake hands with warmth.
  6. "Attract other people to you by first attracting yourself to them."
  7. Your only limitation, within reason, is the one you set in your own mind.

The second and fourth matter most; the rest follow from them. [1:07:42] Every shady deal "destroys just so much of that subtle something within you that is known as character," and Hill quotes Emerson: "Men know not why they do not trust him, but they do not trust him." [1:08:38]

The art of being agreeable [1:09:24]

The biggest gain from being agreeable is "the beautifying effect that it has upon the character." Take the chip off your shoulder and "throw away your hammer and quit knocking." [1:10:09] "No person with a grievance can be also a person with an attractive personality." [1:10:57] Hill names those whose agreeableness wins his custom: a distant filling-station man, Mr. Cobb of the Regal Shoe Store, the Harriman National Bank ("my account is small, but they receive me as though it were large"), John D. Rockefeller, Jr., M. T. Garvin of Lancaster, George S. Parker, and Edward Bok of the Ladies' Home Journal. [1:11:11]

Ruthlessness wins for a time but ends in "the emptiness of your well-filled purse." [1:13:59] Hill closes with an unnamed speaker's meditation at Napoleon's tomb ("I would rather have been a French peasant and worn wooden shoes") and calls Napoleon "a failure because he did not acquire the art of being agreeable." [1:14:23]

Lesson 11: Accurate Thought [1:17:34]

The keystone lesson [1:17:40]

This is "the most important, the most interesting, and the most difficult to present lesson" of the course. Men built ships only of wood until they learned steel floats; the student should not close his mind on a first reading. [1:18:56] The earlier lessons sold success as money, success with "all three S's replaced by dollar signs." This lesson and the rest are for those seeking success "measured in other than material standards." [1:20:22]

Facts, and relevant facts [1:21:45]

Accurate thought has two fundamentals: "separate facts from mere information," and separate important from unimportant facts. Relevant facts are those you can use toward your definite chief aim. [1:21:50] This is why people of equal ability end far apart: one swings a "trip-hammer," the other a "tack-hammer." [1:23:13]

The "they say" chorus [1:24:05]

The inaccurate thinker opens with "I see by the papers" or "They say"; the papers, Hill says, carry "more falsehood than truth." [1:24:40] Without known facts, treat as factual only the evidence "which furthers your own interests without working any hardship on others." [1:26:04] Most thinking rests on expediency; the accurate thinker keeps one standard even at a loss, trusting "the law of averages," and Hill cites Croesus on the wheel of fortune. [1:26:35]

Interested witnesses deserve scrutiny, and slander "a grain of the proverbial salt." [1:29:11] Every rising leader draws slanderers: Lincoln, Washington, Harding, Wilson ("a combination of Nero and Judas Iscariot") and John H. Patterson of National Cash Register. Hill says slanderers "killed both Harding and Wilson." [1:31:05] Constructive hearsay is harmless; the negative kind needs the closest inspection. [1:33:41] The accurate thinker's motto: "I do not believe that I can afford to deceive others. I know I cannot afford to deceive myself." [1:35:19]

Rockefeller and Edison [1:35:41]

Organized facts bring in the law of increasing returns from an earlier lesson. Rockefeller's standout trait was "insisting on facts"; whether he was fair to competitors "may or may not be true," and Hill leaves it "undisturbed." [1:37:42] Edison's light, phonograph and moving picture came from relevant facts, not hearsay. [1:39:10] A relevant fact is "any fact which you can use without interfering with the rights of others" in reaching your aim. [1:40:37]

Creative thought and evolution [1:41:13]

Beyond deductive reasoning lies "creative thought." Citing Judge Thomas Troward's Bible Mystery and Bible Meaning, Hill traces five periods: mineral, vegetable, animal, thinking man, and spiritual, in which man projects his thought "into infinite intelligence." [1:41:56] Man is "but an infant" in the fifth period; only Moses, Solomon, Christ, Plato, Aristotle, Socrates, Confucius and a few others saw thought as the link. Creative thought works "very largely on faith," outside the five senses. [1:44:06]

Auto-suggestion, the subconscious and infinite intelligence [1:45:46]

Auto-suggestion is "the telegraph line" to the subconscious, which is "the intermediary between the conscious thinking mind and infinite intelligence." The subconscious records your suggestions and has infinite intelligence translate them into physical form "through natural means." [1:47:40] Infinite intelligence, reached through the Master Mind of the introductory lesson, is "the very foundation upon which this entire course is built." [1:48:14] The subconscious also accepts every suggestion, good or bad, so listening to slander is "feeding the subconscious mind with food that is poison." [1:48:35]

Hill offers two similes. In the camera, the subconscious is the plate, the conscious mind the shutter, auto-suggestion the lens, and infinite intelligence the developer; the chief aim is the object photographed. [1:49:24] In farming, faith prepares the soil, the definite purpose is the seed, continued faith is sun and rain, and then comes harvest. [1:56:46]

This is not a matter of miracles. You keep working as Lesson Nine instructs and take each step as it opens: "Infinite intelligence will not build you a home," but it will open the way. [1:51:19] Progress is gradual. Man climbed "step by step, perhaps through many reincarnations," and sudden power ruins the unprepared, a subject Hill promises for Lesson Fourteen. [1:55:35] Of the four factors, "you control three." [1:59:03] Creative thought is positive thought; hatred, envy and greed are "the weeds which will choke out the seed." [1:59:47] In time you will give "full credit to infinite intelligence for all that you had previously credited to your self-confidence." [2:01:12]

Mind and health [2:01:55]

Negative information "will actually destroy health." Hill says about 75% of the sick suffer from hypochondria, often from "auto-intoxication," and that many operations could be replaced by "an internal bath and a bottle of citrate of magnesia." [2:02:13] He quotes the minister Dr. Henry R. Rose on a pneumonia patient who recovered once he told her she would live ("No medicine can cure pneumonia") and a woman who resumed eating once her faith returned. [2:03:28] Rose adds a limit: certain cancers must be treated surgically, and "it would be criminal to suggest" otherwise. [2:08:35] Hill adds more examples: Napoleon among plague victims, Goethe and malignant fever, the blindfolded "ice" experiment, and Dr. Schofield's patient with an imagined tumor. [2:09:05]

Bedtime is the best time to direct the subconscious. Tell it firmly you will rise at seven and it will wake you; command it the same way to build "self-confidence, courage, initiative." [2:13:26]

What the mind can do [2:15:20]

Man's chemical value "is said to be about $26," apart from the mind. The mind can build or destroy, "has power over every cell of the body," and produces all achievement; ideas matter more than execution, and most thoughts are mere "opinions or snap judgments." [2:16:02] Hill expects "the next great cycle of discovery" in the mind. [2:18:30]

Hill's summary: the body's billions of "intelligent" cells are directed by the subconscious; an idea held by repetition tends toward its material equivalent; and holding a picture until the subconscious takes it is the law of concentration, the subject of the next lesson. [2:21:05] Desire is "the starting point of all achievement," and through the law of attraction a strong desire draws its physical counterpart. [2:25:13] Every suggestion must become auto-suggestion to work, so "no one may influence another without the consent of the one influenced." [2:27:07]

Persistence [2:31:02]

"The word impossible means less now than ever before." After quotations on will from Buxton, Donald G. Mitchell, Disraeli, Sir John Simpson, John Foster, and Lincoln on Grant, [2:32:49] Hill says desire must be held until the subconscious takes it: "Up to this point, you must stand back of the desire and push it." [2:35:22] He watched the Tunney-Dempsey fight in Chicago; Dempsey was "whipped, in his own mind, before he entered the ring." [2:35:51] Accurate thought "creates ideas," and Hill credits "leading investigators in the field of mental phenomena." [2:37:32]

"Thoughts are things" [2:39:00]

The course's mastery opens a knowledge that "cannot be imparted by one man to another." [2:39:25] "Thoughts are things"; man is "the physical reflection of thought" set in motion by infinite intelligence. [2:40:20] "All thought is creative, however, not all thought is constructive or positive." Plant your aim, see yourself already possessing it, and "Do not doubt, but believe." [2:42:02] Thought is "the only power over which you have absolute control," which is the meaning of Henley's Invictus. [2:44:59]

Hill draws aside "the curtain that hangs over the gateway called death" to show a world of beings born of your own thoughts, which he calls allegorical: "I am not urging you to accept this viewpoint." [2:46:30] Accurate thought here means thought "of your own creation." [2:48:42] Thought, like radio sound, may fill the ether for those with the equipment to receive it. [2:50:00] The way in is "Know thyself." [2:51:19] The lesson ends: "The Master Mind is the principle through which you may become an accurate thinker." [2:52:20]

After-the-lesson visit: Failure [2:54:11]

Everyone must "bear the cross of failure," poverty heaviest of all. Failure teaches humility; ninety-nine useful people come up through poverty for every rich man's son; providence tests the great "through some form of failure." Hill says the course grew from twenty years of hardship. [2:55:09] He closes: "There is no failure." [2:58:44] The reader then gives Hill's full-page quotations, including Edison on solitude and "The most successful physicians are those who mix hope and faith with the medicines they prescribe."
