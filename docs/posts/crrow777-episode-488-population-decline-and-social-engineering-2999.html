Two hosts argue that the world's population has been quietly falling since the early 1970s, that overpopulation warnings were a cover story, and that a century of eugenics-rooted "population control" work caused the decline.

Show: Crrow777 Radio (crrow777radio.com). The transcript renders the name as "Pro Triple Seven Radio", a speech-recognition error. Hosts: Crrow777 (the main presenter, who goes by that name) and Jason, who reads most of the quoted source material on air. Length: about 2 h 6 min, in two hours. Hour 1 is roughly 0:59–50:26; hour 2 is roughly 50:26–2:03:25. Format: Jason reads passages from mainstream sources (the UN, Wikipedia-style reference text, the Population Reference Bureau, BBC News, Our World in Data). Crrow777 then responds, usually by disputing the figures or the motives behind them. Earlier coverage: the hosts say they first covered this subject in episode 135 (2018), and that it was based on Daniel Estulin's work (2:00:26).

The core argument in one paragraph

Crrow777 and Jason argue that (1) population has been falling since at least the early 1970s, and perhaps since the 1960s; (2) official figures of 7–8 billion are estimates that can't be proved, and the real number may be half or less; (3) the true replacement fertility rate is 2.5 children, not the 2.1 that mainstream sources use, and no society has ever recovered from falling below it; (4) the decline was engineered through social programming (women's liberation, the pill, the 1960s counterculture, the "Tavistock" influence on entertainment, and today's "woke" gender messaging), backed by organisations founded or funded by Rockefeller interests and the eugenics movement; (5) mainstream outlets began admitting the decline around 2019–2020, which the hosts read as confirming what they said years earlier; and (6) the decline fits the hosts' larger frame of an approaching "age change" (the yuga cycles, Pisces to Aquarius), with a population collapse followed by a "new era".

Hour 1

Opening thesis (0:59–2:47)

  • Crrow777 says they have long claimed population has been falling "since minimally the 70s". In 2019 he noticed mainstream researchers saying the same and dating it to 1974, "never comprehending why" the hosts chose the early 70s.
  • He asks what the numbers would be "now, with all the death that's occurred" since COVID.

"The myth of overpopulation" (2:47–6:14)

  • Jason reads a definition of the overpopulation "myth": the belief that population will exceed the Earth's carrying capacity and cause collapse, famine, water shortages, war, habitat loss and man-made climate change.
  • Crrow777: economic and social collapse is happening, but because of "leadership and corporation", not population. He calls corporations a "non-breathing entity given the rights of a person" whose limited liability means nobody is held responsible.
  • On resources, he says self-sufficient growers tell him one acre can feed five to ten families, and that the claim that feeding people depends on technology "just isn't the case".
  • He challenges the 8 billion figure: "Prove it. And they can't prove it."

Life expectancy and medicine (6:14–8:04)

  • Jason reads that population growth comes from longer lives, not high birth rates.
  • Crrow777 disputes this: he says most post-vaccination deaths were among the elderly, that people are on "five, six, seven, eight drugs", and that VAERS reporting "was off the table". He mentions that his late mother took no prescription drugs.

The UN's 8 billion milestone (8:04–10:41)

  • Jason reads UN.org: on 15 November 2022 the world population was projected to reach 8 billion. It took 12 years to grow from 7 to 8 billion and is projected to take about 15 years (to 2037) to reach 9 billion.
  • Crrow777 objects to words like "projected" and "estimated", and notes that not every country runs a census. He says China has to estimate its own population.
  • He argues crowding is an illusion of urbanisation: on a coast-to-coast US flight, "90% of your flight will be over unoccupied land".

Daniel Estulin and the 2.5 threshold (10:13–12:04)

  • Crrow777 credits Daniel Estulin's book on the Tavistock Institute (it "has parts on eugenics") with tables he used as the basis for his own research.
  • The central claim: according to Estulin, no civilisation has ever recovered from a birth rate below 2.5. Crrow777 says films and reports from 2019 used 2.0 instead, "a half a child less", while showing country after country in "dire straits".

Total fertility rate: the mainstream definition (12:04–13:57)

  • Jason reads a definition of TFR, and that as of 2023 it ranged from 0.78 in South Korea to 6.82 in Niger, correlating with economic development.
  • Crrow777: the cause is "cultural programming" in materialist, industrialised societies. In their earlier research, high birth rates were concentrated among Catholics, Latin Americans, "certain Muslim sects" and "certain people of colour", together "a very small portion of everyone overall".

Women's liberation as the turning point (13:57–17:03)

  • Crrow777 dates the change in birth rates to the early 1970s and attributes it to women's liberation, which he places at "the centre of all the Tavistockian program" alongside drugs and music.
  • He claims the movement was "run by a known CIA asset" and names Gloria Steinem, saying she "openly admitted" this "in two different places".
  • He claims that before women's lib, families typically started having children at 22–24, and that afterwards this moved to the early 30s, "as high as 34", which "alone" would have a massive effect across decades.
  • He describes bra-burning and the 1970s "streaking" craze as further social engineering.

Divorce, careers and the "sacred feminine" (17:03–19:53)

  • Jason adds that the movement pushed divorce and delayed families for careers, "sometimes until it was too late".
  • Jason says modern culture is destroying "the sacred feminine" by casting women as action heroes. Crrow777 says "woke" gender politics exists only to reduce the birth rate. He describes a documentary on falling birth rates that interviewed same-sex couples about adoption: "that is not a new baby in the world".
  • Jason calls it "a one-two punch": the first was women's lib, the second is today's youth culture.

Learning by example: entertainment (19:53–21:58)

  • Crrow777 says people learn by example. He points to The Big Bang Theory, "the biggest show in the world for about 10 years", for repeatedly presenting children as a drag.
  • He contrasts parental-leave and job-protection policies "mostly in European areas" with the US, where "everything is designed here to not make that very feasible".

More mainstream fertility figures (21:58–28:09)

  • Jason reads that as of 2020 the world TFR was 2.3, that global TFR has fallen rapidly since the 1960s, and that some forecasters expect it to drop below replacement.
  • Crrow777 rejects 2.1 again: "2.5 is the traditional number that I found everywhere before 2019."
  • Japan: he blames women entering careers, and cites villages that "in another 10 years ain't going to be nobody there" and graduate programmes trying to revive rural areas. He reads the number of UNESCO heritage designations in Japan, and worldwide, as "a prep up for a lot less people being around".
  • Jason reads UN estimates from 2017: almost half the world lives in countries below replacement (TFR under 2.1); a quarter of those are at 1.8–2.1 and three-quarters below 1.8. Low-fertility clusters are East Asia, Southern Europe, German-speaking Western Europe and the former socialist countries. Childbearing has fallen 50% since the mid-20th century, from about five children per woman to 2.3–2.5.
  • Crrow777 calls the figures a "numbers game": the passage moves between 2.1 and 2.3–2.5. He also asks what "down 50%" can mean if the earlier baseline was "2.5 or better".

Who really knows the population? (28:09–28:59)

  • Crrow777 says censuses can't cover sub-Saharan Africa or rural China, and suggests phone-data holders (e.g. Apple) probably know the real figure better than any government.

Malthus (28:59–40:18)

  • Jason reads a biography of Thomas Robert Malthus and the "Malthusian trap" from An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798). He gives Malthus's birth as "February 1776"; Malthus was in fact born in 1766, so this is either a misreading or a transcription error.
  • Crrow777: a 1700s generalisation is "almost useless". The modern problem is grocery-store dependence as a control mechanism. He cites the COVID toilet-paper panic as the first mass demonstration of it.
  • Jason: elites pushed people "off the land and into cities", then complained they were "breeding too much".
  • Crrow777 estimates at least 90% of supermarket products are unhealthy. He cites "reasonable research" that 80% of post-vaccination deaths were among people aged about 68 to 80-something, and argues published life expectancies (74 for men, 79–80 for women) are old numbers that "don't hold up anymore".
  • On war: he says he has never seen population estimates that account for World War II or Vietnam death tolls. Either war reduces population or it doesn't.
  • Jason reads that Malthus opposed the Poor Laws, supported the Corn Laws, influenced Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace, and was "said to be highly influential" on Francis Galton and eugenics.
  • Crrow777: "He influenced Darwin. What more do we need to know?" He calls Darwin "laughable", refers back to their earlier episode on dinosaurs (fabricated fossils, "hippos and giraffes put together"), and cites megalithic sites in Peru and Indian temples as evidence that the standard timeline is wrong.
  • He mentions the Georgia Guidestones (and their destruction) as having a "subconscious effect" on the world about acceptable population numbers.

Eugenics and "the unfit" (40:18–46:39)

  • Jason reads that in the late 19th century elites feared being "outbred by the unfit", and that eugenicists used Darwin to justify segregation, sterilisation and murder "for the greater good of evolution".
  • Crrow777: if the "unfit" have more children, by Darwin's own logic they are the fit. He suggests putting the rich on the same struggle, and asks how a democratic vote on who is "undesirable" would turn out. He says Darwin was introduced as "a psychological tool" to push these agendas, and argues from the trust relationship of grantor, trustee and beneficiary that "the creation" can't be governed by people who can't create life.

Close of hour 1 (46:39–50:26)

Themes and patterns

  • Distrust of official numbers. Population counts, life expectancy and replacement rates are all treated as estimates or deliberate obfuscation. The hosts propose alternative counts from phone data, car purchases or household spending.
  • 2.5 versus 2.1. The episode's main quantitative claim is that replacement fertility is 2.5 (credited to Estulin and "everything I read before 2019"). The hosts treat the mainstream 2.1 as a moved goalpost. The episode doesn't address how demographers derive 2.1 (from mortality and the sex ratio at birth).
  • A century-long institutional story. A single thread runs from Malthus through Darwin, Galton, Sanger, the Rockefellers, the Population Council, PRB, the WHO, UNFPA and the UN conferences, to the WEF. The read-aloud passages supply dates and facts; the hosts supply the motive.
  • Culture as the delivery mechanism. Women's lib, the pill, the Beatles and Sgt. Pepper, sitcoms (Leave It to Beaver, The Big Bang Theory), advertising, superhero women and "woke" gender politics are all presented as engineered to lower birth rates.
  • Health suspicions. Vaccines (deaths among the elderly, menstrual effects, fertility), pharmaceuticals, processed food, fluoride and chemtrails are offered as physical contributors. Crrow777 is frank that most of this is suspicion: "I can't possibly prove it", "How you ever prove it, I don't know."
  • Spiritual frame. The decline is ultimately placed inside an "age change" (yugas, Aquarius) that elites are said to anticipate and exploit, ending in a "new era" rather than extinction.
