How Banks Create Money Out of NOTHING

An empirical examination of banking mechanics reveals how commercial lenders legally invent the money supply out of thin air rather than intermediating customer deposits.

The three competing theories of banking

Historically, economic thought has rotated through three distinct models to explain how commercial banks operate:

  • Financial intermediation theory: The dominant modern textbook model, which posits that banks act strictly as middlemen. They collect deposits from savers, assess credit risk, and lend those exact funds to borrowers.
  • Fractional reserve theory: Dominant from the 1920s to the 1960s, this model views individual banks as intermediaries but argues that the banking system collectively creates money through the money multiplier and excess reserves held at the central bank.
  • Credit creation theory: The oldest framework, dating back over a century, which argues that both previous models are incorrect. Banks do not intermediate deposits individually or collectively; instead, every individual bank manufactures brand-new money whenever it extends credit.

Prominent economists have historically shifted between these views—John Maynard Keynes, for instance, supported credit creation early in his career before moving sequentially toward fractional reserve and finally financial intermediation.

The first empirical test of money creation

Despite banking existing for thousands of years, the mechanical accounting process of lending had never been formally tested in an empirical study. To settle the debate, the speaker arranged a controlled trial by taking out a real €200,000 bank loan while examining the bank's internal ledger operations in real time.

If financial intermediation were true, existing customer deposits would decrease to fund the loan. If fractional reserve theory were correct, excess reserves at the central bank would be drawn down. The experiment showed that neither occurred: no deposits were transferred, and reserves were left entirely untouched. Instead, the bank simply created a new deposit balance on its balance sheet out of nothing, validating the credit creation theory.

The legal mechanics behind fictitious deposits

Understanding why banks can create money requires examining banking law rather than conventional economic definitions:

  • Deposits are not property in custody: Legally, a bank deposit is not a bailment held in safekeeping. It is an unsecured loan that the customer makes to the bank, meaning the funds legally belong to the bank, and the depositor merely holds a debt claim against the institution.
  • Lending is an asset purchase: When a customer takes out a loan, the bank does not lend cash. Legally, the bank purchases the borrower’s signed promissory note (an IOU or debt security), adding it to the asset side of its balance sheet.
  • Inventing money through balance sheet expansion: To settle the purchase of the loan contract, the bank records an accounts payable liability to the borrower. It reclassifies this accounts payable obligation as a customer deposit.

Because external parties treat these ledger entries as spendable currency, this accounting practice creates roughly 97% of the modern money supply.
