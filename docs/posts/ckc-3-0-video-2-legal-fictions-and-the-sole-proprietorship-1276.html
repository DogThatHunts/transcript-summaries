Brandon Joe Williams outlines his alternative legal theories regarding sole proprietorships, dual citizenship classes under the Constitution, and strategies for navigating federal jurisdiction.

Legal resources and etymology

Brandon Joe Williams begins the lecture by emphasizing the importance of dictionaries and etymology in legal analysis. He recommends using Black's Law Dictionary, the Online Etymology Dictionary (etymonline.com), and the Etymology Explorer app. Williams explains that the etymological definition of "definition" involves setting boundaries or limits, arguing that any statement lacking clear boundaries cannot legally function as a definition.

The sole proprietorship as a distinct entity

Williams presents his theory on the sole proprietorship, arguing that it is a "disregarded entity" legally distinct from the individual owner. He claims that while the IRS and other institutions treat the owner and the business as the same entity, tax forms like the W-9 require a distinction between the owner's name and the business name. Williams asserts that operating through a sole proprietorship provides commercial protection and allows individuals to represent themselves in court without requiring a bar card.

Williams's theory of dual citizenship classes

According to Williams, the United States legal system recognizes two distinct classes of citizenship: * State citizens: Williams claims this class consists of "white citizens of a state" who possess direct constitutional rights under the Bill of Rights and the Ninth and Tenth Amendments, without being subject to the Fourteenth Amendment. * U.S. citizens: Williams argues this class was created via the Fourteenth Amendment and includes corporations, non-white individuals, and, under the precedent of Plyler v. Doe (1982), undocumented immigrants. He asserts that these individuals possess privileges and obligations administered by the federal government rather than inherent constitutional rights.

Williams further claims that "state citizens" cannot legally hold a Social Security Number (SSN), arguing that SSNs are taxpayer identification numbers belonging strictly to the sole proprietorship rather than the natural person.

Dual governments and territorial jurisdiction

Citing historical quotes from Thomas Jefferson and the Supreme Court case Downs v. Bidwell, Williams argues that the United States operates under two distinct coexisting governments: a constitutional republic of sovereign states and a federal congressional democracy governing federal territories and possessions (such as the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and Guam). Williams claims that the term "United States" in federal statutes refers strictly to the District of Columbia and territories under exclusive congressional jurisdiction, rather than the independent states of the Union.

The incorporation doctrine and criminal prosecution

Williams discusses the incorporation doctrine, explaining how courts have selectively applied portions of the Bill of Rights to Fourteenth Amendment citizens through case law, such as Gitlow v. New York (First Amendment) and McDonald v. Chicago (Second Amendment). Williams argues that under Hurtado v. California, Fourteenth Amendment citizens can be prosecuted via an "information" filed by a prosecutor without a grand jury indictment. Conversely, he claims that "state citizens" can only be prosecuted through a grand jury composed of other state citizens.

Passport application procedures for the sole proprietorship

Williams outlines his recommended method for applying for a U.S. passport using the DS-11 form. He advises applicants to apply on behalf of their sole proprietorship rather than themselves. His instructions include: * Entering the sole proprietorship's name and its associated taxpayer identification number (SSN). * Listing the place of birth as "Washington, D.C." or "District of Columbia" to align with the jurisdiction of the sole proprietorship. * Signing the application "without prejudice" (referencing UCC 1-308) as the "owner" of the entity.

Williams notes that while this method carries a risk of receiving requests for additional information from the Department of State, he views it as a way to maintain a legal separation between the individual and the federal government.
