The credit cycle warning behind Michael burry's 1987 crash prediction

Michael Burry of The Big Short fame has warned that the stock market is nearing a major top, pointing to the risk of a 1987-style crash. While many investors remain hesitant to bet against the current rally as the S&P 500 hits new highs, the underlying credit market suggests a critical shift. Unlike previous years of market optimism, the credit cycle has entered a distinct downswing, raising systemic risks across all speculative assets.

The 1987 parallel: why credit drives equity tops

The Eurodollar University host argues that the true lesson of the 1987 crash lies in the credit cycle rather than the mechanics of computer trading.

  • Shadow banking expansion: Leading up to 1987, non-bank credit (pension funds and insurance companies) grew at an astounding 20% rate, mirroring modern private credit expansion.
  • Early bank slowdowns: Well before the equity crash, traditional bank credit growth had slowed sharply, signaling the initial phase of a credit downswing.
  • The S&L crisis background: By 1987, bank failures had reached the hundreds for three consecutive years. This systemic banking distress created a fragile backdrop that ultimately collided with overvalued equity markets.

Today's market exhibits a similar divergence: soaring stock valuations alongside a deteriorating credit foundation.

Cracks in the credit foundation: fallen angels and rising spreads

The deterioration of the credit cycle is no longer confined to private markets; it is actively spilling over into public credit.

  • Private credit stress: Fitch Ratings reports that private credit loss rates are reaching record highs. Crucially, these losses are increasingly driven by maturity extensions and workarounds rather than simple payment-in-kind arrangements, indicating deeper structural distress.
  • The rise of "fallen angels": High-profile investment-grade companies, such as Oracle and Stellantis, are seeing their bonds trade like junk debt. Paul Benson notes that these downgrades represent classic late-stage credit cycle dynamics.
  • Widening spreads: Credit spreads on public junk bonds, particularly Triple-C rated debt, are rising as investors demand higher premiums for risk.

The "cockroach" problem: systemic underwriting failures

A primary driver of the credit downturn is the repeated emergence of "cockroaches"—fraudulent or severely distressed borrowers exposed by tightening liquidity. Despite industry promises to tighten standards, systemic underwriting failures continue to surface:

  • Tricolor and First Brands: These early failures exposed widespread issues with fake or double-pledged collateral.
  • MFS (UK): In February 2026, MFS collapsed due to fraudulent invoices and double-pledged collateral, despite industry assurances that anti-fraud measures were being implemented.
  • Radiant: The latest emerging failure involves Radiant, an iron business facing allegations of using fake invoices as collateral. This exposure has impacted Jefferies and the Point Bonita fund, which was already winding down from previous First Brands losses.

These recurring scandals demonstrate that lax underwriting standards during the credit bubble were endemic, leaving lenders highly vulnerable as liquidity dries up.

Macroeconomic convergence and the flattening yield curve

The credit downturn is converging with broader macroeconomic weakness, as signaled by the bond market.

  • Yield curve flattening: The flattening of the yield curve indicates that the market is pricing in significant financial and macroeconomic downside.
  • Labor market distress: Recent JOLTS data reveals emerging weakness in the labor market. As companies implement hiring freezes and trim employee hours, their ability to service debt obligations to private credit providers, junk bond holders, and collateralized loan obligations (CLOs) weakens.

While a full-scale market collapse may take years to play out, the structural alignment of credit deterioration, economic slowdown, and extreme equity valuations means the systemic risk is materially higher today than it was a year ago.
