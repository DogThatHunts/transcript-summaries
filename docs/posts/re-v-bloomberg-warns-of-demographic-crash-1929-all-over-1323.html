The demographic shift facing american real estate

A new white paper from the Mortgage Bankers Association (MBA) warns that the U.S. housing market is entering an era defined by a shortage of buyers rather than homes. The MBA projects that U.S. deaths will outnumber births by 2030, while the ReVenture analyst estimates this inflection point will occur by 2034. Over the next 10 to 20 years, this demographic shift is expected to result in structurally lower demand, elevated inventory, and declining home prices.

Global precedents: lessons from Japan, China, and Canada

The analyst points to international examples where demographic stagnation has already severely impacted real estate values:

  • Japan: Following its early 1990s bubble, Japan's aging population led to a 53% nominal drop in home prices between 1991 and 2010. Even today, nominal prices remain 20% below their 1991 peak.
  • China: Residential property prices have fallen 23% since peaking in 2021, coinciding with China's first organic population decline in decades in 2023.
  • Canada: Home prices have dropped 23% from their 2021 peak, fueled by a recent net population loss where deaths outnumbered births.

The uneven geography of organic population growth

The demographic downturn will not affect the U.S. uniformly. Using a "birth-to-death ratio" metric, the analyst highlights stark regional disparities:

  • High-growth areas: Utah leads the nation with 2.1 births for every death, followed by Texas (1.7) and Alaska. Metro areas like Austin (2.2) and Dallas (1.94) continue to add population organically.
  • Declining areas: Seventeen states are already in organic population decline. West Virginia is the lowest at 0.68 births per death. Florida metros like Tampa (0.91), Sarasota, and Naples are also experiencing more deaths than births, making them highly dependent on inbound migration to sustain housing demand.

While some argue that immigration will offset these declines, the analyst notes that U.S. immigration has plummeted to near 50-year lows as of 2025–2026, mirroring a broader pushback against immigration across the Western world.

The risk of elderly homeowner inventory gluts

Another critical metric for future housing supply is the percentage of homeowners over the age of 75. Areas with high concentrations of elderly homeowners face a higher risk of inventory surges from estate sales and senior transitions. Hawaii, Florida, and Arizona have the highest share of homeowners over 75, whereas Alaska, Utah, and Texas have the lowest, making the latter group more resilient to sudden supply spikes.
