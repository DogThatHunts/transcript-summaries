Macro updates and the fed's communication shift

Nils Karsten Larsen and Rob Carver discuss the recent transition at the top of the Federal Reserve, noting a surprisingly hawkish tone from the new FOMC chair, Kevin Warsh (referred to as Kevin Walsh in the transcript). Warsh has expressed a desire to revamp Fed communications, including moving away from forward guidance and reworking the data the central bank releases by moving away from old-fashioned survey methods.

Carver expresses concern over the ongoing degradation of US statistical data quality, attributing it partly to government spending cuts and recent government shutdowns. He warns that for systematic macro or economic trend strategies that rely on these indicators as inputs, losing access to reliable government statistics is "like a pilot flying when his instruments disappear." While third-party big data and AI services can sometimes forecast these statistics, Carver maintains that "more data is always better."

The rise of perpetual futures and exchange disputes

The hosts discuss the CFTC's recent approval of the first perpetual futures contract in the US, which is linked to cryptocurrency. Unlike traditional futures contracts that have set expiration dates and require traders to roll positions, perpetual futures have no expiration date and settle daily.

Carver explains that perpetual futures function essentially as daily-settling futures where the futures price and spot price remain virtually identical, eliminating rolling costs and overnight funding complexities for day traders. However, this development has drawn a lawsuit from the CME Group against the CFTC. The CME argues that perpetual futures should be classified as swaps. Carver notes that while perpetuals simplify things for speculators, they do not serve the original hedging purposes of physical producers (like farmers), who require specific dated contracts.

Portfolio optimization: Sharpe, skew, and drawdowns

Responding to a listener question from Ghana regarding how to balance Sharpe ratios against path risks like drawdown depth, duration, and loss clustering, Carver explains his minimalist approach to portfolio optimization. He rarely drops strategies unless there is overwhelming statistical evidence of a structural break.

Carver argues against using drawdown metrics to evaluate individual strategy components, stating that drawdowns naturally scale with a strategy's performance and history. Instead, he focuses primarily on the Sharpe ratio and occasionally uses skewness as a classification tool. For instance, he aims to limit negative-skew strategies (such as short volatility or certain emerging market FX carry trades) to a maximum of 30% of his portfolio, while allocating the rest to positive-skew strategies like trend following.

Analyzing the "factor zoo" and trend following as a risk premium

Carver highlights an Alpha Architect article titled The Factor Zoo has hundreds of animals, but only a handful of species, which explains that despite the hundreds of academic equity factors identified, only a small number of distinct underlying risk factors actually drive market returns.

This ties into a discussion of a sub-stack post from Beyond Passive Investing regarding what trend following adds to a portfolio's risk premia. Because trend-following equities are net-long on average, a significant portion of their return comes from the standard equity risk premium. Larsen and Carver debate whether investors should artificially tilt or neutralize their trend-following portfolios to avoid doubling down on equity beta if they already hold a traditional 60/40 portfolio. Larsen cautions against artificially reducing sectors, suggesting instead that investors let the trend program run or simply adjust their overall equity allocation directly.

Quantica's research on chasing trend performance

The hosts review a Q1 paper from Quantica titled Chasing Trends or Chasing Performance. The paper analyzes trend-following performance by asset class across three distinct periods: * 2000–2009: Broad-based performance split evenly among equities, currencies, and commodities. * 2010–2019 (The "CTA Winter"): Performance dominated almost entirely by fixed income, driven by predictable central bank rate cuts. * 2020–2026: Performance dominated almost entirely by commodities.

Quantica's research concludes that during periods when a single asset class dominates, performance is driven by a single dominant macro factor rather than diversification. Carver notes that a continuous trend-following system naturally adjusts to these regimes by sizing up positions in asset classes with stronger trends. Consequently, trying to tactically "chase" and manually shift allocations into recently successful asset classes is unnecessary and statistically ineffective.

Overfitting and the role of AI in strategy development

Carver addresses a Wisdom Trading blog post about the dangers of overfitting trading strategies, particularly in the era of AI. He warns that social media is flooded with overfitted backtests generated by AI.

Carver suggests that while AI is a useful tool for creative brainstorming and generating strategy ideas, the actual backtesting and validation must be conducted through a robust, independent process. A proper backtesting process must account for the fact that ideas are often conceptualized using historical hindsight. Ultimately, Carver notes, a backtest should not yield a simple "yes or no" on a strategy, but rather a probability of success that translates into a conservative portfolio weight.
