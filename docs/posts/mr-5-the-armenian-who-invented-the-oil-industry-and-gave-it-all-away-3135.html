Mr 5%: The Armenian Who Invented the Oil Industry and Gave It All Away

How a brilliant, multilingual Armenian middleman negotiated the borders of the modern Middle East, secured a legendary fortune, and left it all to public art.

In this episode, host David McWilliams talks with John Davis about the extraordinary life of Calouste Gulbenkian, the Armenian businessman known as "Mr. 5%" who laid the structural foundations of the modern global oil industry. Born in late 19th-century Constantinople, Gulbenkian leveraged his cosmopolitan background, multilingualism, and deep understanding of both Western capital and the Ottoman Empire to position himself as the ultimate broker. He famously negotiated a 5% stake in the Turkish Petroleum Company, helped redraw the borders of Iraq based on oil geology rather than ethnicity, and even traded oil capital with Joseph Stalin for priceless art from the Hermitage. Fleeing occupied France during World War II, Gulbenkian spent his final years in neutral Portugal, ultimately leaving his massive fortune—worth billions today—to establish a world-class public art foundation and museum in Lisbon.

The rise of the ultimate middleman

Calouste Gulbenkian was born in Constantinople during the second half of the 19th century into a successful Armenian merchant family. David McWilliams explains that under the Ottoman Empire, non-Muslim minorities like Greeks, Jews, and Armenians were taxed but largely left alone to run businesses. Because they were excluded from the civil service and the military, they became highly cosmopolitan traders.

Western Armenians, in particular, were urban, highly educated, and multilingual. Gulbenkian spoke Armenian, Turkish, Greek, French, and English. This unique background allowed him to understand both the "Oriental" mindset of the Ottoman Sultan and the "Occidental" mindset of Western financiers. In the 1880s, as oil was first being developed in Baku and Pennsylvania, Gulbenkian realized that this new commodity would soon drive the global economy. He positioned himself as the essential middleman between Western capital and Ottoman resources.

Redrawing the Middle east for oil

In 1912, anticipating the eventual collapse of the corrupt and weak Ottoman Empire, Gulbenkian established the Turkish Petroleum Company (TPC), securing a personal 5% share of the company's future oil rights. When the empire did collapse after World War I, Gulbenkian played the British and French mandates against each other to ensure the TPC retained its drilling rights across the newly formed nations of the Middle East.

McWilliams notes that Gulbenkian was directly responsible for drawing the borders of modern Iraq. He advised British negotiators to draw the lines based strictly on oil geology rather than ethnic or cultural realities. This decision directly led to the Kurds being denied their own homeland, as the British and French wanted control over the highly productive oil fields of Mosul, which sat directly beneath Kurdish territory.

Dealing with Stalin and the Hermitage art

During the 1930s, the Soviet Union desperately needed Western capital and technology to upgrade its oil production and secure hard currency. Because Gulbenkian spoke Russian and understood the region, he became the go-to broker for the Soviets, dealing directly with Joseph Stalin during the height of the Great Purges.

Rather than accepting cash, Gulbenkian demanded payment in art. The Soviet leadership, placing little value on the czarist art collections of the Romanovs, agreed to sell him masterpieces from the Hermitage Museum in St. Petersburg at a massive discount. Through these transactions, Gulbenkian amassed one of the greatest private art collections in human history, including works by Rembrandt, Rubens, and French Impressionists.

The final years in salazar's Portugal

When World War II broke out, Gulbenkian was living in Paris. As the Nazis occupied France, he briefly aligned with the Vichy regime before realizing he needed to escape to a neutral country. He chose Portugal, which was then ruled by the economist-dictator António de Oliveira Salazar.

Lisbon during the war was a hotbed of international espionage, filled with British, German, American, and French spies. Gulbenkian took up residence in the top suites of Lisbon's finest hotel, continuing to manage his global oil interests without ever having visited the United States or Iraq—the two regions that most heavily defined his business career.

A contrasting legacy for modern billionaires

Upon his death in 1955, Gulbenkian was the richest man in the world, with a personal fortune estimated at $10 billion in today's money. Rather than leaving his wealth to his children or hoarding it, he left the vast majority of his fortune to establish the Calouste Gulbenkian Foundation in Lisbon.

McWilliams contrasts Gulbenkian with modern tech billionaires like Elon Musk, Jeff Bezos, and Mark Zuckerberg. While modern tech figures often have a narrow engineering focus and spend their fortunes on vanity projects like space travel, Gulbenkian was a deeply cultured Renaissance man who believed that great art is the ultimate expression of human civilization. His foundation continues to fund scientific research, mathematics scholarships, and a world-class public art museum in Lisbon.
