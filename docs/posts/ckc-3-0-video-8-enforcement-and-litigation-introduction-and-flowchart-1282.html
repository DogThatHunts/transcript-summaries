A comprehensive breakdown of civil litigation procedures, flowchart mechanics, and discovery strategies designed for pro se litigants challenging corporate and governmental entities.

Overview of enforcement litigation and flowchart mechanics

This lecture serves as the introductory overview for the litigation phase of the CKC 3.0 course. The presenter outlines a structured approach to civil litigation for pro se litigants, drawing from personal case experience and collaboration with legal researchers like Joey Kimbrough ("Pro Se Joe"). The core message centers on abandoning ineffective "sovereignty" administrative remedies in favor of formal court action, leveraging discovery as the primary mechanism to compel responses, and shifting one's mindset to embrace litigation rather than fear it.

Reclassifying administrative processes as pre-litigation

  • The myth of administrative remedies: Traditional "sovereignty" processes—such as fee schedules, status corrections, notary presentments, and "magic letters"—are largely ignored or discarded by agencies and corporations.
  • Binary worldview: Every action in legal disputes falls into one of two categories: pre-litigation or litigation. There is no separate "administrative process."
  • Purpose of pre-litigation: Pre-litigation consists of ordering entities to perform, collecting evidence, and documenting their refusal or denial to establish the foundation for a formal legal complaint.

Anatomy of a cause of action

A viable complaint requires clearly articulated causes of action built upon three mandatory elements:

  1. Duty: Establishing that the defendant had a legal obligation (e.g., contractual duty or statutory duty under state commercial codes like the Uniform Commercial Code).
  2. Breach: Detailing the specific action or omission by the defendant that violated the established duty.
  3. Damages: Articulating the actual harm suffered as a direct result of the breach. Without duty and breach, legal damages cannot be claimed.

Court selection, case research, and filing mechanics

  • Court selection: State courts and small claims courts are strongly recommended over federal courts for pro se litigants due to simpler jurisdictional rules and lower procedural complexity.
  • Federal jurisdiction: Federal cases require establishing federal question jurisdiction or diversity of citizenship (28 U.S.C. Chapter 85).
  • Research tools:
  • PACER: Public Access to Court Electronic Records for federal dockets.
  • RECAP extension: A free browser extension by the Free Law Project that allows users to access previously purchased PACER documents without paying per-page fees.
  • Service of process: Utilizing professional process servers (e.g., ABC Legal) provides verified affidavits of service, preventing defendants from claiming non-receipt.
  • AI usage: Artificial intelligence should be used strictly for procedural queries and case retrieval ("playing fetch"), rather than drafting legal arguments or thinking on the litigant's behalf.

The discovery process as maximum leverage

Discovery is highlighted as the single most critical phase of civil litigation ("3.0 in a nutshell") where the balance of power shifts to the plaintiff:

  • Key tools:
  • Requests for admissions: Force the opposing party to admit specific facts or explain denials.
  • Interrogatories: Form interrogatories (standardized judicial questions) and special interrogatories (customized questions).
  • Requests for production: Compel production of original records, internal flowcharts, and account histories.
  • Depositions and subpoenas: Question witnesses under oath or gather records from non-parties.
  • Motions to compel and sanctions: Failure to comply with discovery requests should be countered with motions to compel and requests for monetary sanctions to penalize time-wasting tactics.

Trial mechanics, objections, and mindset shift

  • Court proceedings: Trials are divided into questions of law (handled exclusively by the judge) and questions of fact (evaluated by the jury in jury trials or by the judge in bench trials).
  • Objections and record preservation: Timely objections to adverse court orders or procedural errors are mandatory to preserve issues for potential appeal.
  • Psychological shift: Moving from fear of dispute to actively seeking resolution through court enforcement eliminates anxiety, preventing corporate entities from lowering a litigant's standard of living.
