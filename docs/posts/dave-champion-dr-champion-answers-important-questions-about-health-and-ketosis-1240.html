Author Dave Champion discusses human physiology, explaining why he believes ketosis is the body's natural state and critiquing mainstream dietary guidelines.

Executive summary

In an interview on The John Dowling Show, author and retired military veteran Dave Champion details the physiological principles outlined in his book Body Science. Champion argues that human physiology natively operates in ketosis—using fat and ketones for fuel—rather than "glucosis," which he characterizes as a stress response to high-carbohydrate diets. He outlines his concepts of the lymphatic lipid system (LLS) and hepatic lipid system (HLS), criticizes standard medical paradigms surrounding cholesterol, fiber, and salt, and shares insights on exercise, blood lab interpretations, and government food policy.

Key insights

  • Ketosis as the primary metabolic state: Champion contends that ancestral humans lived continuously in ketosis and that burning glucose for energy is an emergency mechanism to clear toxic sugar levels from the bloodstream.
  • Lymphatic versus hepatic lipid processing: Dietary fat enters cells naturally via chylomicrons in the lymphatic system (LLS), whereas high blood sugar triggers the liver to generate LDL particles (HLS), leading to fat storage in adipose tissue.
  • Absence of essential carbohydrates: Champion highlights that while essential amino acids and fatty acids exist, there are no essential carbohydrates, arguing humans can thrive exclusively on animal products.
  • Reevaluating standard blood labs: Champion asserts that conventional reference ranges for cholesterol and fasting glucose are modeled entirely on carbohydrate-consuming bodies and fail to accurately reflect health in keto-adapted individuals.
  • High sodium intake for muscle signaling: Champion reports consuming 13 to 15 grams of sodium daily to support cellular signaling and eliminate muscle cramps, directly challenging traditional medical recommendations to limit salt.

Understanding ketosis, glucosis, and fat transport

Champion explains that his interest in metabolic science began after witnessing a gym acquaintance lose 160 pounds on a diet consisting of 80% dietary fat. This led him to explore the distinction between ketosis and what he terms "glucosis"—the state in which 98% to 99% of the modern population exists due to high-carbohydrate, ultra-processed food intake.

To explain how the body processes fats under different dietary conditions, Champion introduced two terms:

  • Lymphatic lipid system (LLS): Dietary fat is emulsified in the digestive tract, packaged into chylomicrons, and transported through the lymph system into the bloodstream. Chylomicrons dock with cells across the body to deliver triglycerides directly for fuel.
  • Hepatic lipid system (HLS): When blood glucose rises sharply, insulin prompts the liver to convert excess glucose into triglycerides, releasing them as low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particles. Champion describes this as an emergency overflow mechanism that leads to fat accumulation in adipose tissue when cells have already fulfilled their energy needs via the LLS.

Challenging dietary assumptions: bio-individuality, fiber, and carbohydrates

Champion rejects popular arguments that individuals require fundamentally different diets based on blood type, gender, or bio-individuality. While acknowledging minor genetic variations, he argues that all human bodies metabolize proteins, fats, and carbohydrates through identical anatomical pathways. He specifically disputes claims that ketosis poses unique physiological challenges for women, noting a lack of supporting evidence.

Regarding plant foods and dietary fiber, Champion claims:

  • No essential carbohydrates: The human body requires specific fatty acids and amino acids from food, but can synthesize necessary glucose internally, making dietary carbohydrate intake optional.
  • Fiber is non-essential: Champion argues that the medical narrative surrounding dietary fiber is unfounded, pointing to research suggesting bowel regularity and stool consistency improve when fiber is minimized or removed, as on a carnivore diet.
  • Nutritional completeness of meat: According to Champion, all nutrients required for human health are available in animal-based foods.

Exercise, muscle recovery, and lymphatic circulation

Discussing physical training, Champion emphasizes that resistance training and moderate cardiovascular exercise are vital for lymphatic function. Unlike the circulatory system, which relies on the heart as a central pump, the lymphatic system relies on skeletal muscle contractions to move lymph fluid throughout the body.

Key physical observations noted by Champion include:

  • Full-body muscle activation: Engaging both upper and lower body muscles daily helps maintain continuous lymphatic movement and immune health.
  • Reduced post-workout soreness in ketosis: Champion reports that keto-adapted individuals experience significantly less delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) compared to those relying on glucose, though inactive muscles may feel tight until exercised.
  • Limits of extreme cardio: Champion asserts that while intense cardiovascular training improves athletic performance, it provides no additional general health benefits beyond moderate exercise.

Rethinking medical blood labs, cholesterol, and sodium

Champion criticizes the medical establishment for applying glucose-based reference standards to keto-adapted individuals. He argues that standard blood panels often misinterpret normal adaptations to ketosis as pathological:

  • Fasting blood sugar and A1C: Elevated fasting glucose (e.g., 100–105 mg/dL) can occur in long-term ketosis despite minimal carbohydrate intake and does not necessarily indicate insulin resistance.
  • Cholesterol as a repair molecule: Champion contends that high LDL cholesterol levels in carnivore or keto diets reflect increased repair activity and cellular support, dismissing standard warnings regarding statin interventions for keto practitioners.
  • High sodium supplementation: Champion recommends significantly higher salt intake than the standard 2.3-gram daily guideline, stating that supplementing up to 13–15 grams of sodium daily restores interstitial fluid balance and prevents muscle cramps during exercise.

Public health policy and ultra-processed foods

Addressing current public health initiatives, Dowling and Champion discuss Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s efforts to reform the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) and eliminate artificial food dyes such as Blue No. 1. Champion expresses measured optimism regarding regulatory focus on ultra-processed foods but calls for a more nuanced scientific approach that factors in Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) thresholds.

Champion emphasizes that consumer freedom must be preserved while advocating that individuals take personal responsibility for their nutritional choices rather than relying on standard public health guidelines.
