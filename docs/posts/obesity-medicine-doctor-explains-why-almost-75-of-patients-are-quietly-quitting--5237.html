Obesity Medicine Doctor Explains: Why almost 75% of patients are quietly quitting GLP-1 Meds ?

Why most people stop taking GLP-1 meds within a year — and what it really means for weight loss and healthcare.

Why almost 75% of patients quit GLP-1 medications

Dr. Baza Khaled, a board certified internal medicine and obesity medicine specialist, explains why a majority of patients discontinue GLP-1 medications like Ozempic and ZepBound within one year.

Key stats on Quitting GLP-1 medications

  • Between 50 and 75% of people who start GLP-1 medications stop using them within one year.
  • A Danish study found 52% of patients had stopped by the one-year mark, with 18% quitting by three months.
  • U.S. industrial analysis shows about two out of every three people stop within a year.
  • The rate varies by country, population, and study, but consistently lands between half and three-quarters of users.

Major reasons for Stopping

  • Cost: The number one driver. Out-of-pocket costs can reach $300–$400 per month, especially with high-deductible insurance plans. Many patients budget for these costs like car payments, and unexpected expenses can cause them to drop.
  • Side effects: Nausea, constipation, vomiting, and persistent stomach discomfort are common. Research shows people with prior psychiatric or gastrointestinal conditions are more likely to discontinue.
  • Age and demographics: Younger adults (18–29) are 48% more likely to quit than adults aged 45–59. Men are slightly more likely to quit than women. Lower-income individuals face higher discontinuation rates.

A structural systemic problem

  • No group is immune to quitting. Researchers found no clear subgroup with lower risk.
  • As one researcher put it, stopping GLP-1 meds isn’t a personal failure—it’s a systemic issue rooted in how these medications are prescribed, priced, and covered.

The biology of GLP-1 medications

  • GLP-1 medications mimic a natural gut hormone that signals fullness.
  • The body’s weight regulation system acts like a stretched rubber band—when the medication stops, the band snaps back.
  • GLP-1 medications were designed for long-term or lifelong use, but current healthcare systems lack support for sustained access.

What happens when you stop?

  • Most people regain a meaningful amount of weight within 6 to 12 months.
  • Trial data (e.g., STEP ONE Extension and SURMOUNT-4) show 40 to 50% of weight loss typically returns.
  • However, some people retain a solid portion of their weight loss, especially if they developed strong habits like better eating patterns, movement, and sleep.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misconception 1: Quitting means failure. This is not true. Most people stop due to cost or side effects, not personal shortcomings.
  • Misconception 2: Stopping is an all-or-nothing, permanent decision. It’s not. Some patients go back on, switch medications, or use lower maintenance doses.
  • Misconception 3: GLP-1 meds are short-term. A survey found interest in starting these meds dropped from nearly 50% to just 14% when people were told about common weight regain.

Practical recommendations

  • Have an honest cost conversation with your doctor before starting.
  • Explore manufacturer savings programs and understand long-term pricing.
  • If side effects occur, ask your doctor to slow down dose increases.
  • Build healthy habits—such as protein intake, movement, and sleep—alongside medication use.
  • If you must stop, don’t treat it as a secret failure. Talk to your doctor about options like lower maintenance doses, different medications, or future access plans.

Final insight

GLP-1 medications are one of the most powerful tools we have against obesity. But right now, the biggest barrier isn’t biology—it’s the system around it. Cost, insurance coverage, and lack of honest upfront information are driving most people to quit. Fixing this requires policy changes, not just medical solutions.
