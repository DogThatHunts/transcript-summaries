How the historical court of equity evolved from subjective judicial whims into a structured system of law governed by memorable maxims.

The historical court of equity faced criticism for being too subjective, with justice jokingly said to vary by the length of the Chancellor's foot. To address this, equity developed a structured set of guiding principles known as maxims to provide consistency and predictability. These maxims define who can access equitable remedies and establish the core foundation of modern trust law.

Key takeaways

  • From whims to maxims: LegaliBus explains that early equity courts relied too heavily on individual judges' moods, prompting the creation of "maxims"—short, repeatable principles that could be argued in court.
  • Gatekeeping justice: Some maxims open the door to remedies (e.g., "equity will not suffer a wrong to be without a remedy"), while others close it to those acting in bad faith (e.g., "he who comes into equity must come with clean hands").
  • The foundation of trusts: A central maxim of equity is that it "regards the beneficiary as the true owner," meaning that while a trustee holds the legal paperwork, equity protects the person for whom the property is actually intended.
