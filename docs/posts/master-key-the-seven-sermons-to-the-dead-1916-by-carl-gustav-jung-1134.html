Master Key | The Seven Sermons to the Dead (1916) by Carl Gustav Jung

A poetic and mystical exploration of Carl Jung's early Gnostic visions, outlining the cosmic balance of the Pleroma, the supreme power of Abraxas, and the path of human individuation.

Introduction to the sermons

In 1913, Carl Jung embarked on a profound exploration of his own unconscious, experiencing intense visionary states that lasted for several years. These experiences formed the foundation of his Red Book and his subsequent psychological theories. In 1916, he privately published The Seven Sermons to the Dead, attributing the mystical teachings to the ancient Gnostic teacher Basilides of Alexandria to frame his psychological insights through a mythological lens.

Sermon 1: the Pleroma and the necessity of distinctiveness

The teachings open with the dead returning from Jerusalem, unsatisfied with what they found, seeking wisdom. The speaker introduces the concept of the Pleroma—an infinite, eternal nothingness that is simultaneously a fullness containing all qualities in perfect balance.

  • The nature of the Pleroma: Because all opposites balance each other within the Pleroma, it has no distinct qualities, and thinking about it leads to self-dissolution.
  • The role of creation (creatura): Created beings exist within space and time, defined by their distinctiveness.
  • The principle of individuation: The primary struggle of any creature is to maintain its distinctiveness. Failing to differentiate oneself leads to falling back into the undifferentiated Pleroma, which represents spiritual dissolution and death.

Sermon 2: the emergence of opposites and Abraxas

The dead demand to know about God. The speaker explains that both God and the Devil are manifestations of the Pleroma that do not cancel each other out, but instead stand as active, opposing forces of fullness and emptiness.

  • God and the Devil: God represents effective fullness and creation, while the Devil represents effective void and destruction.
  • The supreme force of Abraxas: Above both God (Helios) and the Devil stands Abraxas, a deity representing effect and activity itself. Abraxas is more indefinite than God or the Devil, uniting both fullness and emptiness into a single, terrifying reality.

Sermon 3: the terrifying reality of Abraxas

The dead, identified as Christians, struggle to comprehend a deity that transcends their traditional view of the supreme good. The speaker describes Abraxas as the very force of life and becoming, containing both holy and accursed elements.

  • The union of opposites: Abraxas begets truth and lies, good and evil, light and darkness in the same action.
  • A double-sided power: While the sun brings life and the Devil brings death, Abraxas speaks the word that is both life and death simultaneously.
  • The reaction of man: To look upon Abraxas is blinding, and to worship this force is death, yet fearing it is wisdom, and accepting its reality brings a form of redemption.

Sermon 4: the multiplicity of the gods

The dead ask for further instruction regarding gods and devils. The speaker outlines a cosmology populated by countless deities, warning against the danger of reducing this multiplicity to a single god.

  • The four principal forces: The speaker identifies four primary divine principles: the god-sun (the beginning), Eros (the binding flame), the tree of life (the force of bodily growth), and the Devil (the force of dissolution).
  • The danger of monotheism: Forcing many incompatible gods into a single deity mutilates human nature, which thrives on distinctiveness.
  • Solitude versus communion: The gods are many and exist in vast, solitary distances. Humans, being weak, cannot endure total separateness and require community to survive, though they must balance this with individual solitude to avoid losing their identity.

Sermon 5: Spirituality, sexuality, and community

The dead mock the speaker and demand to hear about the church and holy communion. The speaker explains that the divine world manifests in human life through two primary forces: spirituality and sexuality.

  • The celestial and the earthly: Spirituality is described as a celestial, feminine force (mater coelestis) that conceives and embraces, while sexuality is an earthly, masculine force (phallus) that engenders and creates.
  • Forces that possess us: Humans do not own their spirituality or sexuality; rather, these forces act as powerful, independent daemons that possess and rule over humanity.
  • The balance of community: Community is necessary due to human weakness, but it must be balanced. Too much community leads to dissolution, while total isolation leads to sickness.

Sermon 6: the daemons of the soul

The speaker describes how spirituality and sexuality interact directly with the human soul, appearing as distinct symbolic entities.

  • The serpent: The daemon of sexuality approaches the soul as a serpent. It is an earthly, semi-demonic spirit closely aligned with the dead and physical desires, often leading humans toward earthly attachments.
  • The white bird: The daemon of spirituality descends as a white bird. It is a celestial soul that commands solitude, brings knowledge from those who have passed beyond, and acts as a messenger to the celestial mother.
  • The utility of the serpent: Although deceptive, the serpent serves a purpose by showing humans paths and insights that their conscious intellect cannot find.

Sermon 7: the inner star and the gateway of man

In the final sermon, the dead ask to be taught about humanity. The speaker describes man as a portal between two vast infinities.

  • The outer and inner worlds: Man is a gateway through which the outer world of gods and daemons passes into an inner, smaller infinity.
  • The individual star: Within this inner infinity, each person has a single, guiding star standing at their zenith. This star is the individual's unique god, goal, and final resting place.
  • The path to peace: In this inner realm, man acts as the creator and destroyer of his own world. By turning away from the overwhelming spectacle of Abraxas and focusing on this inner star, the soul finds rest and bridges the gap of death.

Following this final teaching, the dead fall silent and disperse like smoke into the night.
