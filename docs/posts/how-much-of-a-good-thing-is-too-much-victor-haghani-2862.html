How Much of a Good Thing is Too Much? Victor Haghani

A hedge fund pioneer explores why inherited wealth rarely survives, how much risk is too much, and why trend-following beats fundamental analysis.

How much of a good thing is too much? Victor Haghani

Victor Haghani, founding partner of Long Term Capital Management and author of The Missing Billionaires, discusses the paradox of wealth preservation: why a million dollars invested in the stock market in 1900 would have grown to over $130 billion today, yet almost no American billionaire can trace their wealth back to a millionaire ancestor from 125 years ago. The answer, he argues, lies in two opposing mistakes—taking too little risk and taking too much—and in the difficulty of maintaining wealth over decades relative to creating it.

Five key insights

  1. Wealth dissipates through poor risk decisions. Families that took no risk missed market growth and couldn't keep pace with inflation and spending; those that concentrated in volatile assets (like the Vanderbilts in transportation stocks) suffered from volatility drag that compounded over decades into near-total loss.

  2. Holding wealth is harder than making it. Making money may require concentrated bets and high risk over a short period; preserving it requires a different skill set—managing volatility, spending discipline, and long-term thinking over 50+ years, not one year.

  3. Expected utility frameworks explain why unlimited risk tolerance is mathematically ruinous. Sam Bankman-Fried's claim of linear utility (that every extra dollar has equal value) logically implies a 99% chance of going bust. Any utility function that isn't concave leads to bankruptcy.

  4. Dynamic asset allocation should respond to changing risk and return. Expected returns and volatility of equities relative to safe assets change over time. Investors should adjust their stock allocation based on these shifts, using metrics like earnings yields and realized volatility rather than static allocations.

  5. Trend-following outperforms fundamental analysis because a little knowledge is dangerous. Investors with deep but incomplete information (like TPG's $40 billion loss on natural gas) often ignore public signals. Mechanical trend-following, by contrast, avoids the trap of private conviction overriding market signals.

The missing billionaires puzzle

Haghani opens with a striking historical fact: in 1900, there were 4,000 American millionaires; today there are 760 billionaires. Yet almost none of those billionaires descend from those millionaires. A million dollars invested in the stock index in 1900 would have grown to over $130 billion today. Where did the money go?

The hypothesis is poor decisions about risk. Some families took no risk at all, investing in treasury bills and watching inflation erode their wealth after tax. Others, like the Vanderbilts, concentrated in volatile assets with 30–40% annual standard deviation. That volatility drag, combined with fixed spending policies, eroded compound returns over decades.

The problem is not just volatility itself but the collision between high volatility and fixed spending. If you spend a fixed percentage of wealth each year and your portfolio swings wildly, the bad years hit harder than the good years help. Over 50+ years, this asymmetry destroys wealth.

Risk, return, and personal utility

Haghani emphasizes that the decision of how much risk to take depends on three factors: expected return of the risky asset relative to safe assets, the volatility of the risky asset, and personal risk aversion (diminishing marginal utility of wealth).

He illustrates this with Elon Musk's portfolio, which carries roughly 60% annual volatility. Assuming an 8% expected return and 5% borrowing costs, the volatility drag (½σ²) is about 18%, making the median return negative 10% per year. The most likely outcome for Musk's wealth in 10 years is down 90%, even though there's a small tail probability of extreme wealth. This is pure math, not a value judgment.

The St. Petersburg paradox—a bet with unlimited expected value but a 99% chance of ruin—illustrates why unlimited risk tolerance is incoherent. Bankman-Fried claimed linear utility (no diminishing marginal utility) to justify unlimited risk-taking for charity. But the logical implication is a 99% chance of going bust and a 1% chance of owning all the world's money. Few would accept that trade-off.

Dynamic index investing and market efficiency

Rather than a static allocation, Haghani advocates adjusting equity exposure based on forward-looking risk and return. Expected returns can be estimated from earnings yields (adjusted for inflation and retained earnings) or from consensus forecasts from major institutions. Risk can be measured via implied volatility from options, realized volatility, or momentum.

When risk rises and expected returns fall, reduce equity exposure. When risk falls and returns rise, increase it. This is not market timing; it is disciplined rebalancing based on observable metrics.

On market efficiency, Haghani disagrees with Cliff Asness's definition. Asness asks whether prices reflect true fundamental value; Haghani asks whether it has become easier or harder to beat the market. He believes it was easier in the 1980s than today—there were more anomalies, less information was available, and fewer sophisticated competitors. But he notes that even obvious inefficiencies (like Palm Pilot vs. 3Com) are hard to profit from due to execution risk and short-squeeze dynamics.

The investor-fund gap and behavioral costs

Morningstar's "Mind the Gap" research shows that investor returns lag fund returns by about 1% per year over long periods. The gap is caused by return chasing: investors buy after strong performance and sell after drawdowns. Cathie Wood's ARK fund is a striking example—the fund is up 5x since 2014, but investors have lost billions because they entered at peaks and exited at troughs.

Target-date funds and balanced funds show the smallest gap because investors are less likely to trade them. The solution is a quantitative, formulaic approach that removes emotion from decision-making.

Haghani notes an apparent paradox: return chasing is clearly harmful, yet trend-following (cutting losses, letting profits run) is historically one of the best strategies. The difference is that trend-following is mechanical and based on price momentum, not on extrapolating past returns. Return chasing is based on the belief that past performance predicts future results. Trend-following is based on the observation that markets have momentum in the near term.

Private information and the crystal ball experiment

Haghani conducted an experiment in which participants were given tomorrow's Wall Street Journal headline today but with market movements blacked out. Most participants did not profit significantly, even with perfect foresight of news. Experienced traders did better, but even they did not triple their money. The lesson: news follows the market more than the market follows news. Font size in headlines correlates with market moves, not the other way around.

Private information is particularly dangerous because it causes investors to ignore public signals. Haghani's father, living in Iran before the 1979 revolution, trusted a private tip from a military contact over the obvious public signals (burning banks, martial law, demonstrations). He nearly lost everything.

Diversification and the 4% rule

Bessembinder's research on all US stocks ever listed shows that just 4% of stocks have generated all of the market's return over the past century. Half of all stocks underperformed treasury bills. This is not surprising given that stocks have log-normal distributions—they can only fall to zero but can rise indefinitely. Most stocks lose money in real terms, even though the market as a whole has a positive expected return.

The implication: you cannot be too diversified if diversification is free. Buy the haystack, not the needle. Buffett's advice to concentrate in a few stocks may work for a genius, but it has misled many ordinary investors into thinking they can identify the next Apple or Microsoft.

The MicroStrategy and leveraged ETF trap

MicroStrategy, under Michael Saylor, is executing a leveraged buyback of Bitcoin: issuing equity and convertible bonds to buy Bitcoin, which pushes up both Bitcoin and MicroStrategy's stock price. The stock trades at 2.5x its Bitcoin holdings, creating a feedback loop.

Two-times leveraged ETFs on MicroStrategy now control about 10% of the company's market cap. If volatility is 10% per day (160% annualized) and expected returns are 10% per year, the volatility drag (½σ²) is 18%, making the median return negative 8% per year. The most likely outcome after one year is down 98%.

Haghani compares this to a casino: zero commissions have made options trading feel like a free game, but spreads are massive and the odds are against retail traders. Young people are attracted to zero-day options and meme stocks because they feel like a path to wealth, but they are lottery tickets. The real path to wealth is developing human capital, earning more than you spend, and investing in a diversified portfolio.

Career advice and organizational structure

Haghani concludes with advice from his father on choosing between job offers. Rather than comparing salaries, ask: if I do well, how far can I rise? What is the career path? Haghani chose Salomon Brothers over JPMorgan because Salomon was flatter and more dynamic—he was three levels below the CEO, could see him walking around, and had more room to grow. JPMorgan had a more structured training program but less upside.

The lesson applies to any major decision: focus on optionality and growth potential, not on current compensation.
