The Fundamentals: Law Built on Roles You Never Agreed to Play. Here's A Complete Understanding

NotAPerson.org argues that statute binds only roles, never the living human. Here is the full argument, every authority linked, and a check against what the courts have held.

This 31-minute narrated reading comes from NotAPerson.org, a UK site whose articles are credited to "NAP | Jason". It sets out what the site calls the foundation beneath all its other material. Its claim is that law runs in two layers. The common law binds the living human directly. Statute binds only artificial roles such as "the taxpayer", "the driver" or "the person liable to council tax". A statute reaches the human, the author argues, only through an agency the human knowingly accepted and a written instrument that moved their property into the role, and neither is ever present. The piece is unusually candid about its own weak points: it marks which steps are settled law and which are "our construction", and it names the mainstream counter-argument. This expanded version follows the argument step by step, links every statute, case and historic text it relies on to the primary source, and adds a claim check. The check compares each legal statement with what the cited authority actually says, and with how UK courts have ruled on closely related arguments.

Key insights

  • Two layers, two ways of reaching you. Common-law duties (do no harm, keep your agreements) attach to the human being as such. Statutory duties, the author argues, attach to roles the statute itself creates, which need a human to act as their agent.
  • The test the author proposes: description or constituted subject? Where a statute merely describes a person who does something, the author concedes the duty falls on the human. Where it bills, numbers, summonses and enforces against a role over time, they say the statute is operating a construct.
  • Two "missing" links at every acceptance. No one discloses that you are taking on a role, and no written instrument transfers your earnings or property into it. From this the author derives a "recurrent resulting trust" that returns the benefit to you each time value moves.
  • The authorities are real; several of the uses are not. The statutes are quoted accurately. But the leading resulting-trust case the article relies on held that no resulting trust arose, and the case cited for "acceptance and burden of proof" is about domicile.
  • The courts have answered the core premise. The article says its argument has not been met "on the merits in these terms". No judgment found addresses its trust mechanism by name. But the High Court has repeatedly held that statute applies to the individual without consent, and that the "living man versus legal fiction" split has no legal basis.

1. Two layers of law

The author starts with an observation. Most people see "the law" as one mass. Murder and a late tax return feel like the same kind of wrong. The author says these belong to two different systems. Each system has a different origin and a different way to reach you. ▶ 02:42

Layer 1 is the common law. Parliament did not write it. Courts developed it, case by case, over centuries. It settles on four rules: do not harm another person, do not take or damage their property, do not deceive them to their loss, and keep the agreements you freely made. The author cites Blackstone's Commentaries on the Laws of England for "inherent" rights: your person, your liberty and your property. These rights belong to you by nature. No authority granted them. [R15]

"The common law binds the living being directly. No role, no registration, no construct, no agent."

The author stresses one limit at this point. Common-law liability for real harm is "wholly untouched" by the theory. Nothing in the article lets a person escape liability for injury or loss to another. ▶ 04:39

Layer 2 is the statutory overlay. Parliament makes it. It contains hundreds of thousands of rules: taxes, licences, registrations and penalties. The author identifies one difference as the key. The common law needs a victim. Most statutory duties need only a rule and a breach. You can get a penalty for a late form. You can get a penalty for an unlicensed activity that hurt no one. The author concludes that the two layers are different in kind: "One answers injury, the other administers a system."

Blackstone does draw a distinction of this kind. He divides persons into "natural persons", which are "such as the God of nature formed us", and "artificial" persons, which "are created and devised by human laws". But his artificial persons are corporations and bodies politic. He does not treat statutory roles such as "the taxpayer" as artificial persons. That step is the author's own. [R15] Partly supported

2. How a statute builds its subjects

The author next contrasts two kinds of person. A natural person has inherent rights. A legal person, such as a company, has only the rights that the law grants. Before incorporation, a company is nothing. It has no body, no mind and no conscience. So it cannot sign, speak or appear. A living human must act for it.

The article cites the Companies Act 2006, section 155. That section says: "A company must have at least one director who is a natural person." The citation is accurate. ▶ 06:27 [R3] Supported

The article then quotes the Interpretation Act 1978, Schedule 1: "'Person' includes a body of persons corporate or unincorporate." The author argues that the statutory word "person" is built around entities that cannot act by themselves. The quotation is correct. But the definition uses the word "includes". It adds bodies of persons to the ordinary meaning of "person". It does not remove human beings from that meaning. Section 5 of the same Act applies the Schedule 1 definitions "unless the contrary intention appears". ▶ 06:55 [R4] [R5] Partly supported

Next comes the step that the author says "almost no one is shown". A statute rarely says "we hereby create a legal person". Instead, it defines a role, such as the taxpayer, the driver, the occupier or the person liable. Then it loads that role with duties. The author argues that a position defined only by granted duties is, in its structure, the same thing as a legal person. So it needs a living agent too.

The author labels this step clearly as their own:

"That a company is a construct needing an agent is settled beyond dispute. That a statutory role, defined by its duties, is the same kind of construct is our reasoned extension, and we hold it to a test in the open."

The test is description versus constituted subject. If a statute only describes a human ("a person who drives"), the author concedes that the duty falls on the human. If the statute constitutes the role (it bills the role, gives it a reference number, summonses it and enforces against it), then the author says the statute "has stopped describing a human and started operating a construct". ▶ 08:24

3. The clearest case: council tax

The author chooses council tax as the hardest test for their theory. They say it is the case where the orthodox reply ("it is just a description") is most tempting.

The first point is about timing. No one is born "the person liable to council tax". At birth there is no dwelling, no residence and no liability. The role exists only because the Local Government Finance Act 1992 defines it. ▶ 08:58

The second point is about how the Act treats the role. The author says the statute does more than describe a resident. It bills the liable person, gives it a reference, summonses it and enforces against it. In England, the author notes, it can commit the person to prison. So the author calls the role a "standing subject" of duties that continues over time, not a passing description.

The machinery that the author describes is real:

  • Section 6 of the Act sets out who is liable. It uses a ranked list: a resident freeholder first, then a resident leaseholder, then a tenant, a licensee, any resident, and finally the owner. Liability follows residence or ownership. It does not depend on consent. [R6]
  • If the tax is unpaid, the council applies to a magistrates' court for a liability order under regulation 34 of the Council Tax (Administration and Enforcement) Regulations 1992. [R7]
  • Commitment to prison is possible in England under regulation 47. It is a last resort. Enforcement agents must first fail to find enough goods. The court must then hold an inquiry into the debtor's means, with the debtor present. It can commit only if non-payment comes from "wilful refusal or culpable neglect". [R8]

These facts support the author's description of the process. Supported They do not support the conclusion that the role is a separate entity. The High Court considered a council tax challenge of this kind in R (Kofa) v Oldham MBC [2024] EWHC 685 (Admin). The claimant argued that the 1992 Act needed her individual consent or a contract. The court held that consent is given through Parliament. It said that individual consent would be "impossible and inappropriate", and refused permission. [R21]

The third point is the author's "deepest" one. Your inherent status as a human bears inherent rights and duties. Council tax liability is a granted obligation, so, the author says, it cannot be part of that status. It can reach you only if you take on, or are validly connected to, the separate role where the liability "lives". The author is careful to add that their theory does not say statute overrides inherent rights. They say the liability "does not inherit you" and needs a connection that you "never knowingly made".

"If the construct and connection analysis holds here, it holds everywhere, because nobody was born the taxpayer, the driver, the licensee, or the employer either."

4. The theatre in the language

The author points to the vocabulary of law: person, role, capacity, act, perform, appear, represent and office. They say this is the vocabulary of the stage. "Person" comes from the Latin persona, the mask that an actor wore. The author says a role is a part, and the living human is the actor that the part requires: "A part cannot play itself." ▶ 13:18

The etymology is correct. Persona meant "mask" and also "role, part, character". The word probably came into Latin from the Etruscan φersu, a masked figure. [R31] [R32] Supported The origin of a word does not fix its legal meaning, however. The article offers this point as an illustration, not as a legal authority.

5. The bridge: agency, disclosure and an instrument

This is the centre of the argument. A construct cannot act. So it needs a human to participate as its agent. The author says this participation is "the only bridge" between the statutory layer and you.

The author then states the rules for an agency or a fiduciary office. No one becomes an agent, a director or a trustee by accident. A fiduciary office cannot be forced on anyone. Acceptance needs a real agreement, including knowledge of what the role carries. The burden of proof is on the person who asserts the agency. For this, the article cites Robins v National Trust Co [1927] AC 515. ▶ 14:51

Robins is a Privy Council appeal from Ontario about domicile. Secondary sources describe it as authority that the party who asserts a change of domicile must prove it. It is not a case about agency or fiduciary acceptance. The general idea that the asserting party carries the burden exists in law, but this case does not apply it to statutory roles. [R16] Partly supported

The system treats you as the agent because you once filed a form, paid a demand or signed in the role. The author's reply: "performance is not acceptance". Then the author says that two things are missing at every presumed acceptance.

The first missing thing is disclosure. No form tells you that you are "taking on a separate granted persona". You cannot give informed acceptance to a change that nobody disclosed to you. ▶ 15:29

The second missing thing is an instrument. Your labour, earnings and property can answer for the role's debts only if the beneficial interest in them passed to the role. The article cites the Law of Property Act 1925, section 53(1)(c). That section requires a disposition of an equitable interest to be "in writing signed by the person disposing of the same, or by his agent". The author says that no such writing ever exists. ▶ 16:37 [R9]

The section says what the article quotes. But it governs the disposal of an equitable interest that already exists under a trust. The article assumes that every tax payment or charge is such a disposal, into a trust held by "the role". No authority found treats a tax payment that way. Under the statutes in §3, the duty to pay arises directly. Nothing has to move into a role first. Partly supported

The article also cites Knight v Knight (1840) for the "three certainties" that a trust needs: certainty of intention, of subject matter and of objects. That citation is correct. In Knight itself, the words were too vague, so the court found no trust. [R17] Supported

6. The "recurrent resulting trust"

From the two failures, the author draws the consequence that they say "most accounts miss". Equity has an answer when a transfer of beneficial interest fails: the resulting trust. The benefit goes back to where it began. The article cites Westdeutsche Landesbank v Islington [1996] for this. ▶ 17:14

"The benefit never truly left. The recipient holds an empty legal title. The living being keeps the substance."

The author then says that the trust does not arise once, at birth registration. It arises every time value moves toward the role without an instrument: each wage paid through "the taxpayer", each asset held in the legal name. So the role is left "holding legal title to an empty shell again and again". ▶ 17:46

This is the step where the cited authority works most against the article. In Westdeutsche, a bank paid money to a council under a contract that was later held void. The bank argued that a resulting trust arose at once, because the transfer had failed. The House of Lords rejected that argument. It held that a resulting trust depends on intention and on the recipient's conscience. No trust arose, and the bank had only a personal claim to repayment. Lord Browne-Wilkinson's judgment in that case is the standard modern authority that a failed transfer does not automatically create a resulting trust. [R18] Not supported

The "recurrent" version is the author's own extension. No court has adopted it.

7. Both sides of the desk

The author points out that the authorities that send the demands are constructs too. The article says HMRC is a legal person created by the Commissioners for Revenue and Customs Act 2005. It says the DVLA and the council are constructs too. Each acts only through its officers. So, the author says, every statutory demand is "one construct addressing another construct". ▶ 19:37

The details are mixed. Section 1 of the 2005 Act lets the Crown appoint Commissioners for HMRC by Letters Patent. County and district councils are bodies corporate under the Local Government Act 1972, section 2. But the DVLA is an executive agency of the Department for Transport. It is not a separate legal person. [R10] [R33] Partly supported

From this point the author makes the argument's moral case. In practical terms, they say, people who operate the statutory system use it to control the conduct of other people and to take their property. By Layer 1's own standard, that is harm and loss. The author limits this claim to cases where a person never knowingly accepted the role:

"We are not saying statute is theft, or that Parliament has no right to legislate, or that all taxation is injury."

8. What the orthodox view relies on, and what the courts have said

The author sets out the opposing case directly. They say the orthodox view agrees with most of the components: a company needs an agent; agency must be accepted; a fiduciary office cannot be forced; and a transfer of benefit needs an instrument. The orthodox view disagrees at two points, and these are the two points that the author flags as their own construction. ▶ 22:03

  1. It denies that a statutory role is a construct at all. "The driver" and "the person liable" are descriptions. The duty attaches to the human directly. The author calls this the "direct attachment reading" and admits that it is "the mainstream default".
  2. Because of this, it denies that any acceptance is needed. The author calls this "the strongest counter, and we do not pretend it is weak".

The author then says that they looked for a case that "squarely" decides the question against them, and did not find one:

"The question appears not to have been put to a court in this form and answered on its substance. That is not proof we are right."

That statement is narrow, and in its narrow form it holds. This check found no judgment that discusses the Law of Property Act 1925, section 53(1)(c) or the "recurrent resulting trust" mechanism by name. Partly supported But courts in England have ruled many times on the premises that the mechanism depends on:

  • In Salmon v Leeds Crown Court [2021] EWHC 1076 (Admin), the claimant said that he was a "living man", distinct from a "fictional corporate person". The court said: "The Claimant's consent was not and is not required for statute law to apply to him." [R23]
  • In Kofa (see §3), the court held that council tax liability does not need individual consent or a contract. [R21]
  • In Southwark LBC v Hadjimina [2026] EWHC 954 (KB), the defendant separated "the legal fiction" from "the living breathing man". The court said that "all citizens are equal under the law and all are subject to the law". It called the consent theory "hopelessly misconceived". [R22]
  • In Hodder v Peacekeepers Foundation [2026] EWCC 37, a group offered a prize to anyone who could refute the idea that statute needs individual consent. The court found "no legally arguable riposte" to Parliament's power to bind individuals, and gave judgment against the group. [R24]
  • Canada's Meads v Meads 2012 ABQB 571 catalogues these "split person" and consent arguments at length. UK courts often cite it. [R29]

So the article's central premise has been answered, though not in its exact terms. That premise is that statute reaches the human only through a connection that the human accepts. The courts apply the "direct attachment reading" as settled law, not as a default that is still open. Not supported

9. "The ceiling": making the trust express

The article ends with a practical step. Equity already separates legal title (whose name is on a thing) from beneficial interest (who gets the good of it). The author notes that equity prevails where it conflicts with the common law. This is correct: Senior Courts Act 1981, section 49 says that "the rules of equity shall prevail" in civil matters in England and Wales. ▶ 25:28 [R11] Supported

"The ceiling" is the site's name for a deed of transfer. The deed puts the beneficial interest into a private express trust. The statutory role becomes a bare trustee, and the living human becomes the beneficiary. The author says that the deed "simply states, once and expressly, what equity has been doing by operation of law all along".

The author sets out the limits openly. They call the trust analysis of the legal person "our own considered extension". They name two doctrines that constrain any such trust:

  • Insolvency Act 1986, section 423 lets a court reverse a transaction at an undervalue that was made to put assets beyond the reach of creditors. In Allen v Hurst [2022] EWHC 2204 (Ch), a declaration of trust for the debtors' children was set aside on this ground. [R12] [R28]
  • The sham doctrine comes from Snook v London and West Riding Investments [1967]. A document is a sham if the parties intend it to give the appearance of rights that differ from the real ones. In JSC Mezhdunarodniy Promyshlenniy Bank v Pugachev [2017] EWHC 2426 (Ch), a settlor who kept control of his trusts was held to remain their sole beneficial owner. [R19] [R27]

Supported Courts have also dealt with trust deeds of this family. In Ryan v LVR Capital [2024] EWHC 1866 (Ch), an "irrevocable pure common law trust", a split identity and promissory notes were called "legal gobbledegook". The claims were struck out, and the court made a civil restraint order. In Perkin v Hawkins [2025] EWHC 3053 (KB), five applications built on a "corporate fiction" and a trust counterclaim were certified "totally without merit". [R26] [R25]

10. Magna Carta and the free man

The author ends with the oldest text in the argument. They say Chapter 39 of Magna Carta 1215 is "still in force". It provides that no free man shall be stripped of his standing or possessions "except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land". The article says the Supreme Court drew on it in R (UNISON) v Lord Chancellor [2017] UKSC 51. ▶ 27:34

The quotation is accurate to the 1215 text. But the 1215 charter is not on the statute book. Its chapters 39 and 40 survive as chapter 29 of the 1297 confirmation, which is still in force. In UNISON, Lord Reed referred to Magna Carta at paragraph 74. He quoted chapter 40, on the right to justice, and noted that it remains law in chapter 29 of the 1297 version. The judgment was about access to the courts. It struck down tribunal fees. [R13] [R14] [R20] Partly supported

The author then gives their reading, and marks it as their own. A presumption is "neither lawful judgment nor the law of the land". An assumption with no disclosure, no acceptance and no instrument does not meet that standard. The courts in §8 take the opposite view. For them, a statute passed by Parliament is the law of the land, and it binds without individual consent.

11. The author's own summary

The article closes with a summary in three parts. ▶ 28:31

  • What the author calls settled: constructs need accepted agents; a fiduciary office cannot be forced; the burden is on the person who asserts; transfers of benefit need instruments; title and benefit are distinct; failed transfers "result back". Most of these are correct general statements. The last one is not, as §6 shows.
  • What the author calls their construction: statutory roles are constructs; inherent status does not contain granted liability; performance is neither disclosed acceptance nor an instrumented transfer; so the resulting trust recurs.
  • What the author does not claim: "immunity, sovereignty, escape from the common law, or that any of this is beyond contest."

"We say it is sound, built from the law's own settled materials, failing twice over at every presumed acceptance, and not yet answered on its merits."

The verdict of this check. The article's building blocks are mostly real law, quoted accurately. The construction that joins them is the author's own, and two of its supports fail on inspection: Westdeutsche rejects an automatic resulting trust, and Robins concerns domicile. Its central premise is that statute binds the human only through an accepted role. English courts have rejected that premise in direct terms, and repeatedly, even where the arguments were not framed in this article's exact vocabulary. Anyone considering a deed or trust based on this theory should note Ryan, Perkin and the Insolvency Act 1986, section 423, and take independent legal advice.
