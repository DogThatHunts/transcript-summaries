Technical analysis for long-term investors

Lance argues that even buy-and-hold investors should understand basic technicals to know when to add to a position, not necessarily to day trade.

Support and resistance

  • Price tends to consolidate in ranges: the bottom is support, the top is resistance.
  • In a range-bound market, buy near support, sell near resistance.
  • When price breaks out of the range, that's often the start of a new trend.

Example: Ethereum consolidating at $2,000

  • Ethereum had multiple legs down; sentiment was bearish ("crypto winter," everyone selling).
  • It then consolidated tightly at $2,000 resistance.
  • When it broke above $2,000, technical traders bought, momentum built, and the move accelerated.
  • This illustrates how technicals can amplify sentiment shifts.

The "right side of the v"

  • A downtrend is defined by lower lows and lower highs.
  • An uptrend is defined by higher highs and higher lows.
  • Don't buy on the way down (fighting the trend). Wait for the turn—the break of the downtrend—then buy.
  • This gives you a clear stop (at the prior lows) and aligns you with the trend, not against it.

Example: Oil after the spike to $120

  • Oil spiked parabolic on panic.
  • Lance waited for it to form support and then break that support to the downside.
  • He shorted the break of support, with a stop at the prior highs.
  • This is the opposite of fighting the trend; he's riding the reversal.

Moving averages

  • A moving average (20-day, 30-day, etc.) is simply the average closing price over a period.
  • Rule of thumb: Be long (or add to longs) when price is above the moving average. Be flat or short when price is below.
  • This works on any timeframe and for any asset.
  • After a major panic or capitulation, wait for price to cross back above the moving average before adding.

Example: Gold at $4,000

  • Gold crashed from $5,600 to $4,000.
  • A gold bug waiting for the right time to add would wait for gold to consolidate and then break above the 20- or 30-day moving average.
  • That break signals a change in trend and is a better entry than buying on the way down.

Combining macro and technicals: when does it work?

Lance is clear: macro alone is not enough; technicals alone can work, but the best trades align both.

The hierarchy of conviction

  1. Macro view + technical setup + sentiment extreme = home run. This is when all the stars align. Example: oil at $120 with peak panic, macro view of demand destruction, and a technical break of support.

  2. Technical setup alone = workable. Lance will take a trade based purely on chart setup (e.g., a breakout above resistance) without a macro view. This is his bread and butter.

  3. Macro view alone = noise. Lance will not take a trade based solely on a macro thesis without a technical setup. He's seen too many macro calls that are right in direction but wrong in timing.

  • Historically, uninversion leads to a bull steepener (front end down faster than long end), then a bear steepener (long end up faster than front end).
  • His macro view: interest rates will be lower in a year.
  • But he doesn't trade this view alone. He waits for technical setups in bonds, the dollar, or other instruments that align with this view.

Lesson: Macro is useful for context and narrative, but it's not a trade signal. The chart is the ultimate reality.

Hedging, position sizing, and risk management

Lance is skeptical of hedging:

  • Hedges are imperfect and often lose on both sides.
  • Example: He shorts NVIDIA but buys NASDAQ as a hedge. If NVIDIA crashes but NASDAQ rallies on earnings, the hedge doesn't work and he loses on both.
  • Better approach: If you lack conviction, reduce size or close the position entirely.

Stops and risk management:

  • A stop loss is a form of risk management, not a hedge.
  • Lance always has a clear stop based on the chart (e.g., at the prior lows or highs).
  • This allows him to structure trades with good risk-reward ratios.

Adding to winners, not losers:

  • Never add to a losing position (fighting the trend).
  • The probabilities are against you when price is going down.
  • Wait for the trend to reverse (the "right side of the V"), then add at the same price or lower, but with better expected value because you're now with the trend.

Practical takeaways for the audience

  1. Have a macro view, but don't trade it alone. Combine it with sentiment and technicals.
  2. Look for distortions, not predictions. When narrative and sentiment reach extremes, that's when to act.
  3. Use simple tools: support/resistance, moving averages, trendlines. They work on any timeframe.
  4. Wait for the trend to change. Don't fight downtrends or uptrends. Wait for the break, then enter.
  5. Buy at panic, sell at euphoria. This is the opposite of what most people do, and it's why most people lose.
  6. Risk management first. Always have a clear stop. Only take trades where the risk-reward is favorable.
  7. Be patient. Most of the time, the market is in "no man's land"—neither euphoric nor panicked. That's when you sit on your hands. Wait for the extremes.
