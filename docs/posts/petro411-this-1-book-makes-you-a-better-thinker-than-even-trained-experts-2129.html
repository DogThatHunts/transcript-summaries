How a tragic miscarriage of justice exposes a common statistical error that distorts everything from courtroom verdicts and financial markets to our everyday decisions.

In this analysis, educator Petro411 draws on the book Math on Trial by mathematicians Lila Schneps and Coralie Colmez to explore how a fundamental misunderstanding of probability can lead to catastrophic real-world consequences, from wrongful convictions to global financial crises.

The tragic conviction of Sally Clark

In 1996 and 1998, Sally Clark lost her two infant sons, Christopher and Harry, to sudden crib deaths. The prosecution, suspicious of the coincidence, called pediatrician Sir Roy Meadow as their star witness. Meadow presented an infamous rule of thumb: "One cot death is a tragedy, two is suspicious, three is murder, unless proven otherwise."

To quantify this suspicion, Meadow used data from a government study showing the odds of a single cot death in an affluent, non-smoking family were 1 in 8,543. He multiplied this number by itself to calculate the probability of two deaths occurring naturally in the same family, arriving at a staggering 1 in 73 million. This simple, authoritative number overwhelmed the jury, leading to Clark's conviction in 1999.

The mathematical flaw: confusing independence with dependence

Petro411 explains that squaring a probability is only mathematically valid if the two events are statistically independent—meaning the occurrence of the first event tells you absolutely nothing about the likelihood of the second (like consecutive coin flips).

However, two infant deaths in the same family are not independent. The siblings share the same parents, genetics, home, and environmental factors. Once one cot death occurs, it serves as environmental or genetic evidence of a shared vulnerability, making a second death significantly more likely. By treating these dependent events as independent, Meadow manufactured a false statistic. When professional statisticians properly accounted for the shared risk factors, they found that two natural cot deaths in a single family were actually more likely than two murders.

The systemic fallout of bad statistics

The misuse of probability in the Clark case was not an isolated incident. After Clark's conviction was overturned in 2003, a UK government review of similar cases led to the release of several other wrongfully convicted mothers, including Angela Cannings, Donna Anthony, and Trupti Patel.

Though Sir Roy Meadow was temporarily struck off the medical register for serious professional misconduct, the human cost was severe. Clark never recovered from the trauma of her imprisonment and died in 2007 at the age of 42.

How the independence illusion affects everyday life

Petro411 argues that this cognitive error is incredibly common and manifests in various sectors: * The 2008 financial crash: Financial institutions modeled mortgage defaults as independent events, assuming a default in Florida had no bearing on one in Nevada. When systemic housing prices fell, defaults cascaded globally because they shared a common macroeconomic cause. * Data backups: Keeping multiple backups of files on the same laptop or cloud account creates a false sense of security. Because they share a single point of failure (the device or account), they are not independent safeguards. * Information echo chambers: Hearing a claim from three different people feels like independent confirmation. However, if all three individuals heard it from the same original source, it is merely a single claim echoed three times.

The single question to defeat the error

To avoid falling into the trap of multiplying probabilities of linked events, Petro411 advises asking one critical question: "Is there a common cause?" Recognizing the distinction between truly separate events and those secretly linked by a shared variable is essential for accurate critical thinking.
