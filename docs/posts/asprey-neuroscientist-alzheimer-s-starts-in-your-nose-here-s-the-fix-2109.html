Asprey | Neuroscientist: Alzheimer's Starts In Your Nose (Here’s the Fix)

Neurobiologist Dr. Michael Leon joins Dave Asprey to explain how olfactory deprivation accelerates cognitive decline and how overnight scent rotation can dramatically boost memory and sleep quality.

Executive summary

In this episode of The Human Upgrade, host Dave Asprey interviews Dr. Michael Leon, Professor Emeritus of Neurobiology and Behavior at UC Irvine, about the critical and often overlooked connection between the olfactory system and brain health. Dr. Leon explains that unlike other senses, smell has a direct neural 'superhighway' to the brain's memory and emotional centers. Modern clean environments, chronic congestion, aging, and viral infections like COVID-19 deprive the brain of olfactory stimulation, accelerating cognitive decline and increasing the risk of Alzheimer's disease. To combat this, Dr. Leon discusses the science of olfactory enrichment—specifically through rotating natural scents at night—which has been shown to dramatically improve memory, enhance deep sleep, alleviate depression, and even suppress PTSD-related nightmares.

Key insights

  1. The olfactory superhighway: Unlike vision or hearing, which must travel through the thalamus, the sense of smell has direct, unfiltered access to the brain's memory and emotional centers.
  2. Olfactory loss as a mortality predictor: Dr. Leon states that all-cause mortality in middle-aged adults can be reliably predicted by olfactory performance, with 157 medical disorders linked to both olfactory loss and systemic inflammation.
  3. The power of scent rotation: Exposing the brain to a high variety of rotating scents (such as 40 different natural essential oils) prevents sensory habituation and stimulates neural plasticity, resulting in up to a 226% memory improvement in clinical trials of healthy older adults.
  4. Overnight enrichment and sleep quality: Delivering low-concentration, pleasant scents during sleep ensures compliance and significantly increases total and deep sleep, facilitating the brain's glymphatic waste clearance.
  5. Mitigating PTSD and autism symptoms: Clinical observations and studies suggest that olfactory enrichment can suppress chronic nightmares in PTSD sufferers and dramatically improve behavioral and sleep outcomes for children on the autism spectrum.

The direct link between smell and brain health

Dr. Leon explains that the human brain evolved in an environment rich with diverse, natural odors. In the modern world, humans have sanitized their surroundings, leading to chronic olfactory deprivation. Because the olfactory system has a direct neural pathway to the amygdala and hippocampus—bypassing the thalamus 'side streets' used by other senses—this lack of stimulation causes the memory and emotional centers of the brain to deteriorate over time. Dr. Leon warns that any condition that chronically blocks this pathway, such as a stuffed nose from allergies or sinus infections, directly increases the risk of memory loss and cognitive decline.

Olfactory decline as a warning sign for chronic disease

According to Dr. Leon, olfactory performance is a powerful biomarker for overall health and longevity. By middle age, all-cause mortality can be predicted by an individual's ability to smell. Dr. Leon's research has identified 157 medical disorders—including anorexia, autism, depression, stroke, heart disease, cancer, and kidney disease—that are associated with both olfactory loss and increased systemic inflammation. Furthermore, the loss of smell associated with COVID-19 has been linked to physical damage along the olfactory pathway and subsequent memory loss, which Dr. Leon suggests is contributing to a rising surge of early-onset Alzheimer's cases.

The science of overnight olfactory enrichment

To counteract olfactory deprivation, Dr. Leon co-developed Memory Air (MemoryAire), a bedside device designed to automate 'olfactory enrichment.' The device diffuses 40 different natural essential oil scents twice a night while the user sleeps. Dr. Leon emphasizes that using 40 distinct scents is critical because the high variety provides constant cognitive novelty, preventing the brain from habituating to the stimuli. In clinical trials, this method achieved a 226% improvement in memory among healthy older adults across multiple standard cognitive tests. Additionally, because the stimulation occurs during sleep, it increases total sleep duration and deep sleep, which is when the brain clears metabolic waste.

Therapeutic applications for PTSD, autism, and depression

Beyond memory enhancement, olfactory enrichment has shown profound therapeutic benefits for mental health and neurodevelopmental conditions. Dr. Leon shares an anecdote of a retired Marine with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD nightmares who experienced complete relief after his first night of overnight scent stimulation. Dr. Leon also highlights MENDability, an online environmental enrichment program utilizing olfactory stimulation that has shown dramatic behavioral, sleep, and cognitive improvements in autistic children and adults. Furthermore, multiple studies indicate that olfactory enrichment can significantly relieve depressive symptoms, even in patients with advanced dementia.

Longevity biomarkers and the olfactory connection

Dave Asprey and Dr. Leon discuss the curious correlation between longevity interventions and olfactory health. Dr. Leon notes that out of 14 recognized longevity-promoting agents and practices—including exercise, calorie restriction, hormone replacement, rapamycin, spermidine, and oxytocin—every single one is associated with an improvement in the sense of smell. In contrast, other sensory systems do not share this broad correlation; for example, only oxytocin has been shown to improve hearing. This suggests that the olfactory system is uniquely integrated with the cellular and metabolic pathways that govern human lifespan.
