A simple amino acid found in space is essential for healing your body — and modern diets may be starving it.

What is Glycine?

Glycine is the simplest amino acid, found in both space and in human biology. It was detected on a comet and is present in the chemistry of icy dust grains in space through dark chemistry. In our bodies, glycine is a building block of proteins and plays a critical structural role.

It is the smallest and least bulky amino acid, and it is the only one small enough to fit into the tight center of collagen’s triple helix structure. Every third amino acid in collagen must be glycine. Removing glycine causes collagen to collapse.

The Glycine deficit: a hidden biological problem

Despite being labeled a non-essential amino acid — meaning the body can produce it internally — glycine production is insufficient to meet daily needs.

  • The body produces about 3 grams of glycine per day.
  • A human adult requires 10 to 15 grams daily to maintain basic functions like repairing collagen in joints, skin, and gut lining, synthesizing creatine for energy, and producing glutathione, a master antioxidant.
  • Collagen makes up about 30% of total body protein, and one in every three amino acids in collagen is glycine.

This results in a 10-gram daily deficit that must be covered by diet.

Biological triage: what happens when Glycine is low?

When glycine is insufficient, the body performs biological triage:

  • It prioritizes immediate survival by using available glycine to produce glutathione to fight oxidative stress and manage toxins.
  • Long-term structural integrity is deprioritized.

This means the body stops repairing: - Collagen in joints - The gut lining - Elastin in skin

Why modern diets drain Glycine

Historically, humans consumed animal parts like skin, cartilage, and bones — rich in glycine.

Today’s diets, however, focus on lean, boneless meats like chicken breast and steaks:

  • These are high in methionine.
  • The body uses glycine to process and clear excess methionine.

This creates a biological double whammy: missing dietary glycine and actively draining internal glycine stores.

Practical ways to bridge the Glycine gap

Dr. Ashley Frazee recommends two pathways:

Option 1: ancestral dietary fix

  • Eat structural animal parts like bones, skin, and connective tissue.
  • Bone broth made by slow simmering bones pulls glycine from bones and connective tissue.
  • Tough cuts of meat (e.g., chuck roast, ribs, shanks) contain high connective tissue that melts into gelatin during low-and-slow cooking — a source of glycine.
  • Eat animal skin (e.g., poultry, fish) and avoid shying away from gristle.

Option 2: supplements

  • Collagen peptides: About 33% glycine by weight. Dr. Frazee adds 10–15 grams to coffee daily.
  • Pure free-form glycine: A white powder that tastes sweet, like sugar. Can be used as a sugar substitute in tea or water.

  • Taking 3–5 grams of glycine before bed can reduce core body temperature.
