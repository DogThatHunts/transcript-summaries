The Lincoln marriage: the cost of constant criticism

Carnegie contrasts successful relationships with the tragic marriage of Abraham Lincoln and Mary Todd Lincoln. For over two decades, Mary Todd Lincoln constantly nagged, criticized, and humiliated her husband—even once throwing hot coffee in his face in front of guests.

Rather than motivating Lincoln to change, this persistent criticism drove him away. He became the only traveling attorney in Springfield, Illinois, who refused to return home on weekends, choosing instead to stay in uncomfortable rural taverns to avoid his wife's nagging. Carnegie argues that nagging is entirely counterproductive; it breeds deep resentment and forces the other person to withdraw.

The Disraeli marriage: admiration and non-interference

In contrast to the Lincolns, British Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli and his wife, Mary Ann, enjoyed a deeply happy marriage despite stark differences in age, intellect, and social standing. Mary Ann devoted her life to her husband's happiness, and Disraeli cherished her completely.

Carnegie quotes philosopher Henry James to highlight the core rule of relationship harmony: "The first thing to learn in intercourse with others is non-interference with their own peculiar ways of being happy, as long as they do not interfere with our own." A successful partnership relies on mutual admiration and letting go of the desire to control or change your partner's harmless habits.

The power of trivialities and courtesies

According to Carnegie, marriages are rarely destroyed by grand tragedies. Instead, love "goes away in little ways." Citing a family court judge who reviewed 40,000 marital disputes, Carnegie notes that trivialities—such as failing to wave goodbye in the morning or neglecting small tokens of appreciation—are the true root of most divorces.

Furthermore, Carnegie points out a bizarre human tendency: people regularly treat strangers with courtesy and respect while reserving their sharpest insults and rudeness for their families. True relationship health requires treating those closest to you with the same basic politeness you would afford a stranger.
