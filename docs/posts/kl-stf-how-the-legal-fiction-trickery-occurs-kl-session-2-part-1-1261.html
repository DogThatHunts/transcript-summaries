The historical split of American governance

KL explains his theory that two distinct governments have existed in America since the ratification of the Constitution in 1789. The first is the "republic," which KL describes as the civil government of the American nations, where citizens are considered "American nationals" under Article 4, Section 2 of the Constitution. The second is a "democracy," which KL characterizes as a municipal corporation established to manage the 19 enumerated powers that individual states could not handle alone, such as national defense.

To clarify this relationship, KL compares the federal government to a homeowner's association (HOA). In this analogy, homeowners contract with a management company to perform specific services, but the company has no inherent authority over the homeowners unless contractually granted. KL argues that the federal government, centered in the District of Columbia, is a foreign entity relative to the fifty geographic Union States and possesses no jurisdiction over private individuals unless they explicitly contract with it.

The 14th Amendment and the creation of corporate citizenship

According to KL, the post-Civil War era marked a significant shift in how citizenship and jurisdiction were defined. He points to the Act of June 30, 1864, and the subsequent passage of the 14th Amendment in 1868 as key mechanisms used to expand federal territorial jurisdiction. KL argues that the 14th Amendment created a new, secondary class of "U.S. citizenship" that is distinct from "Union State citizenship."

KL cites the Slaughterhouse Cases (1873) and U.S. v. Cruikshank (1875) to support his claim that these two citizenships carry entirely different legal rights. He notes that the Supreme Court ruled that the privileges and immunities of U.S. citizenship do not automatically incorporate the Bill of Rights. KL further asserts that the Act of 1871 formally incorporated the District of Columbia as a municipal corporation (which he refers to as "United States of America, Inc."), allowing it to establish corporate franchises in each state (e.g., the "State of Texas" or "State of Illinois") governed by Roman civil law rather than common law.
