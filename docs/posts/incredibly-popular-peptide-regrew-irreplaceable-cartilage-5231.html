Incredibly Popular Peptide Regrew 'Irreplaceable' Cartilage

A drug born from lizard venom may be reversing cartilage damage once thought irreparable.

The origin of a revolutionary drug

In the early 1990s, endocrinologist John Eng studied a vial of dried HeLa monster venom, sourced from a mail-order catalogue in Utah. His colleagues dismissed the idea that a desert lizard could inform human medicine. But Eng noticed a compound in the venom that was 53% identical to human GLP-1, a hormone that regulates insulin release.

He named it XenDIN-4. Unlike natural GLP-1, which breaks down in the body within two minutes, XenDIN-4 lasted for hours in mice, offering a potential treatment for type 2 diabetes.

Eng filed a patent in 1993, but the VA refused to support it. He paid for the patent himself. After three years of rejection, he presented a poster at a diabetes conference in San Francisco. A researcher named Andrew Young from Amelin Pharmaceuticals read it and licensed the patent.

In 2005, the FDA approved Bieta, the first GLP-1 medication derived from lizard venom. It required twice-daily injections and led to only about 5% weight loss. Still, it proved that targeting the GLP-1 receptor could be therapeutically effective.

Subsequent versions—like liraglutide, Victoza, and semaglutide (Ozempic)—became more potent and longer-lasting. Semaglutide, approved in 2017, was given once a week and led to weight loss of up to 15% in patients. In 2021, the FDA approved semaglutide specifically for obesity.

Beyond weight loss, studies revealed surprising benefits: reduced heart disease risk by 20% in people with obesity but no diabetes, and lower rates of substance abuse—18% less for alcohol, 20% for nicotine, and 25% for opioids.

UAB neuroscientist Andrew Hardaway explained that food no longer drives the same behavioral responses, suggesting the drug acts on the brain’s reward system.

Further, GLP-1 receptors were found in tissues beyond the gut and pancreas—such as the heart, kidneys, and brain—indicating broad physiological effects.

The Cartilage breakthrough

For 283 years, doctors have believed that damaged cartilage in joints is irreversible. Cartilage lacks blood supply and nerves, making nutrient delivery and tissue repair extremely difficult.

Traditional treatments like microfracture have shown limited success. But patients on semaglutide began reporting improved joint function—beyond what could be explained by weight loss.

The Step 9 trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2024, showed that semaglutide reduced pain scores by 42 points in 407 patients with obesity and knee arthritis, compared to 28 points in the placebo group. Patients also lost 13.7% of their body weight.

However, a team led by Dr. Dai Chen at the Shenzhen Institute of Advanced Technology questioned whether weight loss was the sole explanation.

They designed an experiment using mice with osteoarthritis. One group received semaglutide; another was pair-fed to lose the same amount of weight without the drug. The pair-fed mice showed worsening cartilage deterioration, while the semaglutide group showed preserved cartilage, reduced inflammation, and fewer bone spurs.

Dr. Dai Chen explained that semaglutide flips a cellular switch in chondrocytes—the cells that maintain cartilage—shifting them from an inefficient fuel source to a clean, efficient one.

The mechanism involves GLP-1 receptors activating the AMPK-PFKB3 signaling cascade, which shifts cells from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation, enabling energy production sufficient for tissue repair.

Human evidence and limitations

To test this in humans, Chen’s team conducted a pilot study with 20 patients aged 50 to 75, all with obesity and knee arthritis. Half received standard hyaluronic acid injections; the other half received both injections and weekly semaglutide.

After 24 weeks, MRI scans showed the semaglutide group had an average 17% increase in cartilage thickness—compared to less than 1% in the control group. Patients also reported reduced pain and improved joint function.

This result challenges the long-held belief that cartilage damage is irreplaceable. The tissue once thought to be unrepairable appears to be regenerating.

However, the study is limited: it involved only 20 patients over 24 weeks. The authors themselves caution that the protective effects of semaglutide on human knee joints should be interpreted with caution and require further validation.

The pair-fed mouse experiment is significant because it demonstrates a mechanism independent of weight loss—providing direct evidence of a biological effect beyond correlation.

Broader implications

GLP-1 receptors have been found in unexpected places, and the drug class continues to reveal effects beyond diabetes.

Osteoarthritis is no longer seen as just wear and tear. Exercise is now recognized as a helpful intervention.

This discovery opens the possibility of a new approach to osteoarthritis—one that may not only reduce pain but actually regenerate damaged cartilage.
