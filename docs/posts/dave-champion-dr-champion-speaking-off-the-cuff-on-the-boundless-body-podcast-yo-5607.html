Lymphatic versus hepatic lipid transport mechanics

Dave Champion details the precise physiological paths of fat transport in the human body under different metabolic states:

  1. The lymphatic lipid system: When dietary fat is consumed in ketosis, it emulsifies in the intestines and is packaged into chylomicrons using the structural protein APO B48. These chylomicrons enter the lymphatic system, pass through the thoracic duct, and enter the bloodstream. They receive APO C2 from HDL particles, which unlocks cargo doors allowing cells to offload fatty acids directly for clean fuel. Once emptied, the APO C2 returns to HDL, locking the cargo doors so the remnant particle can return to the liver for recycling.
  2. The hepatic lipid system: When carbohydrates are consumed, they break down into monosaccharides and travel via the portal vein directly to the liver. High blood glucose and high insulin levels are toxic to the vascular system. To lower blood sugar, the liver extracts glucose and converts it into synthesized triglycerides. It packages these into VLDL and LDL particles (using APO B100) and pushes them into circulation. Because cells running on glucose or already filled with fat reject these extra triglycerides, the LDL particles circulate continuously until they are forced to deposit the excess triglycerides into adipose tissue (fat cells). Elevated circulating LDL is often a symptom of this emergency clearance system being constantly overloaded by a high-carbohydrate diet.

An evolutionary perspective on disease and physical activity

Dave Champion asserts that hominids historically lived almost exclusively in ketosis, with significant carbohydrate intake being a recent anomaly accelerated by the second agricultural revolution. Historically, meat was the food of royalty, whereas grain was the staple of peasants. Today, Western dietary habits expose children to high-glycemic processed foods at much younger ages, shortening the typical 20-to-25-year timeline required for chronic insulin exposure to manifest as overt metabolic disease.

Regarding physical fitness, Dave Champion highlights that exercise should be understood through basic mechanics rather than modern aesthetic trends popularized by figures like Arnold Schwarzenegger or Suzanne Somers. Because the lymphatic system has no central pump, skeletal muscle contractions—such as walking, lifting, pulling, or squatting—are the sole mechanism for moving lymph fluid through lymph nodes and bone marrow. Regular movement combined with ketosis maintains optimal immune function and reduces susceptibility to systemic infections.

The insulin intolerance hypothesis for type 1 diabetes

Dave Champion outlines his theoretical framework for the onset of type 1 diabetes, which he includes as a bonus chapter in Body Science:

  • Autoimmune trigger: In individuals with a specific genetic predisposition, repetitive spikes in insulin are perceived by the body as an existential threat. To prevent catastrophic insulin toxicity, the immune system initiates an extreme autoimmune response that destroys the insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas.
  • The psychotic killer analogy: Dave Champion compares an insulin-sensitive genome to being locked in a room with a psychotic killer who is only triggered by loud music. Turning up the volume (raising insulin levels) repeatedly eventually triggers the killer to attack; once the attack occurs, turning the music down cannot reverse the destruction.
  • Prevention potential: Dave Champion hypothesizes that children with a family history of type 1 diabetes could potentially prevent the disease from manifesting by remaining strictly in ketosis from infancy, preventing insulin from ever spiking above baseline. While proving this hypothesis through a 40-year longitudinal study is economically unfeasible for pharmaceutical companies, Dave Champion suggests it offers a practical strategy for at-risk families.
