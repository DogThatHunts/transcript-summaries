Master Key | The Law of Success, Book 3 of 8 (1928) by Napoleon Hill - "Habit of Saving & Initiative Leadership"

Hill argues that saving is purely a habit, and that initiative, doing what ought to be done unasked, is the foundation of leadership.

Book 3 of 8 of Napoleon Hill's The Law of Success (1928), read by Dan John Miller for the Master Key Society. The ideas are Hill's, reported as he states them, not endorsed or verified here. Every timestamp opens the video a few seconds early.

Book 3 holds two lessons of the sixteen-lesson course: Lesson Four, The Habit of Saving, and Lesson Five, Initiative and Leadership. It ends with "an after-the-lesson visit with the author", an essay on intolerance, and a reading of the volume's full-page inspirational quotations.

Lesson Four rests on one idea: saving money "is solely a matter of habit". Hill sets out the law of habit and shows it working both ways. A limit on your earnings, once fixed in your mind, becomes a ceiling. A definite proportion of savings, written into your definite chief aim, makes savings grow with income. Most of the lesson is evidence: debt, budgets, an advertising man's confession, and fortunes Hill traces to a small sum saved.

Lesson Five follows the lesson on self-confidence, since, Hill says, no one takes the initiative without belief in himself. Initiative is doing what ought to be done without being told, and leadership is built on it. His three steps are to overcome procrastination by auto-suggestion, teach initiative to others, and lead through organized, cooperative effort. Two of his own stories close the lesson and lead into Lesson Six, Imagination.

Lesson 4: The Habit of Saving [00:41]

The law of habit [01:22]

Through repetition, Hill says, any act becomes a habit, and "the mind appears to be nothing more than a mass of motivating forces growing out of our daily habits". Public speakers who repeat a story often enough "forget whether the story is true or not".

Limitation built through habit [02:33]

Millions stay poor, Hill argues, because they have made "destructive use of the law of habit" and of "the law of attraction through which like attracts like". Fix in your mind a limit on what you can earn and your subconscious accepts it. Then fear of poverty, "one of the six basic fears", hedges you in until "opportunity will no longer knock at your door".

Playing both ends against the middle [04:05]

The remedy has two parts. First, through the law of definite chief aim, write an exact description of what you want, including the money you intend to earn. Keep it fixed in mind "in the manner described in Lesson 2" until a "prosperity consciousness" replaces the "poverty consciousness". Second [05:17], make the same written statement commit you to saving "a definite proportion of all the money you earn". Dwell on fear of poverty instead and your earnings shrink to bare necessities.

Whole communities work the same way: "crime waves are very largely the products of sensational journalism" [06:23], and one pessimist "can destroy the work of half a dozen competent men". An Illinois banker's office sign reads: "We talk and think only of abundance here" [08:14].

The slavery of debt [09:21]

"Debt is a merciless master, a fatal enemy of the savings habit." Poverty kills ambition. Add debt and its victims "are practically doomed to failure". Hill describes a friend whose wife "tries to make a $20,000 showing on a $12,000 income". Debt is "something like quicksand".

He separates two classes of debt [13:01]. Debts for luxuries are "a dead loss" and should be avoided. Trading debts, for goods or services that can be converted back into assets, are acceptable within reasonable limits. Going beyond those limits leads to speculation, which "swallows more of its victims than it enriches". Hill claims debt causes "more suicides every year than all other causes combined" [14:39], and that fear of poverty attracts fear of ill health and of old age. His example is a New York bank clerk who lived beyond his income, lost two jobs to his creditors, and jumped from a Broadway building [16:02].

How to master the fear of poverty [17:04]

Stop buying on credit and pay off existing debts gradually. Then adopt as part of your definite chief aim "the habit of saving a regular proportion of your income, even if this be no more than a penny a day". Stopping a habit is not enough: "The discontinuance of a habit leaves a hole in the mind" [18:17], and the saving habit must fill it. The course's formulas, such as the self-confidence formula of Lesson Three, take hold through the same law.

Why money matters [19:10]

Hill would be "greatly disappointed" if a student thought success is measured in dollars alone. Yet "the cold, cruel, relentless truth" is that a man without money is "so many grains of sand", judged by his bank balance and stepped on by the crowd. Edison and Ford, he says, would have stayed obscure or been swallowed by competitors without the habit of conserving resources [21:28]. Lack of reserve capital causes "more of the business failures than to all other causes combined" [22:22].

The arithmetic of small sums [23:27]

Hill cites a building-and-loan table of what $5 to $50 a month grows to in ten years. A New York banker's chauffeur on $20 a week itemised a week's spending [24:11]: cigarettes, gum, soda, cigars for friends, picture shows, shaves, papers and shoe shines came to $7.47, and he saved 53 cents. Saving $25 a month instead, Hill says, would have given him $5,000 by age 31. A small fund, he argues, is what lets you take an opportunity. Paste the table on your mirror, he says, and on every schoolroom wall.

His own account follows [27:26], the one that made him add saving to "the fifteen laws of success". He lists $4,000 inherited and lost in a friend's auto-supply business, $30,000 from training 3,000 salesmen sunk into a magazine with no reserve, and lecture and writing fees spent as they came. With $6,000 of unsaved salary, that makes $47,000, which at compound interest "would have grown into the sum of $94,000". He once spent a $500 speaking fee on a car down payment "before it had been in my hands thirty seconds" [29:35]. Spending thrills and saving does not only because spending is the habit we practise. Because saving demands self-denial, it also builds "self-control, self-confidence, courage, poise, and freedom from fear" [31:03].

How much should one save? [31:33]

For a salaried man: savings 20%, living 50%, education 10%, recreation 10%, life insurance 10%. The average man, Hill says, saves nothing and spends 60% on living, 35% on recreation and 5% on insurance. Recreation is the item to watch "as carefully as the greenhouse keeper watches the thermometer" [33:00]. Under Prohibition, Hill writes, tens of thousands earning $50 a week spend a third of it on "entertainment which comes in a bottle".

The Canton investigation [35:12]

Hill says he is "not a reformer", then reports data from 1926, when he was partnered with Don R. Mellett, publisher of the Canton Daily News. Mellett was fighting the city's underworld and was assassinated that July. Investigators' reports came to Hill's office, so the figures are "absolutely accurate" [36:10]. A $6,000-a-year official paid a bootlegger $300 a month. A policeman on $160 a month spent $400 on roadhouse dinners. In 41 high schools where Hill lectured [39:27], fewer than 2% of students showed any tendency to save, and only 5% of 11,000 thought saving essential to success.

Woolworth, parents, and the one rule [40:22]

F. W. Woolworth made over $100 million catching the nickels and dimes people throw away. His stores' red fronts are apt, "for red denotes danger". Parents ignorant of "the psychology of habit" train children to spend, and a man given an unexpected $100 has spent it in his head by nightfall [41:52]. Then comes the rule [42:48]. Whoever saves a definite proportion of all he earns is "practically sure" of financial independence. Whoever saves nothing "is absolutely sure never to be", whatever his income, barring a large inheritance.

"I have made a million dollars, but I haven't got a cent" [44:55]

Hill reprints, from The American Magazine, the confession of advertising man W. C. Freeman. After school-teaching and book-selling, Freeman talked his way onto Bridgeton's Morning Star at a rising daily wage that reached $50 a week in two months [52:21]. He rose through Philadelphia papers to earn four times his father's salary as a school superintendent, but "expenses increased with every increase in my income," and he was never out of debt again [58:13]. Horses, Pinehurst winters, golf trophies at $250 apiece and catered dinners followed [59:54], while he "borrowed money from Peter to pay Paul". Friends forced him into bankruptcy, and a Florida comeback died in the real-estate collapse [1:06:19]. He regrets not the 90% he spent but the 10% he failed to keep, which would now be $100,000 [47:04]. Thousands, Hill says, could tell the same story [1:09:22].

Budgets for ordinary incomes [1:09:43]

From over 16,000 families he says he has analysed, Hill sets budgets for a couple. On $100 a month they should save $10 to $12, on $150 save $25, on $200 save $50, and on $300 save $55 to $65. Almost any family can save 5% of gross income. "A man with a Ford income has no business purchasing a Studebaker car" [1:13:38]. Hill warns against the installment plan and keeping up with the Smiths [1:16:29]. Nothing is as humiliating as "poverty in old age" [1:15:40].

Savers get the opportunities [1:16:56]

Employers prefer savers for the character saving shows, and the employer who makes his staff save "is a practical philanthropist". Hill's cases [1:18:17]:

  • A Philadelphia printer saves $900 in three years, rescues his failing plant, and draws over $25,000 a year as half-owner.
  • Ford's early backers, among them James Couzens, the friends who staked Woolworth, Van Heusen's backers, and the El Producto cigar makers, who sold out to the American Tobacco Company for $8 million [1:19:23].
  • Rockefeller as a $40-a-month bookkeeper and James J. Hill as a $30-a-month telegrapher won backing because they had shown they could save [1:21:02].
  • Chicago printer George B. Williams got credit for having saved $15 a week out of $30. In 1918 he gave Hill credit of "upward of thirty thousand dollars" for the Golden Rule magazine, because Hill had "formed the savings habit", though his savings had been "lost in the war" [1:22:32].
  • J. P. Morgan, Hill reports, would rather lend a million to a saver of sound character than a thousand to a spendthrift [1:24:15].

"The greatest thing in life is freedom," Hill concludes, and there is none without some financial independence. A lifetime of compelled toil is like prison [1:27:08]. The lesson ends with Polonius: "Neither a borrower nor a lender be" [1:28:17].

Lesson 5: Initiative and Leadership [1:28:40]

Where the lesson sits [1:28:50]

The sixteen lessons "harmonize and blend" like links in a chain. This one follows self-confidence because no one leads without belief in himself. Initiative is "the very foundation" of leadership, "as essential to success as a hub is essential to a wagon wheel" [1:29:58]. It is "that exceedingly rare quality that prompts, nay, impels, a person to do that which ought to be done without being told to do it."

Elbert Hubbard's classes of men [1:30:16]

Hill quotes Elbert Hubbard. The world's big prizes go to those who do the right thing without being told. Next come those who do it when told once ("carry the message to Garcia"), then those who act only "when necessity kicks them from behind", and last those who will not act even when shown. "To which class do you belong?" Leadership "is something that you must invite yourself into", and the course "is not intended as a shortcut to success" [1:32:46].

Securing $25,000 without collateral [1:33:23]

In 1916 Hill needed $25,000 for a school of advertising and salesmanship and had no security. He made the sum his definite chief aim, spent six weeks on a plan, and took it to a business college that was struggling against competitors. The college would market his course on its own credit as a new department. Once the department's receipts matched the college's outlay, it would become his. Within a year it did [1:38:31]. The obvious method, a bank loan, was not the best, a point that leads to imagination.

First step: master procrastination [1:40:43]

Procrastination "is gnawing at the very vitals of your being", and the cure is autosuggestion. Hill's formula is to be posted where you see it night and morning [1:41:30]. Having chosen a definite chief aim, you will take some definite action toward it each day. You will (a) do one thing that ought to be done without being told, (b) find one new thing of value to others "without expectation of pay", and (c) tell one person about the habit. Like a muscle, initiative grows with use, starting with "the small commonplace things" of daily work.

"Those who work for money alone ... are always underpaid, no matter how much they receive" [1:43:59]. Hill's example is his stenographer [1:44:44]. After typing his motto, "your only limitation is the one that you set up in your own mind", she began answering his letters after hours in his style. When his secretary resigned she simply took the job, and she now earns four times her starting pay.

Second step: teach initiative to others [1:47:50]

"A man learns best that which he endeavors to teach others," just as a salesman must first sell himself. Any statement repeated to persuade others is believed by the speaker too, "whether the statement is false or true". Try an experiment [1:50:08]: keep selling initiative to an acquaintance who never does more than expected. Through autosuggestion, the change you will notice most is in yourself. Hawthorne's "The Great Stone Face" makes the same point, that we come to resemble what we admire.

Third step: the right brand of leadership [1:52:03]

One brand of leadership is forced on unwilling followers. Napoleon had every fundamental but one, "the spirit of helpfulness to others as an objective" [1:52:45]. The other brand, Lincoln's, leads to "self-determination and freedom and self-development and enlightenment and justice". Hill says there can be "no doubt" which the course recommends, then that it makes "no recommendations" on ethics and leaves the choice to the reader [1:55:01].

The Penalty of Leadership [1:55:51]

Hill reprints the essay: "he that is first must perpetually live in the white light of publicity." Whistler, Wagner, Fulton and Ford all drew envy, as did the Law of Success. "That which deserves to live, lives."

Organized effort and cooperation [1:59:24]

The third step also means organized effort. No one achieves lasting results without cooperation, and harmonious alliance multiplies each member's power. Lack of cooperation, Hill says, has destroyed "more business enterprises than have all other causes combined" [2:00:39], and lies behind strikes, high rents and the high cost of living. He faults rival churches, while insisting that without the churches "no man would be safe in walking down the street" [2:05:25]. He recommends Benjamin Kidd's The Science of Power, with reservations [2:06:05].

Major Bach's address [2:07:10]

Hill reprints Major C. A. Bach's address to student officers at Fort Sheridan during the World War. Commissions make officers, not leaders, and men led by discipline alone "obey the letter of their orders, but no more". Bach's qualities of leadership [2:11:56]:

  • Self-confidence, from exact knowledge and the ability to impart it. "There is no substitute for accurate knowledge."
  • Moral ascendancy [2:14:28]: self-control, physical endurance and moral force. "Your company will be the reflection of yourself."
  • Self-sacrifice [2:16:56]: the longest hours, sympathy for your men, even your own money.
  • Paternalism "in its better sense" [2:18:14]: the men's food, shelter and health before your own, which builds esprit.
  • Fairness [2:20:08]: punishment fitted to the man, credit given ("Everybody hates a human hog"), no abuse of rank.
  • Initiative and decision [2:22:16]: "Genius is merely the capacity for taking infinite pains," and "any reasonable order in an emergency is better than no order."
  • Dignity [2:24:55]: "Be the friend of your men, but do not become their intimate."
  • Courage [2:26:10]: stand by your orders, take the blame, judge without favour. "Courage is more than bravery."
  • Study men [2:31:10], as Robert E. Lee did. "Know your men, know your business, know yourself."

Decision, persistence and the other laws [2:32:41]

Returning to Lesson Two, Hill says a chief aim must be active: "You might as well have no definite chief aim as to have such an aim without continuous effort to achieve it." Quick, firm decision is "one of the major requisites for leadership" [2:34:09]. His analysis of 16,000 people, he says, found leaders decisive even in small matters and followers never so, for lack of self-confidence.

The LaSalle salesman and the real-estate man [2:36:05]

A LaSalle Extension University salesman met a shabby small-town real-estate man who said of the course, "Well, I hardly know whether I do or not." The salesman refused to sell to him and told him that indecision had beaten him. Then he apologised and offered to take him to St. Louis. The man wept and enrolled, and three years later he ran sixty salesmen there [2:42:41].

The Lumberport bridge [2:43:53]

About eighteen years earlier, muddy from a three-mile walk, Hill asked Lumberport, West Virginia, bank cashier V. L. Horner why the trolley line stopped short of town. The answer was a $100,000 bridge that the traction company would not pay for. Watching a freight train block the county road at the Baltimore and Ohio's switching yard [2:46:33], Hill saw three interested parties. The railroad, the Harrison County commissioners and the Monongahela Valley Traction Company each agreed to pay one third [2:48:35], and streetcars were running within months. The deal got him a job with the traction company's counsel and led to LaSalle. He says it used four of the fifteen laws, plus some "doing more than paid for" [2:49:56].

Bridge to imagination [2:53:10]

"Initiative is the moving force that pushes the leader ahead, but imagination is the guiding spirit that tells him which way to go" [2:53:23]. Organized effort is "directed according to a plan that was conceived with the aid of imagination, guided by a definite chief aim, and given momentum with initiative and self-confidence" [2:55:00].

After the lesson: Intolerance [2:55:37]

Hill's essay calls the bitterest intolerance religious, racial and economic. He hopes to find beyond death "no Jews or Gentiles, Catholics or Protestants" [2:56:54]. Beside a picture of two locked stags, he traces human combat to three forms of competition, "sex, economic, and religious" [2:57:53]. His examples are two feuding school owners, two foremen who bankrupted their plant, the Hatfields and McCoys, Philadelphia millionaires racing each other in fences and cars, and Carnegie and Frick's rival skyscrapers [3:02:21]. "It is not your duty to be tolerant, it is your privilege." Lock hands, he urges, not horns [3:05:39].

Claims worth checking

  • Hill states that debt "causes more suicides every year than all other causes combined" [14:39]. He also states that lack of reserve capital [22:22] and lack of cooperation [2:00:39] each cause more business failures than all other causes combined. He gives no source for any of the three.
  • Hill states that Woolworth's system "netted him over $100 million in a few years' time" [40:22], and that El Producto sold to the American Tobacco Company for $8 million [1:20:26].
  • Hill states that Rockefeller saved as a $40-a-month bookkeeper and that James J. Hill was a $30-a-month telegrapher when he conceived the Great Northern [1:21:02].
  • Hill states that he analysed 16,000 families [1:09:43] and 16,000 people [2:34:16], and surveyed 11,000 high-school students [39:46]. He describes no method.
  • Some figures do not add up as stated. Freeman's eighty trophies at $250 each come to $20,000, not $45,000 [1:00:50]. Saving $25 a month for ten years ($3,000 deposited) and ending with $5,000 implies a high rate of interest [25:51].
  • Hill presents "The Penalty of Leadership" without naming its author [1:55:51]. He states that the Hatfield–McCoy feud began over a pig and a dog between long-friendly families [2:59:34], and that Carnegie "lost his mind" after his feud with Frick [3:02:53].
