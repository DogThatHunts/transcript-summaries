In this episode, host NKL talks with economist and author Michael Pettis about the systemic roots of global trade imbalances, expanding on the framework from his book Class Wars Are Trade Wars. Pettis argues that trade deficits and surpluses are not driven by national thriftiness or cultural habits, but are instead the direct result of domestic income distribution policies. By repressing wages to boost competitiveness, surplus economies like China and Germany export their under-consumption, forcing deficit nations—primarily the United States and other Anglophone countries—to absorb excess capital, which drives up debt, inflates asset bubbles, and hollows out domestic manufacturing. As the US moves to reindustrialize and close its trade deficit, Pettis warns that Europe will face a brutal choice: either allow its own manufacturing sector to collapse under the weight of redirected global surpluses or implement protective trade and capital interventions.

  • The German Hartz reforms distorted the Eurozone: Rather than boosting productivity, Germany's 2003–2004 labor reforms suppressed wage growth, creating massive corporate savings that flowed into peripheral Europe and inflated property bubbles in countries like Spain and Ireland.

The systemic link between income inequality and trade imbalances

Pettis explains that trade imbalances are fundamentally driven by domestic income distribution rather than price effects. When income is well-distributed within a society, total consumption and investment can absorb everything produced. However, when income inequality is high—meaning ordinary workers retain a low share of what they produce—consumption lags, leading to excess capacity.

To prevent unemployment, these high-saving, low-consuming nations must export their excess production, resulting in persistent trade surpluses. Pettis notes that this is not a new theory, pointing to historical precedents from economists like John Maynard Keynes and Joan Robinson, who argued that persistent surpluses represent a "beggar-thy-neighbor" policy that exports domestic unemployment to trading partners.

How German wage repression destabilized the European periphery

Pettis presents the Eurozone as a clear example of income imbalances driving trade dynamics. In 2003 and 2004, Germany implemented the Hartz labor reforms, which successfully suppressed wage growth relative to productivity. This shift boosted business profits (which are saved) at the expense of household wages (which are consumed), causing German savings to soar.

Because of the shared currency, this excess German capital naturally flowed into peripheral European nations like Spain and Ireland. Since these advanced economies did not lack investment capital, the inflows did not boost productive capacity; instead, they lowered lending standards, fueled massive property bubbles, and drove up household debt. When the debt capacity of the periphery was reached in 2009, the adjustment was forced through high unemployment.

The exorbitant burden of the US dollar

While conventional economic models suggest high US income inequality should lead to high national savings, the US consistently runs massive trade deficits. Pettis explains this paradox by pointing to the unique role of the US dollar. Because the US has deep, liquid, and legally secure financial markets, it acts as the primary sink for global excess savings and capital flight.

This foreign capital inflow is not driven by US investment needs; rather, it is an exogenous force. Because the capital account must balance the current account, a $100 capital inflow mechanically forces a $100 trade deficit. This inflow drives up the dollar, making US manufacturing uncompetitive, and forces US national savings down through either rising unemployment, fed-induced household debt, or expanding fiscal deficits.

China's structural trap and the limits of debt capacity

China's investment-led growth model was highly successful when the country was deeply underinvested in the 1980s and 1990s. By suppressing consumption and wages, China forced up domestic savings to fund massive infrastructure and manufacturing projects. However, Pettis argues that China closed its investment gap by the late 2000s.

Because the Chinese government cannot easily transfer wealth back to households without slowing GDP growth and reducing its share of global manufacturing, it remains trapped in the old model. To maintain growth, China rotates through speculative investment bubbles—moving from property to manufacturing, and now back to infrastructure—all funded by a debt burden that is officially 315% of GDP and accelerating.

The coming European dilemma in a post-us deficit world

Pettis warns that a bipartisan consensus has emerged in the US to reindustrialize and reduce the trade deficit. Because the US, UK, and Canada represent roughly 70% to 80% of global trade deficits, any reduction in the US deficit will trigger a global arithmetic crisis.

If the US stops absorbing global surpluses, manufacturing powerhouse nations like China, South Korea, and Germany must either reduce their surpluses—which would cause severe domestic economic pain—or find a new deficit sink. Pettis argues that Europe is the only economy large enough to absorb these redirected surpluses. If Europe does nothing, its manufacturing sector will collapse; to prevent this, European policymakers will be forced to intervene in their external accounts using tariffs or capital controls.
