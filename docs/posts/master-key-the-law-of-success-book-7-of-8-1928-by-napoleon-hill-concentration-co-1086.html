The power of concentration

Napoleon Hill defines concentration as the act of focusing the mind upon a given desire until ways and means for its realization have been worked out and successfully put into operation. This practice relies on two primary psychological laws: the law of autosuggestion and the law of habit.

The mechanics of habit and environment

Habit grows out of repetition and environment. Hill compares a well-formed habit to a groove on a phonograph record, which the mind automatically follows. To eliminate undesirable habits, Hill states that one must actively build new, counteracting mental paths. He outlines five rules for building positive habits: 1. Enthusiasm: Start the new habit with force and deep feeling. 2. Concentration: Keep attention firmly fixed on the new path and away from the old. 3. Repetition: Travel the new path as frequently as possible. 4. Resistance: Resist the temptation to fall back into older, easier paths. 5. Definiteness: Ensure the new path aligns directly with a definite chief aim.

Environment serves as the "mental feeding ground" from which the mind extracts thought material. Hill emphasizes that daily associates are the most influential part of this environment, advising individuals to surround themselves with people who inspire enthusiasm, self-confidence, and ambition.

The mastermind alliance and mental energy

When two or more people ally themselves in a spirit of perfect harmony for a definite purpose, they unlock a seemingly superhuman power. Hill compares this blending of minds to chemical compounding, where combining distinct elements (like hydrogen and oxygen) creates an entirely new substance (water).

Hill attributes the achievements of industrial leaders like Henry Ford and Thomas Edison to the harmonious cooperation and concentration supported by their wives. Mrs. Edison personally observed that Edison's greatest asset was his capacity for unbroken concentration. Hill also references the methods of psychologist Dr. Elmer Gates, who sat in quiet, passive concentration to receive "thought waves" from the ether, suggesting that organized thoughts remain in existence as waves that a properly attuned mind can detect.

Memory training and overcoming a wandering mind

An accurate memory relies on three principles: 1. Retention: Vividly recording a sense impression by concentrating on it. 2. Recall: Reviving recorded impressions from the subconscious mind. 3. Recognition: Identifying a recalled impression and associating it with its original source.

Hill recommends using the Law of Association to improve memory, such as linking a new name to a familiar object, location, or historical figure. He shares a case study of a department manager who overcame a wandering mind and saved his career by establishing a strict, repetitive daily schedule and systematically practicing focused attention.

Persuasion, crowd psychology, and the dynamics of suggestion

Hill argues that physical force cannot control the human mind; enduring success requires persuasion, suggestion, and cooperation. To illustrate this, he highlights the case of Alvin York, a Tennessee mountaineer and conscientious objector who was persuaded to fight in World War I after officers framed the war as a holy crusade against "the beast" (the Kaiser), aligning the cause with York's deep religious beliefs.

Hill also explores crowd psychology and religious revivals, drawing on the work of Gustave Le Bon, William James, and F.M. Davenport. He notes that a crowd's individual minds fuse into a highly suggestible, emotional "composite mind" with lowered critical reasoning, making them susceptible to hypnotic-like suggestion.

The principle of cooperation

Cooperation is the beginning of all organized effort and the foundation of leadership. Hill divides cooperation into two forms: the alliance between individuals (the mastermind) and the coordination between the conscious and subconscious minds.

The dual nervous system and infinite intelligence

Hill presents a physiological hypothesis explaining how the mind communicates with "infinite intelligence." The human body features a dual nervous system: * The cerebrospinal system: The voluntary system centered in the brain, serving as the organ of the conscious mind. * The sympathetic system: The involuntary system centered in the solar plexus, serving as the organ of the subconscious mind.

These systems are physically connected by the vagus nerve. By consciously focusing on a desire, an individual transmits a vibratory current through the voluntary system to the solar plexus, handing the idea to the subconscious mind. The subconscious processes the idea and returns a definite, workable plan to the conscious mind.

Developing group power and specialized alliances

Power is defined as organized effort, which requires concentration, cooperation, and coordination. Hill argues that outstanding success requires an organization composed of individuals who supply specialized talents that others lack.

A successful business, for example, must coordinate buyers, salesmen, and financial experts. Hill points to James J. Hill and Andrew Carnegie as master coordinators who surrounded themselves with specialists to build their empires, creating a collective "mastermind" focused on a single purpose.

The psychology of action versus procrastination

Procrastination is a primary cause of failure. Hill asserts that the world pays individuals for what they do, or what they can get others to do, rather than merely what they know. He shares anecdotes of unemployed men in Chicago who blamed the world for not giving them a "chance," arguing instead that opportunities must be actively created through disciplined action.

Hill's personal health philosophy and the worry habit

Hill claims that physical inaction leads to "auto-intoxication" (self-poisoning of the intestines), which he asserts is the root cause of headaches, laziness, and a lack of mental alertness. He outlines his personal health regimen, which he claims keeps him highly energetic: * Drinking hot water every morning and eating a simple, vegetable-heavy diet. * Walking an average of 10 miles daily for exercise and meditation. * Performing a specific relaxation exercise by lying flat across a straight-backed chair. * Taking lukewarm salt-water enemas every ten days to cleanse the intestines. Hill warns against warm water enemas, claiming they relax the muscles and create a dependency. * Avoiding aspirin, which he argues merely deadens the nerve line of communication to the brain without removing the underlying poison.

Hill also warns that worry, envy, jealousy, hatred, doubt, and fear are toxic states of mind that destroy digestion and eliminate the desire to achieve. He recommends a hearty laugh or singing as a "gloom chaser" to stimulate positive action.

The fifteen forces of the standing army

Hill concludes with an overview of the 15 forces required for organized effort: a definite chief aim, self-confidence, the habit of saving, imagination, initiative and leadership, enthusiasm, self-control, doing more than paid for, a pleasing personality, accurate thought, concentration, cooperation, profiting by failure, tolerance, and the golden rule.
