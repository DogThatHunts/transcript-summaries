Master Key | The Law of Success, Book 7 of 8 (1928) by Napoleon Hill - "Concentration & Co-operation"

Hill's lessons on concentration and cooperation: habit, environment and the "magic key" of focused desire, joined in harmony with other minds to form the Master Mind.

Book 7 of 8 of Napoleon Hill's The Law of Success (1928), read by Dan John Miller for the Master Key Society. The ideas are Hill's, reported as he states them, not endorsed or verified here. Every timestamp opens the video a few seconds early.

Book 7 holds two lessons of the sixteen: Lesson 12, Concentration, and Lesson 13, Cooperation. Hill opens by calling concentration a "keystone" lesson, because the psychological law behind it bears on every other lesson of the course [00:49]. Concentration is where the earlier machinery (the definite chief aim, auto-suggestion, habit, imagination) is focused on a single object. Cooperation is where that focused power is multiplied through other people.

The argument runs in one line. Habit and auto-suggestion fix a desire in the mind, and environment supplies the material for thought. Concentration, "the magic key", holds the desire until a plan appears and is carried out. When two or more minds join in perfect harmony on the same end, the result is the Master Mind, which Hill defines as "group concentration of mind-power." Lesson 13 widens this in two directions. Inward, it is cooperation between the conscious and subconscious minds through the nervous system, Hill's hypothesis for contact with "infinite intelligence." Outward, it is cooperation between people in businesses, professions and nations. A long middle section on action, procrastination and health argues that knowledge pays only when it is expressed in service.

Lesson 12 also carries a long quoted section on crowd psychology and religious revivals, offered as an example of concentration used to fuse a crowd into one mind. The book ends with the essay "Your Standing Army", which lines up the course's fifteen qualities as soldiers, followed by Hill's inspirational quotations.

Lesson 12: Concentration [00:41]

Definition, habit and auto-suggestion [01:07]

Concentration is "the act of focusing the mind upon a given desire until ways and means for its realization have been worked out and successfully put into operation." Two laws enter into it: auto-suggestion, covered in an earlier lesson, and habit [01:18]. Habit grows from environment and repetition and, once formed, "resembles a cement block that is hardened in the mold." It is the basis of memory training [01:36]. It stores thought in the subconscious, where it shapes prejudices and opinions, and the mind follows it like a phonograph needle in its groove [03:10].

Habit can be a tyrant, but Hill says it can be harnessed like any other natural force [04:19]. A habit is "a mental path" that deepens with each passing, like folded paper or a riverbed [05:37]. The only reliable way to remove an old habit is to build a new one in its place, so the old path fills up from disuse [07:21]. Hill sums up the three terms in one image: auto-suggestion is the tool that digs the path, concentration is the hand that holds it, and habit is the map [10:40].

Environment [11:16]

Environment means the books we read, our associates, community, work, nation, clothes and songs, and "most important of all, the religious and intellectual training we receive prior to the age of 14 years" [11:22]. "If your environment is not to your liking, change it." Start with a clear mental picture of the environment you need, as Lesson 2 teaches for every desire [12:25]. Daily associates matter most, so choose people who are in sympathy with your chief aim [13:16]. Hill illustrates environment at the scale of civilization with a passage on Native Americans [14:30] and points ahead to Lesson 15's "social heredity" [15:28]. He adds that clothes affect self-confidence and that "a well-organized desk indicates a well-organized brain" [15:59]. A Chicago laundryman who put a player piano in his workroom earns more and pays better wages than his competitors [16:37].

The magic key [17:14]

Hill says the "magic key to success" is no discovery of his. It is the key used by New Thought and other schools of positive optimism [17:33]. It unlocks riches, fame, health and education. Carnegie, Rockefeller, Hill, Harriman and Morgan used it, and it can "unlock prison doors." The key is one word: concentration [18:33].

He disclaims occultism, though "all the scientists of the world have failed to explain the strange phenomena produced through the aid of concentration" [19:10]. Concentration means keeping the mind on one subject until it is mastered, controlling attention, and breaking and building habits. It is "complete self-mastery" [19:28]. Ambition and desire drive it. Most people never use the key because they "desire nothing in particular" [20:26].

The formula begins with putting "your foot on the neck of skepticism and doubt" [21:24]. Hill uses public speaking as the example. The student writes and signs a pledge to concentrate on the desire for ten minutes on rising and on retiring [21:56]. He then pictures himself one to ten years ahead with the income, home, savings and influence the skill brings [22:54]. Use the key only for worthy ends, and "quit living in the past" [24:11].

Mind chemistry and the Master Mind [24:56]

A deeply seated desire held with faith makes one "a lodestone" that attracts the desire's material counterparts [25:39]. Hill then states what he calls perhaps the most important principle of the course. When two or more people ally themselves in perfect harmony for a definite end, and the alliance is faithfully kept, it brings each of them "power that is superhuman and seemingly irresistible in nature" [26:03].

He argues by analogy. Water is neither hydrogen nor oxygen [26:47]. Matter is made of electrons, which are electricity, and thought is "the highest form of energy known," possibly the same energy a dynamo makes [27:40]. So minds may combine into "a sort of composite mind" [28:47]. His evidence is that some people's presence inspires us and others' depresses us. He grants that he has "no scientific basis for this hypothesis" [29:56]. Harmony is the essential term: one atom of hydrogen with one of oxygen makes no water, and an alliance "in name only" makes no power [31:32].

The power behind great men [31:49]

A Letcher County, Kentucky, family stood still for six generations until one son married an educated Virginia woman. Their children became a college president, a lawyer and a physician, and he became a dental surgeon [31:55]. Hill says biographies show the real power is often "a patient little wife" behind the scenes [33:49]. His examples are Henry Ford's wife [34:21] and Mrs. Edison, who told him that concentration was her husband's greatest asset [35:55]. He adds that Edison needed only three or four hours of sleep, and cites Lesson 7 on enthusiasm [36:30]. An apple seed draws a tree from soil and air by a law no one can explain. In the same way, a shared desire draws its material counterpart from blended minds [36:41].

Thought waves and the ether [38:49]

Concentrate on a subject, Hill says, and related facts "pour in from every conceivable source." Dr. Elmer Gates of Washington, D.C., having exhausted the recorded facts on a subject, "sits for further information" with a tablet and writes down thoughts that come "from he knows not where" [39:43]. Hill explains this through radio. Thought sends waves through the ether that only an attuned mind picks up [40:30]. Every organized thought ever released may still circle in the ether, so an attuned mind might reach "all knowledge that has been accumulated through the organized thoughts of the past" [41:31]. With this in mind, he sends the reader back to Carnegie's Master Mind in Lesson 2 [42:28].

Ford shows concentrated effort. He kept one standardized car until 1927 [43:29] and ended a debate over a new rear axle with "there is going to be no more change in that axle" [44:26]. Edward Bok's A Man from Maine shows Cyrus H. K. Curtis sinking a fortune into the losing Saturday Evening Post until it became one of the world's most profitable magazines [45:01].

Memory [46:13]

Memory has three principles. Retention is recording an impression, like a camera plate. Recall is retrieving it, like a card index. Recognition is identifying it as the original, which separates memory from imagination [46:38]. The method is to make the impression vivid by repetition, to associate it with something familiar, and to repeat it with concentration [47:50]. Forgotten names were never properly recorded, so repeat a new name four or five times [49:20]. Hill's images include a letter as big as a door hanging over a mailbox [49:55], Elizabeth Shearer filed with ten-foot scissors and Queen Elizabeth [50:15], and a telephone number filed with Lake Michigan and the ages of his brother and father [51:19]. A department manager tells how he cured his "wandering mind" with a written daily schedule kept at fixed times, until "I found repetition becoming comfortable" [52:12].

Control of attention: the climax [56:47]

"Concentration itself is nothing but a matter of control of the attention" [55:48]. Hill saves for last what he calls the most important part of the lesson. "Crystal-gazing is nothing but concentrated attention" [57:16]. He believes concentrated attention can attune the mind to the ether so that one may "tune in and understand that which is in the mind of any person." It may even reach "the universal mind in which all knowledge is stored" [57:49]. This may sound irrational to the orthodox, but to the serious student it seems "absolutely probable" [58:28]. The reader may cultivate the ability or say "BOSH!" and "thereby mark yourself a fool" [1:00:36]. Hill declares that mankind is entering "the Mind-Power Age" [1:01:28]. The Master Mind exists to apply the law of concentration more effectively than one person can [1:01:59].

Persuasion vs. force [1:02:39]

"Force alone never has built and never can build enduring success." The World War discredited German "might makes right" [1:03:17]. No one can control a normal mind that chooses to control itself [1:03:55]. Genius appears when something pushes a mind past its "average stopping point." The stimulants Hill means are mental, such as interest, desire, enthusiasm and love, not drugs [1:04:40]. The mind is a field that grows whatever seed is sown [1:06:01].

His case is Alvin C. York, told through a newspaper article. York was a Tennessee gunman who converted at a revival [1:09:26] and refused the draft as a conscientious objector [1:10:11]. Draft officers persuaded him from the Bible that the war was a holy cause against the Kaiser [1:10:36], and he became the war's foremost individual hero [1:12:00]. Hill draws the moral that many people classed as criminals could be redirected "through suggestion" in the same way [1:13:23]. Anything that arouses anger, hatred or fear is "destructive and very bad for you" [1:13:53].

Crowd psychology and the revival [1:14:32]

To show how minds in a crowd blend, Hill quotes at length an account he credits to J. A. Fiske [1:14:44]. It holds that revival phenomena are "psychical rather than spiritual," akin to hypnotic suggestion [1:15:04]. It cites David Starr Jordan and William James comparing revivalism to intoxication [1:16:05]. On crowd psychology it draws on Gustave Le Bon's The Crowd ("In crowds it is stupidity and not mother wit that is accumulated") [1:22:55] and on Frederick Davenport's Primitive Traits in Religious Revivals [1:26:10]. The revivalist's methods are matched step by step with the hypnotist's, leading to the line "Every good revivalist would make a good hypnotic operator" [1:31:48]. The account ends with Davenport's hope for "intelligent, undemonstrative, and self-sacrificing piety" [1:46:12]. Hill says these authorities show concentration being used to fuse a crowd into a single mind, and that the reader is now ready for cooperation [1:46:57].

Lesson 13: Cooperation [1:47:32]

Two forms of cooperation [1:47:40]

"Cooperation is the beginning of all organized effort." Carnegie built his fortune with not more than a score of men (Lesson 2) [1:47:47]. The lesson covers cooperation between people under the law of the Master Mind, and cooperation between the conscious and subconscious minds, a "reasonable hypothesis" for drawing on infinite intelligence [1:47:59].

The dual nervous system [1:48:47]

The sympathetic system, centred on the solar plexus or "abdominal brain," is the organ of the subconscious. The cerebrospinal system is the organ of the conscious mind, and the vagus nerve links them [1:49:39]. Intuitive ideas enter at the top of the brain and travel down to the solar plexus. They come back up to the corpus callosum "in a definite and workable form" by "the silent alchemy of the subjective mind" [1:52:30]. Auto-suggestion works through this system, which Hill calls "a direct line of communication between your ordinary conscious mind and infinite intelligence" [1:54:23]. His demonstration: resolve before sleep to wake at 4 a.m. and you will. Then ask why you do not impress the subconscious with larger desires [1:54:49].

How power is developed through cooperation [1:55:46]

This is "an age of cooperative effort." Hill cites professional associations, unions, the League of Nations and World Court [1:56:23], Ford's dealer network [1:58:11], and the Federal Reserve, which "practically ensures the United States against a money panic" [1:58:53]. Power is organized effort, built from "concentration, cooperation, and coordination" [1:59:39]. Personal power comes from mastering the fifteen principles, and group power from alliances [1:59:59]. An organization needs complementary talents. A law firm needs case-preparers and trial lawyers [2:01:21], and a business needs buyers, salesmen and finance men, who balance each other [2:03:43]. James J. Hill was no engineer but hired engineers [2:04:41]. The United States is itself a cooperation of states. Here Hill says Lincoln cared more for saving the Union than for "the freedom of the slaves of the South" [2:06:02].

Cooperation versus competition [2:08:55]

The Norwegian maelstrom stands for the fate of those who sail "the frail bark of independence." "Success in life cannot be attained except through peaceful harmonious cooperative effort, nor can success be attained single-handed or independently" [2:10:14]. Fortunes won by cooperation "inflict no scars upon the hearts of their owners" [2:11:04].

Action [2:12:18]

"The world pays for but one kind of knowledge," the kind expressed in constructive service. Of thirteen poor business-college boys, Hill says, eleven became successes, one died, and the thirteenth "is compiling this reading course on the Law of Success" [2:13:35]. Procrastinators live by "I am doing all I am paid to do, and I am getting by" [2:16:41]. Idle men in Chicago's parks told Hill, "The world will not give me a chance." One of them held two university degrees and boasted of a first family of Virginia [2:16:48]. A Maine city of blue laws is "dying with the dry rot" of inaction [2:22:21]. Frank A. Vanderlip, given no work at the National City Bank, proposed a bond department and the bank's first advertising, and rose to its presidency [2:24:47]. Hill holds up the "dreamer-doer" as the type who carries dreams into action [2:27:57].

The body and Hill's regimen [2:28:11]

Laziness is "the influence of an inactive mind on the cells of the body" [2:28:38]. City life breeds "auto-intoxication," and most headaches, Hill says, can be cured within an hour by an enema [2:29:45]. His regimen is hot water on rising, whole wheat, vegetables, walks averaging ten miles a day, rest lying back across a chair, an enema every ten days, and hot then cold showers [2:30:42]. He gave up aspirin, which he likens to cutting a fire-alarm wire, and halved his food [2:33:42]. He has taken no drug in five years [2:35:39]. He concedes he may be called "a long-haired crank" and asks only for a trial [2:36:20].

Worry, laughter and thought [2:37:49]

Worry, envy, hatred, doubt and fear are "fatal to action" and destroy desire [2:37:49]. Hill's "gloom chaser" is a hearty laugh, forced if necessary [2:38:51]. "As a man thinketh in his heart, so is he." A fear thought travels to the leg muscles [2:40:15]. Thoughts reach other minds through telepathy (Lesson 1) and, "a million times more important," the cells of one's own body [2:41:13].

Dynamos and balance wheels [2:41:49]

Men of action are of two types: the promoter-salesman (the "dynamo") and the caretaker (the "balance wheel") [2:42:16]. The world pays for "what you do or what you can get others to do" [2:43:17]. Find your type and choose a chief aim that fits it [2:45:57]. Embezzlers are often promoters confined to bookkeeping [2:47:11]. Singing stimulates action too. Hill cites Billy Sunday [2:50:15], says the German army won while it sang [2:50:33], reports a 45 percent wartime production rise from singing groups [2:51:38], and describes Filene's store in Boston, where staff dance before opening [2:52:22].

Harmony, motive and the Master Mind [2:54:05]

A football team or a board can succeed without harmony. "Plain cooperative effort produces power," but harmony of purpose "develops superpower" [2:55:06]. The signers of the Declaration of Independence formed one of the most powerful Master Minds [2:55:55]. Leaders need a motive that followers will embrace, as political slogans show [2:56:52]. Men work harder for an ideal than for money [2:57:52]. The three major motives are self-preservation, sexual contact, and financial and social power [2:59:52]. Perfect harmony requires a motive strong enough for each member to forget his own interests [3:01:13]. The lesson closes by previewing Lesson 14, on failure, the author's favorite [3:03:00].

Your Standing Army [3:03:40]

The after-lesson essay presents the fifteen qualities as soldiers, from definite chief aim to the golden rule [3:03:40]. Definite purpose is the most important officer, and only "2 out of every 100" have one [3:05:27]. Examples include Columbus, Lincoln ("to free the black slaves of the South"), Roosevelt, Ford, Burbank, Edwin C. Barnes, James J. Hill, Woolworth, Curtis and Martin W. Littleton [3:06:17]. "Nothing is impossible to the man with a definite purpose" [3:07:35]. The first step must be taken without outside aid [3:12:45]. Prayer "always works when backed with unqualified faith" [3:14:38].

Exercises and self-analysis

  • Five rules for a new habit [08:07]: put enthusiasm into the first expression; keep attention on the new path; travel it often; resist the old paths; make sure the path leads to your chief aim, then do not doubt.
  • Choose your environment [12:25]: picture the setting your aim needs, and pick associates and books that support it.
  • The magic key pledge [21:56]: write and sign a statement of the desire and concentrate on it for ten minutes at night and in the morning, picturing yourself years ahead in possession of it.
  • Memory drills [47:50]: repeat, associate and exaggerate. Repeat a name four or five times after confirming it.
  • Line-of-print test [55:54]: read a line, close your eyes and see it; memorize poetry the same way.
  • Chief-aim sitting [59:00]: memorize your chief aim. Twice a day, in a quiet place, relax, close your eyes, put your fingers in your ears, repeat the aim and see yourself possessing its object.
  • Waking test [1:54:49]: resolve to wake at a set hour, then apply the same method to larger desires.
  • Against procrastination [2:48:34]: do the most distasteful task first. Post "Do not tell them what you can do, show them." Repeat aloud twelve times each night: "Tomorrow I will do everything that should be done, when it should be done, and as it should be done."
  • Self-analysis [2:45:57]: decide whether you are a dynamo or a balance wheel, and use the introductory lesson's chart before fixing your aim.
  • Health and mood trials [2:36:38]: an enema for the next headache, the forced-laugh "gloom chaser" [2:38:51], and a church song service before work [2:51:20].
  • Inventory the fifteen soldiers [3:11:50]: study one quality at a time and develop the missing ones.
