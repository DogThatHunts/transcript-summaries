600mg Every Morning is The Cheapest Anti-Aging Trick

A look at N-acetylcysteine, a compound once pulled from shelves for being a drug, and its potential benefits and limitations for aging and health.

What is N-acetylcysteine?

N-acetylcysteine, or NAC, is a form of the amino acid cysteine. Your body uses cysteine as the rate-limiting ingredient to produce glutathione, one of its main antioxidants. Think of glutathione as your cell's rust guard—protecting cells from damage by neutralizing free radicals and supporting liver and immune function.

As you age, glutathione levels decline, leading to increased oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and insulin resistance. While glutathione is a natural antioxidant, research on it is limited. NAC, in contrast, has decades of clinical trials behind it and is an approved drug for specific medical conditions.

The strongest evidence for NAC's effectiveness comes from emergency room use: it is the standard antidote for acetaminophen (Tylenol) overdose. In such cases, an overdose depletes the liver's glutathione, and NAC prevents liver injury by restoring glutathione levels.

NAC also shows promise in the lungs, with a growing body of research indicating potential benefits for heart health, blood pressure, blood sugar, and healthy aging.

Key research findings

  • A 2023 trial at Baylor College of Medicine studied 24 older adults given either a glycine plus NAC supplement or a placebo for 16 weeks. Results showed:
  • Glutathione levels rose by 164%.
  • Inflammation markers dropped: IL-6 by 78%, C-reactive protein by 41%.
  • Insulin resistance fell by 64%.
  • Waist size and systolic blood pressure decreased.
  • Walking speed, grip strength, and chair standability improved.
  • Improvements approached levels seen in young adults.

Note: The trial was small—only 24 participants, with only 12 receiving the glycine plus NAC formulation.

  • A study in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition reanalyzed two double-blind trials in 82 middle-aged men:
  • One group took 1.8 grams of NAC daily (three 600mg tablets) for four weeks.
  • Homocysteine levels dropped by 12% (vs. 4.1% in placebo).

  • Systolic blood pressure also decreased.

  • A study in the Journal of Internal Medicine found that 12 people with high lipoprotein(a) reduced their levels by 7% after six weeks of 1.2 grams of NAC daily. The authors called this change not clinically significant.

  • A small study in the Journal of Diabetes gave 13 adults with type 2 diabetes 1,200mg and then 2,400mg of NAC daily for two weeks. Glucose tolerance and insulin response did not improve.

  • A systematic review in Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews found promising but inconsistent evidence for NAC and memory improvement. Combinations of antioxidants showed benefit, but NAC's individual contribution remains unclear.

What the evidence shows

  • Strong evidence: NAC is the standard antidote for Tylenol overdose.
  • Promising but early evidence: Benefits for aging and blood pressure, particularly when combined with glycine.
  • Limited or inconclusive evidence: Effects on blood sugar, memory, and lipoprotein(a) levels.

Side Effects and considerations

  • Common side effects: nausea, vomiting, and upset stomach (up to 23% of users).
  • NAC has a strong sulfur-based smell, similar to rotten eggs.
  • Taking it with food can help reduce gastrointestinal discomfort.
  • NAC can strengthen the effects of nitroglycerin and may interact with blood pressure medications.

The Amazon shelves story

  • In 2021, Amazon removed NAC from its shelves because the FDA classified it as a drug, not a supplement, since it was approved as a drug in 1963.
  • In 2022, NAC returned to Amazon shelves due to the FDA exercising enforcement discretion while reviewing the legal status of supplements containing NAC.

This shift was primarily about regulatory classification, not the safety or efficacy of NAC itself.
