General introduction to the law of success

Originally published in 1928, The Law of Success by Napoleon Hill is a comprehensive philosophy designed to outline the principles of personal achievement. This introductory volume, read by Dan John Miller, focuses on the concept of the "Master Mind." Hill dedicates the course to Andrew Carnegie, who first suggested the project, Henry Ford, whose achievements form its foundation, and Edwin C. Barnes, whose friendship sustained Hill through years of adversity.

Hill begins with a personal statement illustrating how practical ideas attract capital. He recounts the story of Dr. Gonzales, a young Chicago clergyman who delivered a sermon titled "What I Would Do If I Had a Million Dollars." Impressed by Gonzales's vision of a practical technological school, industrialist Philip D. Armour immediately provided the million dollars to establish the Armour Institute of Technology. Hill asserts that capital is always available for those who can create actionable plans.

The power of imagination and cooperation

According to Hill, every major business enterprise, invention, and railroad began in the imagination of a single individual. He points to F.W. Woolworth's five-and-ten-cent store, Thomas Edison's inventions, and Marshall Field's rebuilding of his department store after the Chicago fire as prime examples of the creative faculty of the mind.

While success starts with individual imagination, Hill emphasizes that no one can succeed without the cooperation of others. To secure this cooperation, individuals must first show a willingness to cooperate. He highlights the importance of doing more work than one is paid for, arguing that this habit naturally attracts favorable attention and opportunities for promotion.

The fifteen laws of success

Hill outlines the core laws that form the foundation of his success philosophy:

  1. A definite chief aim: Eliminates aimlessness and helps individuals discover their life's work.
  2. Self-confidence: Helps master the six basic fears and distinguishes true confidence from egotism.
  3. Habit of saving: Systematically distributes income to accumulate personal power.
  4. Initiative and leadership: Develops the instinct to lead rather than follow.
  5. Imagination: Stimulates the mind to create new ideas and plans.
  6. Enthusiasm: Forms the foundation of a pleasing personality to influence others.
  7. Self-control: Acts as a balance wheel to direct enthusiasm constructively.
  8. The habit of doing more than paid for: Leverages the "law of increasing returns" to secure disproportionate rewards.
  9. Pleasing personality: Enables individuals to adapt to and dominate various environments without friction.
  10. Pure thinking: Teaches how to separate facts from mere information and organize them.
  11. Concentration: Focuses attention on a single subject until practical plans are developed.
  12. Cooperation: Emphasizes teamwork and coordinates efforts to eliminate friction.
  13. Profiting by failure: Teaches how to transform temporary defeats into stepping stones.
  14. Tolerance: Prevents racial and religious prejudices from closing the mind to knowledge.
  15. Practicing the Golden Rule: Secures harmonious cooperation from individuals and groups.

The physics of thought and vibration

Hill provides a scientific and philosophical framework for his theories, reducing physical matter to molecules, atoms, and electrons. He notes that all matter is in a constant state of vibration, and posits that different rates of vibration produce different phenomena, such as sound, heat, light, and ultimately, thought.

Drawing on the theories of Dr. Alexander Graham Bell, the inventor of the long-distance telephone, Hill argues that thought is a form of electrical energy carried through the "ether" without wires. He asserts that the human brain operates as both a broadcasting and receiving station for thought vibrations. Hill believes that these vibrations remain in motion indefinitely, turning the universe into a vast "mental library" of human thought.

The chemistry of the mind and the master mind

Hill introduces his theory of "mind chemistry," claiming that individual minds possess electric fields that either attract or repel one another upon contact. When two or more minds coordinate in a spirit of perfect harmony, they create a "Master Mind"—a separate, third mind that provides the group with a sixth sense, heightened imagination, and access to a broader storehouse of ideas.

Hill attributes the immense wealth and power of Henry Ford, Thomas Edison, and Harvey Firestone to their harmonious mental alliance. He also highlights the "Big Six" of Chicago (William Wrigley Jr., John R. Thompson, Albert Lasker, McCullough, Ritchie, and John Hertz) as a group that leveraged this principle to generate millions in annual revenue.

Hill presents several controversial theories regarding mind stimulation, asserting that: * Sexual contact is the most powerful mind stimulant and brain recharger, provided it occurs between partners with genuine affection. * The "revival meeting" is a psychological demonstration of mind chemistry, where music and emotional arousal temporarily blend individual minds into a mass mind. * Spiritualist phenomena do not involve communication with the dead, but are instead the result of participants accessing the subconscious minds of others or thought vibrations preserved in the ether.

Redefining education and the power of vision

Hill argues that the word "educate" is widely misunderstood. Derived from the Latin educo, it means to develop from within through use, rather than to merely memorize rules or accumulate facts.

To illustrate this, Hill recounts Henry Ford's libel suit against the Chicago Tribune. When the newspaper's attorneys tried to prove Ford was an "ignoramus" by asking historical trivia, Ford replied that he had a row of push-buttons on his desk to summon experts who could answer any question. Ford argued that there was no reason to clutter his mind with details when he was surrounded by men who could supply them on demand. Hill asserts that true education is the power to get whatever one needs without violating the rights of others.

In another example, Hill tells of an "uneducated" man of vision who purchased fifty acres of seemingly worthless mountain land from a college president for ten dollars an acre. By building unique log cabins, a dining room, and a gasoline station, the man created a highly profitable tourist resort, earning fifteen thousand dollars in his first year. Hill uses this to demonstrate that practical imagination is far more valuable than theoretical, unapplied knowledge.

The six basic fears of mankind

Hill identifies six specters of fear that hold individuals back from success:

  • The fear of poverty: Arising from physical heredity and man's evolutionary habit of preying on his fellow man economically.
  • The fear of old age: Stemming from the fear of impending poverty and sectarian teachings about fire and brimstone.
  • The fear of ill health: Born of the biological warfare between body cells and disease germs, and reinforced by social suggestions.
  • The fear of loss of love: Rooted in historical mate-stealing, manifesting today as jealousy and social insecurity.
  • The fear of criticism: Capitalized on by industries like fashion, which force conformity through the fear of being out of style.
  • The fear of death: Driven by humanity's age-old anxiety about the afterlife and religious depictions of damnation.

Hill maintains that these fears can be conquered through organized knowledge and deliberate self-suggestion. He recommends reading Benjamin Kidd's The Science of Power, Ralph Waldo Emerson's essay on compensation, and James Harvey Robinson's Mind in the Making to help emancipate the mind.

Inspirational quotes and closing thoughts

The volume concludes with a selection of aphorisms and inspirational quotes from Hill and other thinkers: * "Don't be afraid of a little opposition. Remember that the kite of success generally rises against the wind of adversity, not with it." * "Render more service than that for which you are paid and you will soon be paid for more than you render." * "A winner never quits and a quitter never wins." * "If you cannot do great things yourself, remember that you may do small things in a great way."
