Master Key | The Law of Success, Book 1 of 8 (1928) by Napoleon Hill - "The Master Mind"

Hill's general introduction lays out the fifteen laws, a vibration-and-ether theory of thought, and the Master Mind: two or more minds in perfect harmony creating a third mind.

Book 1 of 8 of Napoleon Hill's The Law of Success (1928), read by Dan John Miller for the Master Key Society. The ideas are Hill's, reported as he states them, not endorsed or verified here. Every timestamp opens the video a few seconds early.

Book 1 is the general introduction to the course, which Hill also titles Lesson 1, "The Mastermind." It teaches no single law the way the later books do. It sets out the course's history and promise, previews each of the fifteen laws, and lays what Hill calls a scientific foundation: matter is organized electrons, all energy is vibration carried by the ether, and thought is one more vibration, so every brain both broadcasts and receives.

On that foundation Hill builds the one idea he claims as his own, the Master Mind: when two or more minds ally in "perfect harmony" for a definite purpose, a "chemistry of the mind" produces a third mind that each member can draw on. He illustrates it with Ford, Edison and Firestone, the Chicago "Big Six," U.S. Steel, revival meetings and sales schools, and extends it to marriage, health, leadership and education. His central argument is that power is organized knowledge put to use, and that organized effort depends on harmony. The book closes with a summary, a personal analysis chart of ten famous men, and an essay on the six basic fears.

Front matter: dedication and a personal statement [00:30]

Hill dedicates the course to Andrew Carnegie, "who suggested the writing of the course," to Henry Ford, whose achievements underlie practically all sixteen lessons, and to Edwin C. Barnes, Edison's business associate, whose friendship carried him through "much temporary defeat."

Imagination and capital [01:19]

A young Chicago clergyman named Gunsaulus preached on "What I Would Do If I Had a Million Dollars," describing a school where young people would learn by doing. Philip D. Armour heard it and gave him the million the next morning, founding the Armour Institute of Technology. Every great railroad, business and invention, Hill says, "began in the imagination of some one person," as with Woolworth's five-and-ten, Edison's inventions, and Marshall Field picturing a new store on the ruins of the Chicago fire [02:46]. The course itself grew from "a chance remark of the late Andrew Carnegie."

Cooperation and the personal analysis chart [04:47]

Success is individual effort only in that each person must decide what he wants; after that it is "a matter of skillfully and tactfully inducing others to cooperate," and you must cooperate before you may ask for cooperation, hence the eighth lesson, the habit of doing more than paid for. Hill introduces a chart of ten well-known men, eight successes and two failures, with blank columns for the student's own ratings at the start and end of the course [05:50]. If you dislike your work, become so useful that those with power promote you [06:54]. Thousands walked over the Calumet copper mine before one man dug; as Russell Conwell's Acres of Diamonds teaches, opportunity is where you stand [07:44].

Acknowledgments and the elements of the lesson [08:44]

The course rests, Hill says, on analysis of over one hundred successful people across more than twenty years. He names Ford, Edison, Harvey S. Firestone, Rockefeller, Charles M. Schwab, Woodrow Wilson, James J. Hill, Woolworth, Elbert Hubbard, Barnes, Luther Burbank, Carnegie, Theodore Roosevelt, Marshall Field and Alexander Graham Bell, "to whom the author owes credit for most of Lesson One," and says he studied most of them "at close range, in person" [10:22]. Apart from the Master Mind he "lays no claim to having created anything basically new," only organized old truths simply enough for high-school students [11:29]. He quotes Judge Elbert H. Gary's praise and lists forty "elements" of the lesson, from power and telepathy to "so-called spiritualism explained" and "the effect of sexual contact as a mind stimulant" [13:01].

Lesson 1: The Master Mind [18:02]

The lesson opens with its motto, "You can do it if you believe you can," and defines success as adjusting to life's changing environments "in a spirit of harmony and poise." Its twofold aim is to help the student find his or her weaknesses and to build a definite plan for bridging them [19:30]: intolerance, greed, jealousy, suspicion, revenge, egotism, the tendency to reap where one has not sown, and spending more than one earns.

The history of the course [20:52]

The philosophy was first a lecture given over seven years across the United States. Its "first big victory" trained three thousand inexperienced people as a "sales army" who earned over a million dollars in six months and paid Hill thirty thousand [21:28]. Don R. Mellett, publisher of the Canton Daily News, became Hill's partner and was assassinated on July 16, 1926; he had arranged with Judge Gary to give the course to every U.S. Steel employee for about $150,000, a plan halted by Gary's death that Hill offers as "proof of the soundness" of the course [22:13]. Students should read the introduction many times and not worry about its technical terms at first [23:23].

The Master Mind introduced [24:01]

The Master Mind is "a newly discovered law of psychology": "a mind that is developed through the harmonious cooperation of two or more people who ally themselves for the purpose of accomplishing any given task." A small group of Prudential agents who tried it each wrote more insurance in three months than in any previous year [24:54].

Personality and the definition of success [25:56]

The personalities behind a business decide its success; people buy personalities as much as merchandise, and most insurance buyers never read their policies. Success "is the attainment of your definite chief aim without violating the rights of other people" [27:33], and the greatest problem of life is "learning the art of harmonious negotiation with others."

The fifteen laws, lesson by lesson [28:56]

  1. A Definite Chief Aim: an end to aimlessness [29:05].
  2. Self-Confidence: mastering the six basic fears and telling egotism from real self-confidence [29:25].
  3. Habit of Saving: "No one may succeed in life without saving money" [29:51].
  4. Initiative and Leadership [30:11].
  5. Imagination: "how to build new houses out of old stones" [30:30].
  6. Enthusiasm: the foundation of a pleasing personality [31:09].
  7. Self-Control: the balance wheel of enthusiasm [31:28].
  8. Doing More Than Paid For: the Law of Increasing Returns [31:45].
  9. Pleasing Personality: the fulcrum for "the crowbar of your efforts" [32:15].
  10. Accurate Thought (heard as "pure thinking" in the reading): separating facts from information [32:47].
  11. Concentration [33:20].
  12. Cooperation: the applied Master Mind [33:51].
  13. Profiting by Failure: failure versus temporary defeat [34:24].
  14. Tolerance: freedom from racial and religious prejudice [34:48].
  15. The Golden Rule, which, Hill stresses, has nothing to do with religion [35:35].

The laws take fifteen to thirty weeks to master [36:13]. Read with a notebook, and teach them to others, to your children and to your wife [36:40].

Power and organized knowledge [37:27]

Power is "one of the three basic objects of human endeavor." It comes from natural physical laws or from organized knowledge, the more important because it lets man harness the first. Knowledge comes from facts organized by others or from personal experience; this lesson deals with the first.

Matter, vibration and the ether [38:34]

Hill starts with matter. The "eighty-odd" elements reduce to molecules, atoms and electrons; "There is no solid physical matter," and elements differ only in their electrons and atoms. Remove two positive electrons from mercury, he says, and it becomes platinum; one more step makes gold, the alchemists' goal [43:29]. An unanalyzed "fluid or force" keeps the atoms moving, and its rate of vibration determines what we perceive: sound, heat, light, ultraviolet, and higher still "the power with which man thinks" [47:25]. Ether fills space and conducts "all vibrations from sound to thought," while air carries only sound [49:52].

Alexander Graham Bell on vibration [52:05]

A long passage attributed to Bell describes an iron rod vibrated faster and faster: felt, heard, silent, then glowing. In the gap the senses miss lie wireless waves and perhaps thought. Thought may be "a form of electrical disturbance" passing through the ether; current once made a dead man's muscles move; and a day may come when "thoughts will be conveyed directly from brain to brain."

Every mind both a broadcasting and a receiving station [58:43]

Hill says he has proved, "to his own satisfaction at least," that every brain broadcasts and receives thought. He quotes Thomas Paine on thoughts that "bolt into the mind of their own accord," the source of almost all Paine's knowledge [59:20]. Every brain is connected to every other through the ether, and Hill is as sure of it as that H2O makes water [1:01:37]. Every released thought stays in motion forever, so the universe is "literally a mental library," a hypothesis he says he will develop in the Self-Confidence lesson [1:02:44]. Fossils in the rocks and thought recorded in the ether form "nature's Bible," "an authentic source of communication between the Creator and man," and radio moves telepathy "from the possible to the probable" [1:05:33].

Chemistry of the mind [1:06:50]

The term Master Mind, Hill says, appears in no textbook; it came from an interview with Carnegie described in Lesson 2. The mind is made of the same energy as the ether. Some minds clash on contact and others attract, producing love at first sight or instant dislike, because the "electrons of the ether" in each set up a chemical reaction [1:08:09]. So a Master Mind is a blending of two or more minds "in a spirit of perfect harmony," from which "the chemistry of the mind creates a third mind"; it disintegrates the moment the alliance breaks [1:09:33].

The same chemistry, he says, explains "soulmate" and "eternal triangle" cases, the quarrelsome first years of marriage, and long-married couples coming to look alike [1:10:08]. A speaker can feel one hostile mind among a thousand; salesmen sense the moment to close; lawyers develop "a sixth sense" for a lying witness [1:12:27]. Anyone can change his mind's chemistry to attract or repel others without a word [1:16:14].

Harmony, leaders and recharging [1:16:42]

Without perfect harmony minds will not blend, "wherein lies the secret of success or failure of practically all business and social partnerships." Leaders blend followers by discipline "voluntary or forced"; Napoleon had a naturally magnetic mind [1:18:48]. One very negative mind blocks a Master Mind. Sales "pep meetings" recharge members, since the brain runs down like a battery and must be recharged by contact with "a more vital mind" [1:19:53]. Hill names sexual contact between people of genuine affection as one of the strongest recharging stimuli, and says great leaders are "people of highly sexed natures" [1:21:38]. A perfectly vitalized brain is "practically, if not entirely, immune" from disease, and medicines, faith, chiropractic and osteopathy only set the mind's chemistry in motion [1:22:43].

Mind chemistry and economic power [1:24:34]

Unlimited power awaits any group that submerges individual interests in perfect harmony. Ford, Edison and Firestone are Hill's lead case [1:26:09]. Ford is "the most powerful man now living on earth," able to "outwit the money trust," and took advance cash orders for over 375,000 cars in 1927 [1:27:45]. Edison is the keenest student of "nature's Bible" [1:28:19]. All three began without capital or schooling, and their yearly retreats to the woods formed, Hill says, a Master Mind that is the real source of their power, though they may not know it [1:30:28].

A Master Mind needs two or more members; six or seven is best [1:32:55]. Hill reports the suggestion that Jesus drew his power from blending the minds of his twelve disciples, until Judas broke faith. Members reach one another's subconscious minds and gain "what appears to be a sixth sense" through which ideas flash in [1:33:39], like batteries joined on one wire stepping up power until it connects with the ether, as a broadcasting station does [1:34:48]. Séance phenomena come from the group's minds or the ether, not from the dead [1:36:57].

The Chicago "Big Six" (William Wrigley Jr., John R. Thompson, Albert Lasker of Lord and Thomas, McCullough of Parmelee, and Ritchie and Hertz of Yellow Cab) meet to trade ideas and earn an estimated $25 million a year [1:39:27]. Elbert H. Gary, "a more or less commonplace small-town lawyer," built U.S. Steel by blending a group of minds [1:42:09]. "Power, manpower, is organized knowledge expressed through intelligent efforts" [1:43:25].

Ford's real assets [1:43:50]

In a college essay exercise, one student of several hundred valued Ford's organized knowledge at 75 percent of his assets. Destroy every Ford plant and dollar, Hill says, and Ford would still be the most powerful man economically on earth [1:45:54]. His start probably came through alliance with Edison, and before that with his wife: "Many a man who never knows the real source of his success is made by his wife" [1:47:42].

Organized effort [1:49:06]

Mergers of banks, railroads and steel plants show that organized effort is the source of industrial power. "Knowledge, general in nature and unorganized, is not power"; extinct animals show that "non-organization means annihilation." Those consciously using mind chemistry could be counted on two hands [1:52:00]. Real harmony is as rare as "genuine Christianity among those who proclaim themselves Christians" [1:53:50]. Wilson's League of Nations was a planned Master Mind of nations [1:54:14].

The psychology of the revival meeting [1:55:10]

With music and emotion the revivalist creates a temporary Master Mind he calls "the Spirit of the Lord." Hill says he produced the same atmosphere among scientists and salespeople. At Harrison Parker's sales school in Chicago, three thousand novices sold over ten million dollars of securities in under nine months, each needing revival-style meetings to be revitalized weekly [1:56:54]. The energy may be the Spirit of the Lord, Hill allows, but it works under any name.

The brain, the body and harmony [1:58:37]

The brain takes in an outside energy, probably ether breathed with oxygen, and distributes it as life. Worry and fear disturb digestion, and a nagging family member can sink a person "into oblivion"; hence "a man's wife may either make or break him" [2:01:34]. Harmony is a law of nature behind health, astronomy, music and business, and "lack of harmony is the first, and often the last and only, cause of failure" [2:05:10]. Each person has at least two personalities, sometimes six, to harmonize [2:06:52], and a Master Mind leader must "change the color of his mind, chameleon-like" [2:08:33].

The meaning of education [2:09:14]

"Educate" comes from educo, to develop from within; unused faculties atrophy. "An educated person is one who knows how to acquire everything he needs in the attainment of his main purpose in life, without violating the rights of his fellow men" [2:10:51]. In Ford's libel suit against the Chicago Tribune, which had called him an "ignorant pacifist," Ford answered a history question with a joke and then said push-buttons on his desk could summon men to answer anything; Hill notes he quotes it "from memory" [2:12:12]. A college president sold fifty acres of "worthless" mountain land to an uneducated man who built tourist cabins and a filling station on the new highway, netting fifteen thousand dollars the first year, then turned a worked-out farm into lakeside lots for a $25,000 profit [2:17:10]. Readers should look for similar "cabin city" sites [2:20:32]. A long list of who can profit follows, from railroad men to unhappy married couples [2:21:26].

Summary of the introductory lesson [2:27:21]

Every meeting of minds creates another mind, but it may be "a negative power" rather than a Master Mind, as some chemical combinations make poisons [2:28:04]. Hill's example is the mother-in-law [2:29:52]. The leader's chief task is grouping men whose minds blend; Carnegie, knowing nothing technical about steel, built his fortune this way, and so did Ford [2:30:36]. The most effective alliances are between men and women, whose minds blend more readily, sexual contact adding stimulus, and a woman can drive a man to success or, by nagging, to defeat [2:31:55]. Elbert Hubbard divorced his first wife for the woman who inspired him, and Hill openly justifies divorce where harmony cannot prevail, while warning that "the trouble may be with your own brain" [2:33:36].

A car dealer paired his twelve salesmen and formed a "one-a-week club," seating non-members at a lunch table set with tinware [2:37:56]. Hill suggests a "policy-a-week club" for insurance, yet says no one should apply this lesson until he has mastered the next five [2:40:08].

The personal analysis chart [2:42:24]

The chart grades Ford, Franklin, Washington, Lincoln, Theodore Roosevelt, Taft, Wilson, Coolidge, Napoleon Bonaparte and Jesse James; the last two are the failures. A zero on any law causes failure, and every success grades 100 percent on a definite chief aim [2:42:51].

The six basic fears [2:43:39]

The fears of poverty, old age, ill health, loss of love, criticism and death come through physical heredity, nature's plan of the strong preying on the weak, and social heredity from the Dark Ages [2:45:07]. Fear of poverty grows from man preying on his fellows economically [2:46:59]; fear of old age from poverty and "false and cruel sectarian teachings" [2:47:53]; fear of ill health from germs and from those who profit by illness [2:48:23]; fear of loss of love fills asylums, since "jealousy is nothing but a form of insanity" [2:49:12]; fear of criticism, Hill claims, makes men bald from tight hat bands and drives fashion [2:50:04]; fear of death is exploited with promises of heaven and threats of hell [2:51:14].

"An enemy discovered is an enemy half-whipped." The remedy is organized knowledge: Benjamin Kidd's The Science of Power, Emerson's "Compensation," a book on auto-suggestion, and James Harvey Robinson's The Mind in the Making [2:52:46]. One month of directed self-suggestion puts your foot "upon the neck" of every fear [2:55:30]. "The man who is powerful fears nothing, not even God" [2:56:21]. The book ends with lines from Shelley and a page of Hill's sayings [2:56:50].
