During the 1970s Great Inflation, nominal incomes grew 9–10%+ even during recessions; today, income growth has stalled. Without income growth, higher oil or energy costs trigger demand destruction, not inflation.

  5. The Fed is fighting the wrong enemy: By hiking rates to prevent a wage-price spiral that cannot exist without robust income growth, the Fed is tightening into an economy that is already contracting. Income growth and inflation mechanism

EurodollarU walks through the inflation transmission mechanism: money enters the economy → business activity → wages and incomes → consumers afford higher prices → businesses pass costs along. During the Great Inflation (1965–1980s), nominal personal income grew at 9–10% sustained, even during recessions. This income growth was the mechanism that sustained inflation.

Today, nominal income growth has collapsed alongside the labor market. The speaker compares 2026 income growth to near-recession periods (2012, 2015–16, 2001, 1990–91), not inflationary periods. Without this income growth, higher oil prices cannot be inflationary; instead, they destroy demand because consumers lack the income to pay for both energy and other goods.

Debunking the oil-price inflation myth

EurodollarU argues that the 1970s Great Inflation is widely misunderstood. The inflation began in the early 1960s, driven by monetary growth through the Eurodollar system, which boosted nominal incomes. By the time OPEC's oil embargo hit in October 1973, the Great Inflation was already eight years old and halfway through. Oil prices created two recessions (demand destruction), not the inflation itself. Richard Nixon's 1971 wage and price controls were a response to runaway income growth, not oil prices.

The lesson: oil prices are a symptom and demand-destruction trigger in weak-income environments, not an inflation driver.

TIPS market as truth-telling mechanism

The TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) market and break-even inflation rates show near-zero inflation risk across all maturities. If the marketplace believed oil prices would become inflationary, demand for inflation protection would rise, pushing break-even rates higher. Instead, they remain flat and low. This is the market's way of saying: "We don't see the income growth needed for inflation, so we don't see inflation risk."

EurodollarU argues that anyone discussing inflation risk without citing TIPS, inflation swaps, or other financial market signals is either "lying to you or doing you a very big disservice."

The disconnect between Fed and market

The Federal Reserve and mainstream economists focus on the unemployment rate and occasional strong monthly payroll numbers, missing the trend. The bond market, credit spreads, and TIPS market have already priced in weak growth and low inflation. The Fed's continued focus on inflation risk and rate hikes is fighting a battle the market has already decided is unnecessary.

This disconnect explains why interest rates have been falling from 2023 onward despite the Fed's hawkish rhetoric: the market is pricing the real economy (fragile labor market, weak income growth, demand destruction), while the Fed is pricing a narrative (resilient labor market, inflation risk).
