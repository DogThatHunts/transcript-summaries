The illusion of stimulus and the reality of Chinese bank de-risking

The record contraction in bank lending

EurodollarU highlights that July loan flows in China experienced a record contraction. While July is historically a seasonal low point for lending, this dramatic drop exposes a deeper, systemic retreat. Chinese banks, which serve as the centerpiece of the domestic economy and previously fueled the massive post-2008 real estate bubble, are aggressively pulling back.

Because the real economy cannot support the weight of this historic property bubble, banks have accumulated massive amounts of hidden bad loans. These non-performing assets are tied up not only in real estate but also in local government financing vehicles (LGFVs). To protect their balance sheets, banks are actively de-risking by halting credit flows to any sector perceived as risky.

Milton Friedman's interest rate fallacy in action

EurodollarU argues that China's current situation perfectly illustrates Milton Friedman's interest rate fallacy. While mainstream economists and media outlets routinely interpret low interest rates as economic stimulus, EurodollarU asserts that low rates are actually a symptom of severe economic weakness and high risk.

In a bid to de-risk, Chinese banks are refusing to lend to households and corporations, choosing instead to purchase safe government bonds. This massive demand for safety has driven government bond yields to historic lows. This behavior is a hallmark of "depression economics," where the financial system becomes trapped in a self-reinforcing loop: a weakening economy prompts banks to pull back lending and buy bonds, which starves the economy of credit and worsens the downturn.

Why Beijing is cracking down on the wealthy now

Beijing recently announced a surprise tax crackdown targeting offshore capital gains and tax avoidance schemes of wealthy individuals, giving taxpayers until October to file and pay before facing severe penalties. EurodollarU explains that this is not a new law, but rather the sudden enforcement of existing rules that authorities previously ignored.

This sudden enforcement is a direct response to the fiscal desperation of local governments. Caught in the crossfire of the real estate bust and the banking sector's credit freeze, many local governments are in dire financial straits, reportedly failing to pay suppliers, maintain basic services, or pay employees. Beijing hopes to use the recovered tax revenues to shore up local government finances, prevent defaults on local debt, and potentially restore their access to the credit markets.

Macroeconomic deterioration and the fragile export thread

Recent macroeconomic data corroborates this downward trajectory. While China has benefited from a temporary export boom, its domestic economy is in a severe bust. The manufacturing PMI stumbled unexpectedly, and the July non-manufacturing PMI fell to 49—one of the lowest levels in the history of the series, comparable only to the zero-COVID lockdowns of late 2022.

EurodollarU notes that the manufacturing sector is the thin thread holding the Chinese economy together. If global demand softens or trade tensions escalate, China will lose its last line of economic support. This looming threat is precisely why both Chinese banks and the central government are acting with such urgency.

The long road of managed decline since 2018

According to EurodollarU, the roots of this crisis trace back to 2018, a pivotal year when Chinese leadership realized the country's debt-fueled growth model was unsustainable and shifted toward a policy of "managed decline."

Since then, the gap between industrial production (supported by exports) and retail sales (reflecting domestic demand) has widened significantly. Retail sales have plummeted, demonstrating that domestic consumers are facing severe economic hardships. Ultimately, EurodollarU concludes that government stimulus measures are merely reactive attempts to manage a disorderly decay rather than tools capable of engineering a genuine economic recovery.
