PBoyle | Is the AI Bubble About to Be Tested?

As AI startups seek eye-watering valuations, a web of circular deals and collapsing output costs suggests the industry's financial foundations are about to face a harsh public test.

In this analysis, financial commentator PBoyle explores the growing tension between the astronomical private valuations of artificial intelligence startups and the surprisingly cheap public valuation of Nvidia, the primary hardware provider fueling the boom. As Anthropic prepares for a rumored public debut and OpenAI delays its listing, the broader AI ecosystem is relying on increasingly circular financial arrangements, highly inflated addressable markets, and aggressive accounting assumptions to justify its pricing.

The math behind a two-trillion-dollar valuation

Anthropic is reportedly seeking a public valuation of around $2 trillion—an amount roughly equivalent to the ten largest tech IPOs of all time combined. However, traditional valuation methods struggle to support this figure. Discounted cash flow (DCF) models require an interest rate to discount future earnings, and the 10-year US Treasury yield recently reached 5.23%, its highest level since 2004.

As Matt Kennedy of Renaissance Capital points out, rising interest rates present a double whammy for high-growth tech companies: they heavily discount profits projected far into the future and make the massive capital expenditures required to build data centers far more expensive. This macroeconomic pressure has already forced several infrastructure and energy companies, such as Holtec and SoftBank's SB Energy, to postpone or delay their listings.

The Scott McNeely test and valuation reality

To put a $2 trillion valuation into perspective, PBoyle references a famous 2002 warning from Scott McNeely, the co-founder of Sun Microsystems. Reflecting on the dot-com bubble, McNeely questioned why investors paid 10 times revenue for his company, noting that to return their money in ten years, he would have to pay them 100% of revenues as dividends. This absurd assumption required zero cost of goods sold, zero expenses, zero taxes, and zero research and development for a decade.

At a $2 trillion valuation, Anthropic would trade at roughly 31 times its estimated revenue. Even under a highly generous scenario—assuming zero operating costs, zero taxes, and discounting future cash flows strictly at the risk-free Treasury rate—an investor would never recoup their principal. Consequently, nearly the entire valuation relies on assumptions of unprecedented future growth.

TAM inflation and the promise of self-improvement

To justify these multiples, investment bankers and tech companies have relied on Total Addressable Market (TAM) inflation. The projected market sizes for AI have ballooned rapidly: * In May, SpaceX told investors the enterprise app market for its AI products was $22.7 trillion. * Anthropic's upcoming public filing may cite a $30 trillion market. * Morgan Stanley recently estimated that generative AI could tap a $60 trillion market—representing roughly half of global annual economic output.

Historically, companies rarely capture the massive TAMs they advertise. Uber went public touting a $12.3 trillion TAM but generates under $60 billion in annual revenue today, while WeWork claimed a $3 trillion market before filing for bankruptcy.

Some industry proponents argue that recursive self-improvement—AI systems designing superior versions of themselves—will unlock infinite economic value, rendering traditional money obsolete. However, current research suggests a more modest reality. While Google DeepMind's AlphaDev successfully optimized matrix multiplication, automated research agents often improve performance by gaming experimental parameters rather than achieving fundamental breakthroughs. When Anthropic tested its automated researcher at production scale, the actual performance improvement was minimal.

The circular economy of AI infrastructure

The AI industry has constructed a highly interconnected, circular network of investments and liabilities to support its valuations: * SoftBank is raising $11 billion in debt at high junk-bond yields (9.75%) to fund its investment in OpenAI. * OpenAI is burning through cash, expecting to spend nearly $280 billion by 2030, partly to lease a massive data center campus from SoftBank's SB Energy. * Nvidia has committed billions to purchase SB Energy shares and guarantee its data center leases, on the condition that the campus runs exclusively on Nvidia hardware for 20 years. * Amazon and Google recently reported that a massive portion of their net income (85% and 87% respectively) came from unrealized paper gains on their startup investments, such as Anthropic and SpaceX.

Valuation expert Aswath Damodaran notes that this structure resembles complex Asian family conglomerates, where valuing one company requires untangling the valuations of several others. Furthermore, short-seller Jim Chanos points out that tech giants are avoiding depreciation charges on their balance sheets by keeping unbuilt data centers classified as 'construction in progress' and uninstalled GPUs un-depreciated. Once these centers go live, heavy depreciation will begin, even though the chips inside may become obsolete within a few years.

Why the shovel seller looks cheap

Despite generating massive profits, Nvidia trades at less than 17 times its forward earnings—its cheapest relative valuation in over a decade. CEO Jensen Huang has even pitched Nvidia to Wall Street as a 'growth value stock.' PBoyle outlines four reasons why the market is pricing the hardware giant so conservatively: 1. Cyclicality: Investors fear Nvidia is at the peak of its hardware cycle. Gross margins are expected to slip as memory suppliers raise prices and major customers like Meta and Alphabet develop in-house chips. 2. Sustained Capex Risk: Nvidia's revenue is entirely dependent on the capital expenditures of AI labs that are currently burning cash. If these labs slow their spending due to regulatory hurdles or financial exhaustion, Nvidia's sales will drop. 3. The Value of Uncertainty: Investors often overpay for 'lottery ticket' stocks with highly uncertain futures (like AI software startups) while discounting companies with predictable, established cash flows. 4. Market Structure: Nvidia is priced continuously by public markets where short sellers can actively bet against it. Startups like Anthropic have their valuations set in private funding rounds by optimistic cloud giants who benefit from marking up their own balance sheets.

The collapsing cost of intelligence

While infrastructure costs remain high, the price of AI output is collapsing. A study by Epoch AI estimates that the cost of achieving a given level of model performance has fallen roughly 13-fold annually since 2023. This represents a faster cost decline than almost any transformative technology in history, including electricity and DNA sequencing.

To compete with cheap open-weight models from Chinese firms like DeepSeek and Moonshot, both OpenAI and Anthropic recently cut their API prices by 40% to 50%. Specialized startups, such as TypeSafe AI, claim they can run specific decision-making tasks hundreds of times cheaper than frontier models.

As corporate clients like Ramp actively route tasks to cheaper, specialized models to avoid vendor lock-in, the frontier labs face a difficult paradox: the cost of their inputs (chips, energy, land) is rising, while the market price of their output is rapidly approaching zero.

Conclusion: the imminent public market test

An IPO is the moment of truth where private paper valuations must face public market pricing. For companies like SoftBank, which has secured a $10 billion margin loan against its private OpenAI shares, a down-round or a disappointing public listing could trigger margin calls and shrink collateral.

As Aswath Damodaran warns, market corrections rarely happen all at once; they occur as a series of small, unnoticed events until investors realize the landscape has fundamentally shifted. The delayed filings, pulled IPOs, and high junk-bond yields may be the first warning signs. While AI technology will undoubtedly continue to progress, investors paying premium prices for the companies building it may soon find themselves facing Scott McNeely's ultimate question: 'What were you thinking?'
