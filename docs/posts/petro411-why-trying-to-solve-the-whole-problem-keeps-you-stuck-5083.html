Why Trying to Solve the Whole Problem Keeps you Stuck

Claude Shannon, the mathematician whose work helped make your phone, the internet, and digital storage possible, often solved seemingly impossible problems by deliberately ignoring most of the details. He understood that when every part of a problem is treated as equally important, the structure that actually determines the answer can disappear underneath the noise.

  • Shannon focused on the essential structure shared by different communication forms—like telephone conversations, photographs, radio transmissions, and written sentences—before they were seen as distinct problems.
  • By asking how much information can be transmitted through a noisy channel and how it can be reliably recovered, he created a mathematical framework that applies across diverse communication types.
  • This method—remove the problem’s non-essential details, solve the simplified version, then gradually reintroduce complexity—is a practical tool for personal challenges.

For example, when facing a life crisis like 'my life is going nowhere,' the full scope feels overwhelming. Simplifying it to 'during the next three months, I need to test one new professional direction without giving up my current income' creates a tangible, workable goal. The missing details remain, but the problem now has a clear structure that governs everything else.
