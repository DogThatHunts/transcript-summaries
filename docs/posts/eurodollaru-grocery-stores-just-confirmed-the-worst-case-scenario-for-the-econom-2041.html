EurodollarU | Grocery Stores Just Confirmed the Worst-Case Scenario for the Economy

As grocery giants warn of declining sales and consumers slash spending on essentials, real-world economic indicators signal a looming labor market correction that mainstream models ignore.

In this episode, hosts Jeff and Steve of Eurodollar University discuss how recent earnings reports from grocery giant Kroger and retail data expose a stark disconnect between mainstream economic narratives of resilience and the financial reality of everyday Americans. As persistent energy costs and flat wage growth squeeze household budgets, consumers are pulling back on essential purchases and consolidating shopping trips. Jeff and Steve argue that this shift in consumer behavior, coupled with rising input costs for small and medium-sized businesses, is forcing a quiet hiring freeze that could soon trigger a broader labor market correction. They contrast the Federal Reserve's reliance on lagging econometric models with the highly accurate "small 'e' economics" reflected in consumer sentiment surveys, which currently signal recession-level anxiety.

  • Grocery pullbacks signal extreme budget pressure: When consumers cut back, hunt for discounts, and buy fewer items at supermarkets like Kroger, it indicates that financial strain has reached non-discretionary, daily essentials.
  • The squeeze on small and medium-sized businesses: Unlike large corporations, small and mid-sized businesses did not receive a lasting profitability boost from pandemic-era stimulus and are now caught between rising input costs (like energy) and consumers' refusal to accept higher prices.
  • The labor market is the next variable to crack: Because businesses cannot raise prices further to protect their margins, they are running lean operations and freezing hiring, making job cuts the most likely next step.
  • Consumer surveys align with historical recessions: Data from the University of Michigan and the New York Fed show that consumer expectations for rising unemployment have reached levels historically consistent with every major recession since the 1970s.
  • Lagging Fed models ignore real-time warning signs: Jeff and Steve assert that the Federal Reserve and mainstream media overlook critical, real-time indicators of consumer distress—such as reduced restaurant traffic and consolidated shopping trips—in favor of flawed, lagging mathematical models.

The grocery store warning sign

Kroger's recent decision to lower its sales forecast serves as a critical warning sign for the broader economy. Because groceries are non-luxury essentials, any pullback in this sector indicates that financial pressure has reached the most basic parts of daily life. Jeff notes that retailers like Walmart and Kroger are trying to cut prices to entice customers back, but even these discounts are failing to restore previous traffic levels.

Instead of making multiple spontaneous trips to the store, consumers are consolidating their shopping into single, highly planned trips and avoiding high-margin impulse buys. This shift at the margins is highly damaging to grocery chains and retailers that operate on razor-thin margins to begin with.

The squeeze on household budgets and small businesses

While mainstream analysts celebrate static wage growth as a sign of stabilizing inflation, Steve points out that flat wages in an environment of rising energy and food costs create a severe squeeze on households. A global shortage of diesel fuel and rising crude prices mean that energy costs are likely to remain elevated through the winter, further draining consumer purchasing power.

This squeeze is equally severe for small and medium-sized businesses (SMBs). Unlike multinational corporations, SMBs did not accumulate massive cash cushions during the 2021–2022 stimulus boom. Today, they face rising input costs but cannot raise their output prices because consumers have hit a hard limit on what they are willing or able to pay. As a result, these businesses have been forced to run incredibly lean operations, cutting employee hours and freezing hiring to defend their margins.

Why the labor market is poised to crack

When businesses run out of room to absorb rising costs and can no longer raise prices, the only variable they can control is labor. Jeff and Steve argue that the ongoing hiring freeze is the precursor to a more painful labor market correction.

While the mainstream media focuses on the absence of mass layoffs, Jeff explains that a lack of hiring is often the primary driver of recessions. Consumers are highly sensitive to this shift; they see colleagues not being replaced and hear management discussing budget constraints. This real-time anxiety is reflected in the University of Michigan survey, where consumer expectations for rising unemployment have reached levels identical to the worst periods of past recessions.

Mainstream models versus real-world economics

There is a profound disconnect between the Federal Reserve's econometric models and the lived experience of the American public. Steve highlights that the New York Fed's own survey showed the expectation for unemployment to rise reached 44.4%—the highest level since April 2020. Despite having access to this data, policymakers often ignore consumer sentiment, believing their mathematical models are superior.

Jeff argues that everyday Americans possess a far deeper understanding of "small 'e' economics" than PhD economists. Consumers observe real-time leading indicators—such as declining restaurant traffic, longer intervals between haircuts, and reduced discretionary spending—long before these trends register in official government data. When consumers perceive that their jobs are at risk, they behave prudently by cutting spending, creating a self-reinforcing economic slowdown.
