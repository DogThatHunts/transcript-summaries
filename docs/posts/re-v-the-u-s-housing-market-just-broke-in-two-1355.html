The U.S. housing market just broke in two

Overview

RE-V argues that the American housing market is experiencing unprecedented bifurcation. Rather than a uniform national trend, the market has split into two opposing zones: the Midwest and Northeast maintain low inventory and rising home values, while the South, Southwest, and West Coast face inventory surges and falling prices. This divergence is driven by migration patterns, local economic growth, and affordability differences, and it has profound implications for buyers and sellers depending on their location.

Regional inventory patterns

The core divide is stark in inventory levels. The South and West have returned to pre-pandemic inventory levels, while the Midwest and Northeast remain down 50% from historical norms. RE-V highlights that housing supply in the South and West now totals 741,000 listings, compared to just 215,000 in the Midwest and Northeast—more than three times as many homes for sale in the surplus regions.

States with the lowest home prices—Wisconsin, Iowa, Kentucky, West Virginia, and Ohio—show the strongest buyer demand. West Virginia leads the nation with buyer demand up 30% from average, driven by homes available for $170,000 to $300,000. Meanwhile, the Northeast maintains acute shortages: Connecticut's inventory is down 50% from normal, and New Jersey's is down 25%, fueling continued bidding wars despite higher price points.

Price movements and market signals

The inventory surplus directly predicts price direction. RE-V presents data showing an r-squared correlation of 0.48 between inventory surplus and home value growth—meaning half of the variation in price movement is explained by supply levels alone. States with high inventory (Washington up 64%, Tennessee up 58%, Arizona up 53%, Texas up 52%) are seeing significant price declines, while low-inventory states (Wisconsin, North Dakota, Illinois) show appreciation of 4–5% year-over-year.

RE-V illustrates this with specific examples. A Washington townhouse listed at $835,000 in 2025 (a 44% increase from its 2017 purchase price of $582,000) has since dropped to $699,000 and remains unsold, reflecting the 64% inventory surplus in the state. A Nashville property purchased for $1.3 million in 2023 sold for just $985,000—a $300,000 loss—demonstrating the rapid deflation in high-inventory markets.

Why the split is happening

RE-V attributes the regional divergence to three factors: migration patterns, local economic growth, and affordability. The Midwest and Northeast are experiencing reverse migration as people move back closer to family, onshoring of manufacturing is creating jobs, and some are relocating due to climate concerns. The Atlantic recently covered this trend, noting that the Midwest has become the place to move, primarily on affordability grounds.

Conversely, the Sun Belt experienced explosive migration and builder activity during the pandemic boom, creating oversupply. As that migration wave slows and some residents exit, inventory piles up and prices fall.

The durability question

RE-V raises an open question: how durable is this new bifurcation? If the trend persists, buyers in the South and West may see persistently higher inventory levels and continued price reductions, while Midwest and Northeast buyers will face sustained competition and appreciation. The data strongly suggests this split will continue, at least in the near term, but RE-V does not speculate on longer-term reversals.
