BREAKING: Private Credit Defaults Just Hit a Record High

A record-high default rate in private credit exposes a deepening liquidity squeeze, trapping lender capital and forcing a quiet rationing of cash across the financial system.

The illusion of the 6.3% default rate

According to Fitch Ratings, the trailing 12-month default rate across 1,300 U.S. private credit borrowers climbed to a record 6.3% in August. However, host Jeff Snider argues that the headline default rate obscures a more critical systemic issue: 45% of these August defaults were caused by "stressed maturity extensions."

Rather than a borrower completely halting payments, these defaults represent transactions where lenders are forced to grant borrowers more time because the borrowers cannot refinance or repay their debt on the original schedule. This shift indicates that defaults are no longer single, dramatic events, but have instead transformed into rolling, protracted negotiations that trap lender capital.

The mechanics of stressed maturity extensions and payment-in-kind interest

When a loan maturity is extended, the expected return of principal to the private credit fund is delayed, preventing the fund from distributing cash to investors or redeploying it into new opportunities. To avoid immediate bankruptcies, lenders are increasingly turning to alternative structures:

  • Payment-in-kind (PIK) interest: Borrowers add unpaid interest to the outstanding loan balance rather than paying in cash. Data from Houlihan Lokey reveals that in Q2, 11.8% of loans on a size-weighted basis elected to pay at least some interest in kind, representing 6.3% of total interest dollars.
  • Coupon reductions: Lenders may reduce the interest rate in exchange for additional collateral or equity from the private equity sponsor.

While these mechanisms postpone liquidations, they widen the gap between a fund's reported accounting income and its actual cash income, reducing overall liquidity within the asset class.

The wider liquidity squeeze: real estate and redemption traps

This liquidity mismatch is not isolated to private credit; it is actively spreading to private real estate funds. Open-ended funds allow investors to request redemptions, but the underlying physical properties cannot be liquidated quickly.

  • Blackstone Property Partners (BPP): Blackstone is attempting to facilitate secondary sales for investors in its $11 billion U.S. real estate fund. To deter flighty investors, Blackstone has offered reduced management fees for those who keep their redemption requests below a specific threshold.
  • Invesco: Invesco has explored tender offers and cut fees in its own U.S. core real estate fund to manage redemption pressure.

When secondary market transactions are arranged to provide immediate cash, new buyers often demand steep discounts, turning liquidity pressure into direct valuation pressure.

The concentration of stress in small-to-mid-sized borrowers

Credit stress is highly concentrated among smaller, highly leveraged companies that lack diversified revenue streams and capital-raising alternatives. Houlihan Lokey's data highlights a stark divergence based on borrower size:

  • Small borrowers (EBITDA of $10M–$20M): 12% of loans are now valued below 90 cents on the dollar, a massive jump from just 1% in 2023.
  • Core middle market (EBITDA of $20M–$100M): 6% of loans are priced below 90% of par, marking a three-year high.
  • Large borrowers (EBITDA over $100M): Only 3% of loans are valued below 90% of par.

Sector-wise, Fitch identified healthcare and industrial manufacturing as the most stressed industries in August, with both recording a 9.9% default rate. While software defaults remained low at 0.6% (down from 1.2% in July), Snider warns that future AI disruption and declining valuations could quickly turn software into a source of credit trouble.

Affiliated lending and regulatory scrutiny in insurance companies

Insurance companies have historically been a vital source of stable, long-term capital for private credit. However, regulatory scrutiny is exposing hidden concentration risks within these portfolios.

Delaware Life and Clear Spring Life and Annuity—two insurers controlled by Mark Walter's TWG Global—recently disclosed more than $20 billion in loans and investments that had to be reclassified as "affiliated" after regulatory inquiries. While TWG Global maintains that no investors have been harmed and no criminal charges have been filed, the disclosure raises concerns about potential conflicts of interest, specifically whether policyholder premiums were used to finance entities benefiting the parent company's owner.

To resolve these inquiries and clean up its balance sheet, Walter has had to restructure holdings, including agreeing to sell the Los Angeles Lakers (at a $12.5 billion valuation) and negotiating the sale of stakes in the Chelsea soccer club.

The AI bridge loan gamble and rising credit default swaps

At the upper end of the market, massive capital demands for AI infrastructure are testing the limits of bank balance sheets. A consortium of 10 banks is currently providing a temporary $22 billion bridge loan to support Blackstone and Alphabet's Crux AI Cloud Venture, backed by Google Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) and customer contracts.

Typically, such large financings bypass banks to go directly to the bond market. The use of a bank bridge loan assumes that investment-grade bond investors will eventually refinance the debt. If the bond market becomes saturated with AI debt or spreads rise, banks may be forced to hold this highly concentrated exposure longer than anticipated.

Reflecting these growing credit concerns, SoftBank's five-year credit default swaps (CDS) recently climbed to their highest level since 2023, signaling that the cost of insuring AI-related credit exposure is rising.

Conclusion: what real monetary tightening looks like

Snider concludes that true monetary tightening is not merely a policy rate selection by central bankers. Rather, real tightening occurs when money moves more slowly through the financial system. The market is shifting from an era where capital was distributed without close scrutiny to a cautious, restrictive environment where liquidity is actively rationed, forcing private funds and insurers into a phase of "bubble cleanup."
