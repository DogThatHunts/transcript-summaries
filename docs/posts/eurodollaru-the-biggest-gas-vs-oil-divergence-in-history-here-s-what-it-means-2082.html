A record-high crack spread reveals why gasoline and diesel remain stubbornly expensive even as global crude prices fall and Chinese demand collapses.

The historic divergence between crude oil and refined fuel

Despite a significant drop in crude oil prices from their wartime highs, consumers continue to face stubbornly high prices at the pump. Jeff Snyder of EurodollarU explains that this disconnect is driven by an extraordinary bottleneck in the refining sector, where the margin for converting raw crude into usable fuel has reached historic levels. While the world has found itself with an unexpected surplus of crude oil, it remains starved of finished products like gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel in the places where they are needed most.

Understanding the crack spread and the 3-2-1 formula

Snyder points out that the primary metric explaining this divergence is the "crack spread," which measures the difference between the price a refinery pays for crude oil and the wholesale market value of the refined products it produces. It serves as a real-time indicator of refining capacity abundance or scarcity.

To standardize this measurement, the industry uses the 3-2-1 crack spread model:

  • Inputs: Three barrels of crude oil.
  • Outputs: Two barrels of gasoline and one barrel of distillate (such as diesel or heating oil).
  • The calculation: The market value of the finished products minus the cost of the three crude barrels, divided by three to establish a per-barrel gross margin.

Currently, this spread sits at an unprecedented $60 per barrel, implying a gross margin of over $180 across the three-barrel model. Snyder notes that while this provides an enormous economic incentive for refiners to process crude, it also highlights that crude prices have collapsed far faster than finished fuel prices.

The aftermath of the Hormuz disruption and supply mismatches

According to Snyder, the divergence is a direct consequence of how different parts of the energy supply chain recovered from the recent Strait of Hormuz closure and subsequent mid-June ceasefire between the U.S. and Iran:

  • Rapid crude recovery: Once the Strait partially reopened, stranded tankers sailed and stored crude quickly re-entered the market, dragging crude prices down toward pre-war levels.
  • Sluggish fuel recovery: Converting crude into fuel is a multi-week process. Crude must be unloaded, processed, blended to meet local environmental specifications (such as American summer gasoline), and transported through pipelines and terminals.
  • Depleted inventories: Global stockpiles of finished fuels were heavily drawn down during the months of disruption and cannot be replenished quickly, especially with refineries already operating at near-maximum utilization rates (95.8% in the U.S.).

China's dual impact: bearish for crude, tight for fuel

Snyder highlights that China is experiencing severe domestic economic deterioration, marked by a property downturn, weak retail sales, and a struggling banking sector. This has led to a dramatic reduction in energy demand:

  • Chinese crude imports collapsed by 41% year-over-year in June, falling to near a decade low.
  • Chinese refinery processing volumes dropped 18% to their lowest level since March 2020.
  • Independent Chinese refiners, which represent a third of the country's capacity, saw operating rates drop to a nine-year low.

While this lack of Chinese buying is highly bearish for global crude oil prices—forcing exporters like Saudi Arabia to slash official selling prices—Snyder explains that it has a counterintuitive effect on finished fuels. Because Chinese refiners are processing less crude, they are producing and exporting far less gasoline and diesel, further tightening the international marketplace for finished products.

U.S. and European inventory strains

Even though consumer demand is weak—U.S. gasoline consumption is down 2.2% compared to last year—inventories continue to fall because supply is even tighter.

  • Distillates and diesel: U.S. distillate inventories are 12% below their five-year seasonal average. Snyder notes this is highly unusual given weak manufacturing and freight conditions, pointing to a severe supply deficit rather than high demand.
  • European vulnerability: Europe is highly sensitive to diesel shortages due to its historical reliance on imports, lost domestic refining capacity, sanctions, and strict fuel specifications.

Because retail pump prices incorporate crude costs, refining margins, taxes, and distribution, a drop in crude is easily offset if the refining spread rises. Snyder emphasizes that motorists will not see relief until crack spreads normalize.

Four scenarios for the energy market's future

Snyder outlines four potential paths for how this market imbalance could resolve:

  1. The best case: Crude shipments normalize, refinery runs increase, inventories are rebuilt, and crack spreads collapse, passing lower costs to consumers.
  2. Persistent bottlenecks: Chinese economic weakness keeps crude cheap, but refining and logistical bottlenecks persist, keeping fuel prices high and crack spreads at record levels.
  3. Severe demand destruction: A broad-based, global economic downturn crushes overall fuel consumption, forcing crack spreads down for the worst possible reason.
  4. The double whammy: Crude prices rebound due to geopolitical factors before refining bottlenecks are resolved, hitting consumers with both high oil costs and elevated refining margins.
