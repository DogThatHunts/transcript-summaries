Tom Bilyeu dismantles the California wealth tax proposal and the 'buy, borrow, die' myth, exposing structural flaws in healthcare and tax policy.

California wealth tax proposition 40 fails but reveals dangerous logic

  • The measure appeared on the ballot and was defeated, yet its presence signals a troubling shift in political thinking.
  • Adam Conover argued for a one-time 5% tax on net worth above $1 billion, claiming it would raise ~$100 billion to plug a Medicaid funding gap caused by federal cuts.
  • Bilyeu counters that the tax is mechanistically flawed: it targets wealth (illiquid assets), not income, forcing liquidation that crashes asset values and drives away the very taxpayers it targets.
  • Within days of the proposal, an estimated $1 trillion in net worth left California, taking billions in future tax revenue with it.
  • The tax would hit only ~250 people (0.00006% of Californians), but their companies and employees generate disproportionate revenue; losing them compounds the fiscal hole.

Healthcare costs are a structural incentive problem, not a funding problem

  • U.S. healthcare spending hit $5.7 trillion in 2025; employer family plans exceed $28,000/year, rising 9% annually.
  • The U.S. has fewer doctors, nurses, and hospital beds per capita than peer nations but pays 254% of Medicare rates for the same services.
  • Root cause: a 1940s wage freeze pushed compensation into tax-free employer health benefits; Congress made this permanent in 1954.
  • This breaks the price signal: the patient doesn't pay, the employer doesn't feel the cost (pre-tax dollars), and HR — not the patient — chooses plans with no incentive to control costs.
  • Post-Obamacare, insurers must spend 80–85% of premiums on care, capping overhead at 15–20%. Since profit is a percentage of premiums, insurers gain when hospital prices rise.
  • Self-insured employers use third-party administrators who lack strong incentives to negotiate lower prices.
  • Result: every actor in the chain (hospitals, insurers, employers) benefits from higher prices; no one innovates to lower them.
  • Bilyeu argues single-payer won't fix this — European systems face similar cost crises — and only restoring patient-level price sensitivity via free-market competition can reverse the trend.

The "buy, borrow, die" narrative is a myth; reality is "buy, save, die"

  • Popular claim: billionaires avoid taxes by never selling assets, borrowing against them, rolling loans forward, and wiping gains at death via step-up in basis.
  • Federal Reserve data (Fox, Michigan; Liscow, Yale) on top 1% households ($14M+ net worth) shows new borrowing is only 1–2% of economic income per year.
  • Unrealized gains are 20–40x larger than borrowing; leverage does not rise with portfolio value because debt is dangerous — collateral volatility triggers margin calls.
  • Wealthy individuals actually earn large taxable income (salaries, dividends, interest, realized gains), pay tax on it, spend a fraction, and save/invest the rest.
  • Assets compound untaxed until death, when step-up in basis erases the gain for heirs.
  • Only 32 Forbes 400 members pledge public stock as collateral; Elon Musk alone accounts for 47% of pledged value, and loan turnover runs ~30% quarterly — loans are repaid and reissued, not held forever.

The real fix: eliminate step-up in basis at death

  • Tax heirs at the original cost basis of the benefactor, not the stepped-up value at death.
  • This captures the unrealized gain when assets are eventually sold, functions as a wealth tax only at realization, and avoids forced liquidation or valuation disputes.
  • Heirs can still hold assets, but any future sale triggers tax at the original basis.
  • Simple, structurally sound, and avoids the destructive mechanics of a net-worth tax.

Property tax is not a valid analogy for a wealth tax

  • A viewer compared wealth tax to property tax (annual charge on home value regardless of sale).
  • Bilyeu rejects property tax as legitimate: "Either it's my property and I own it or it's not." Seizing a paid-off home for unpaid property tax is "fucking crazy."
  • Property taxes are somewhat constrained (caps on assessment increases, ~1% rates), but a wealth tax on volatile, illiquid assets lacks such guardrails and would be far more destructive.
