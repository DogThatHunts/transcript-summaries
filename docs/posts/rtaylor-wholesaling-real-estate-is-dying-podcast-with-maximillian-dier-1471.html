Key insights

  • Regulatory changes are squeezing on-market wholesaling: New disclosure mandates (such as Ohio's wholesale disclosure requirements) and strict title company interpretations are making on-market real estate assignment difficult, pushing operators toward off-market outreach or actual property takedowns.
  • The "1-1-1-1 rule" prevents beginner failure: Maximillian emphasizes that new investors must focus on one target market, one property type (single-family), one deal structure (cash deals), and one lead generation channel before attempting to scale or diversify.
  • Turnkey cash-flow properties dominate buyer demand: Cash buyers are currently paying premiums for inexpensive Midwest and Southern small multifamily properties (duplexes/triplexes) that produce a verifiable 20% cash-on-cash return with existing tenants.
  • Subject-to deals require genuine cash flow to survive: The prior model of acquiring low-equity 3% interest rate mortgages with zero cash flow has collapsed, resulting in foreclosures for operators unable to subsidize deficits through alternative rental schemes like sober living or mid-term rentals.
  • Scaling JV disposition through specialized talent: Maximillian and Richard leverage audience-submitted contracts and direct-to-seller leads by centralizing underwriting and relying on dedicated disposition specialists to move volume across multiple markets.

Finding superpowers and lead generation strategies

The conversation begins with an examination of how each investor aligns their daily workflow with their specific operational strengths. Maximillian Dier emphasizes that his primary advantage lies in rapid underwriting and buyer matchmaking, allowing him to partner with other wholesalers through JV deals rather than spending all day cold calling. For proprietary lead generation, Max prefers paid inbound marketing, Google ads, and trained virtual assistant teams over cold text blasting.

For investors starting with little to no capital, Max highlights the efficiency of door-knocking high-equity government distress lists—such as pre-foreclosures, code violations, and tax delinquencies. He notes that while distressed homeowners rarely answer cold phone calls, in-person outreach often yields the largest assignment fees, citing recent deals producing $20,000 to $55,000 in revenue.

The 1-1-1-1 framework for new wholesalers

To avoid getting overwhelmed by competing real estate tactics, Max advocates for strict operational constraints for beginners:

  1. One market: Lock into a single, predictable cash-flow market (such as the Rust Belt or Midwest) rather than attempting nationwide virtual wholesaling.
  2. One property type: Stick exclusively to single-family residential properties before considering land or commercial assets.
  3. One transaction strategy: Master straightforward cash wholesale transactions before attempting complex creative financing or subject-to structures.
  4. One marketing channel: Focus on a single outreach method matching the available budget—realtor outreach or free government lists for zero capital, graduating to texting, cold calling, and paid ads as reserves grow.

Legal hurdles and the pivot to fix-and-flips

Richard Taylor argues that on-market wholesaling is increasingly unviable due to title company pushback and emerging state laws that mandate explicit assignment disclosures to sellers. When attempting to wholesale MLS-listed properties through agents in states like Ohio, title companies routinely refuse to close without signed disclosures, and double closes face scrutiny regarding buyer intent.

Because of these friction points, Richard has shifted a substantial portion of his operation toward purchasing and flipping lower-priced houses ($150,000 or less) in markets like Detroit, Akron, and Oklahoma City. By using private hard-money lenders who fund 100% of the purchase price and renovation costs, he avoids wholesaling disclosure hurdles while retaining the ability to refinance into long-term DSCR rental loans if a property fails to sell.

Zach presents a counterpoint, arguing that wholesaling remains legally protected under constitutional contract law and that regulatory changes primarily restrict sloppy or predatory practices. He maintains that off-market direct-to-seller wholesaling continues to operate smoothly across all major markets when proper assignment contracts and double closes are used.

The reality of creative finance and buyer appetites

Richard and Max discuss the declining appetite for low-interest "subject-to" acquisitions that lack immediate cash flow. During previous years, investors bought properties with low interest rates but slim margins, hoping to increase revenue through pad splits, mid-term rentals, or wraps. As operational costs rose, many of these operators faced default.

Today, cash buyers and institutional funds primarily seek cash-flowing properties that offer reliable returns without extensive turnaround work. Richard notes that 50–60% of his community's successful dispositions involve inexpensive Midwestern duplexes and triplexes that produce 20% cash-on-cash returns, while fix-and-flip opportunities make up roughly 40% of their transaction volume.
