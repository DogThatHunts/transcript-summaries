The retail warning: Walmart, Home Depot, and Lowe's

Walmart's recent earnings report shocked Wall Street by posting its worst sales growth in six years. While sales did not contract outright, the sharp deceleration in growth indicates that consumers are pulling back. Steve Van Metre highlights a critical, overlooked detail: Walmart is using its tariff refund checks to double the number of products on which it is rolling back prices, rather than using the funds to pad corporate profits. This move indicates that even the lowest-priced retail giant recognizes its customer base can no longer sustain current price levels.

This retail distress is echoed by Home Depot and Lowe's. Both companies reported that big-ticket purchases and major home renovation projects have virtually vanished as consumers refuse to take on new debt. Instead, consumers are "trading down" to minor, cash-based purchases like paint and plants to spruce up their existing homes. Jeff Snyder points out that this behavior represents a classic "lipstick effect"—consumers still want to spend, but they only have the budget for small, inexpensive items.

The deteriorating labor market and the "forgot how to grow" economy

Snyder describes the current economic state as an "I-shaped" or "forgot how to grow" economy, which was already highly fragile before the recent energy price shocks. While mainstream commentators point to a resilient labor market, Snyder notes that the household survey paints a far more pessimistic picture, showing a loss of over 900,000 jobs and a steady decline in full-time employment since January.

Van Metre explains how this labor weakness manifests in the real economy. As retail sales slow, store managers quietly cut employee hours, asking workers to go home early. This reduction in hours worked directly impacts aggregate real incomes, creating a self-reinforcing downward spiral. While a segment of the population remains insulated and prosperous, an increasing majority of consumers are falling further behind as their inflation-adjusted incomes shrink.

Housing and industrial production confirm systemic weakness

According to Van Metre, the housing market serves as a major leading indicator of this economic rollover. Pending home sales have dropped sharply for consecutive months, and housing starts have fallen to near five-year lows. While mainstream analysts blame high mortgage rates, Snyder argues that the housing slowdown is fundamentally driven by deteriorating job security and falling real incomes.

This lack of consumer demand is further validated by industrial production data. Manufacturers of consumer goods have steadily cut back their output over the last year. Because producers see that raising prices causes sales volumes to disappear, they are actively reducing production. This alignment between retail warnings, housing data, and manufacturing output suggests that the economic slowdown is widespread and systemic.

Bond market signals and the AI stock bubble

Snyder and Van Metre argue that the stock market is currently "high on its own AI supply," ignoring macroeconomic risks due to speculation surrounding artificial intelligence and data center construction. However, the bond market tells a very different story. The yield curve remains historically flat, a signal that historically precedes severe economic downturns and recessions rather than inflation.

While some commentators view the 30-year Treasury yield hitting a multi-year high as a sign of reignited inflation or market rejection of government debt, Snyder clarifies that the long bond yield is only rising gently. This movement represents the yield curve attempting to steepen from an incredibly flat position, reflecting the market's expectation of future economic weakness and eventual interest rate cuts. As macroeconomic risks continue to mount, credit markets are likely to tighten, potentially popping both the private credit and AI debt bubbles.
