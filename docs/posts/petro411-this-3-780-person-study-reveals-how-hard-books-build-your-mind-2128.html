The illusion of fluency vs. reading skill

Many people believe that reading a high volume of books is the key to increasing intelligence. However, cognitive research suggests that reading more does almost nothing for intelligence if the material is too easy. Easy, fluent reading creates an "illusion of fluency"—a psychological trap where smooth reading fools the reader into thinking they have mastered the material. To actually build cognitive capacity, readers must engage in "desirable difficulties," a term coined by memory researcher Robert Bjork to describe learning conditions that feel challenging in the moment but yield deeper, more durable retention.

The 2015 twin study: how reading skill drives intelligence

A landmark 2015 study by Stuart Ritchie, Timothy Bates, and Robert Plomin tracked 1,890 pairs of identical twins (nearly 3,800 children) between the ages of 7 and 16. Because identical twins share 100% of their DNA and grew up in the same household, genetic and environmental variables were held constant.

  • The findings: The twin who developed better reading skill pulled ahead in intelligence later in life.
  • Quantity vs. quality: The sheer volume of reading did not independently predict intelligence gains. Instead, the development of reading skill through increasingly difficult material drove the cognitive improvements.
  • Abstract reasoning: The cognitive gains were not limited to vocabulary; they also appeared in non-verbal, abstract reasoning—an area of intelligence traditionally thought to be fixed.

The neuroscience of deep reading

Cognitive neuroscientist Marianne Wolf notes that humans have no innate "reading gene" or dedicated reading region in the brain. Every time a person learns to read, the brain must physically rewire itself. The "deep reading circuit"—the slow, effortful machinery of inference and reflection—is only constructed when readers push past comfortable, automatic reading and engage with text that causes active friction and cognitive discomfort.

Four techniques to build your mind

To maximize the cognitive benefits of reading, Petro411 outlines four evidence-based techniques:

1. engage in desirable difficulty

Choose books that are slightly above your current reading level. The ideal material should cause enough friction that you must regularly pause to digest, absorb, and process the ideas.

2. interrogate the text

Actively engage with the material by asking two core questions: * Why is this true? * How does this connect to what I already know?

These practices, known in cognitive psychology as elaborative interrogation and self-explanation, help build "crystallized intelligence" by integrating new facts into an existing web of knowledge.

3. retrieve, don't re-read

A 2013 review of learning techniques by Dunlosky et al. categorized re-reading as a "low utility" strategy. While re-reading boosts confidence, it does not improve long-term retention. Instead, practice active retrieval: close the book after a chapter and force yourself to summarize the arguments in your own words.

This effort feeds into what William Dickens and James Flynn call the "multiplier effect"—a process where small cognitive gains compound over time, leading individuals to actively seek out more demanding material (niche-picking).

4. read for the mind behind the text

Deep reading requires constant inference. To build non-verbal reasoning, read like a detective by analyzing what the author is claiming, what they are quietly assuming, and how they construct their arguments.

Schooling as a cognitive driver

The link between environmental stimulation and intelligence is supported by broader educational research:

  • The schooling effect: A 2018 meta-analysis by Stuart Ritchie and Elliot Tucker-Drob found that each additional year of education causally increases IQ by 1 to 5 points.
  • Twin education differences: A 2025 re-analysis of historical twin data revealed that identical twins with similar schooling differed by fewer than 6 IQ points. However, identical twins who received vastly different educations differed by around 15 IQ points—nearly as much as complete strangers.
