The expedition and exile

De Cordier began as a conceptual artist working with radio telescopes and cosmic waves before becoming fascinated by ancient Egypt and lost civilizations. He was drawn to the labyrinth specifically because, unlike the pyramids—which contain no written information—ancient authors described the labyrinth as a vast stone library with over 3,000 rooms filled with hieroglyphic records.

In 2008, he organized the Mataha expedition in cooperation with Ghent University and Egypt's National Research Institute for Astronomy and Geophysics. The team conducted a non-invasive geophysical survey using seven or eight different scanning technologies. At the time, this was the largest such survey ever performed on an Egyptian archaeological site. The expedition successfully detected a massive foundation layer at five meters below ground—matching descriptions by Herodotus and other ancient authors—and found evidence of hundreds of rooms and enormous walls beneath it.

The research was initially well-received. De Cordier presented findings at a packed conference in Ghent attended by 500–600 people, with major media outlets (ABC, NBC, European press) covering the discovery. UNESCO representatives expressed interest in making the site a World Heritage site. However, days after the conference, the National Research Institute called and demanded the research be pulled from publication and all websites. De Cordier was told he had been placed on a blacklist and could not enter Egypt.

De Cordier attributes the reversal to political sensitivity. The National Research Institute is part of Egypt's military complex, and the discovery was classified as a national security breach. The situation escalated when the Belgian king visited Egypt on a diplomatic mission and showed Mubarak's wife a magazine featuring the "Belgian discovery" of the labyrinth. She reportedly called Zahi Hawass, Egypt's most prominent archaeologist, and the situation "went ballistic." De Cordier notes that the labyrinth's connection to lost civilizations and "giant halls of records" moved it beyond conventional archaeology into territory that authorities found threatening.

The site and ancient accounts

The labyrinth is located at Hawara in the Fayoum region, approximately 100 kilometers south of Cairo. Ancient Greek authors—Herodotus, Strabo, and others—described it as vastly larger and more impressive than the pyramids themselves. Herodotus claimed it contained over 3,000 rooms and was so enormous that if you compiled all the temples of ancient Greece, they still would not match its magnitude. No recorded explorer in history successfully penetrated its deeper levels.

Flinders Petrie, the father of modern archaeology, conducted the last major investigation in 1888. He discovered a massive stone foundation layer at five meters depth (304 × 244 meters) and attempted to explore deeper passages, wading through salty water up to his chest in pitch-black conditions. However, he eventually concluded from "scanty remains" that he could not draw proper conclusions. Afterward, academia dismissed the labyrinth's existence entirely, declaring it either a myth or a completely quarried-out structure.

A significant obstacle to exploration has been the Mahmoudieh Canal, built in 1820 by Linon de Belafont (the engineer who also built the Suez Canal) on orders following Napoleon's visit. The canal was diverted directly across the site, flooding the pyramid and surrounding area with groundwater. De Cordier argues this may have been intentional—an archaeological mission that simultaneously submerged the site's entrance, making deeper exploration impossible for over a century.

The geophysical findings

The 2008 Mataha expedition confirmed Flinders Petrie's foundation layer and detected evidence of enormous underground structures. According to De Cordier, the geophysical data showed:

  • A massive foundation layer at approximately 5 meters depth
  • Hundreds of rooms and enormous walls beneath that layer
  • Bedrock ("mother rock") beginning at approximately 18 meters depth
  • Carved-out spaces in solid bedrock extending to depths of 30, 60, 80, and 100+ meters

Satellite scans conducted in 2014 and 2015 by researchers including those from University College London provided even more detailed imagery. These scans revealed:

  • Four distinct underground layers at depths of 30, 60, 80, and 100 meters
  • A central atrium approximately 40 × 40 meters in size, described as resembling a shopping mall in scale
  • Enormous carved-out spaces in bedrock, with one measuring approximately 100 × 40 meters
  • A mysterious metallic object approximately 40 meters in length, positioned centrally within the atrium
  • An omega-shaped moat surrounding the structure
  • Persian tomb shafts (30–40 meters deep) and other features

De Cordier notes that the satellite scan data was subject to non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) for over a decade, partly due to concerns about how the Egyptian government and media would react to public disclosure. The metallic object detected in the scans remains unexplained; the satellite expert speculated it could be electrum (an ancient gold-silver alloy) or orichalcum (the legendary metal of Atlantis described by Plato), though its true composition and purpose remain unknown.

Recent excavations and current status

In March 2024, De Cordier learned that the Egyptian government had begun excavating the site. The Fayoum inspectorate launched what appears to be a properly conducted archaeological mission, draining the site and exposing megalithic walls and giant stone structures at the upper levels. According to interviews with officials, they stated they would excavate "as deep as they are able to go."

De Cordier has spent recent months in Egypt coordinating with government officials, the Supreme Council of Antiquities, and academics to develop a master preservation plan. However, the government's excavation began before this plan was finalized, suggesting either genuine interest in exploration or, as De Cordier cautiously notes, a possibility of "planting a flag" and later claiming nothing significant was found.

The excavations remain relatively shallow (only a few meters deep as of the interview), and the truly significant findings, according to the satellite data, begin around 20 meters depth where the bedrock structures become apparent. De Cordier is advocating for the construction of a sophisticated field research station—a "moon base" design with autonomous power, air filtration, and drilling capabilities—to enable non-invasive exploration using nano-robots, laser scanning, and boreholes rather than destructive excavation.

Theories on purpose and age

De Cordier argues that the labyrinth was designed as an ancient library or knowledge repository, comparable to the Library of Alexandria but far more extensive and protected. Ancient authors consistently described it as a "hall of records" containing the accumulated knowledge of ancient civilizations. Some researchers have compared it to a "hard drive" while the Library of Alexandria was the "software."

Regarding age, De Cordier notes that conventional Egyptology attributes the labyrinth to Amenemhat III (12th Dynasty, circa 1860 BCE), but this dating is based on limited evidence. The carved-out bedrock structures, which show no signs of degradation, could potentially be far older—possibly tens of thousands of years old. De Cordier suggests the labyrinth may be a remnant of a sophisticated pre-dynastic or even pre-ice-age civilization.

He connects this to broader theories about cyclical cataclysms driven by solar activity and cosmic radiation. According to these theories, advanced civilizations periodically experience extinction-level events (solar nova events, younger dryas-type catastrophes), and survivors rebuild. The labyrinth, in this view, was designed as a time capsule—a backup of human knowledge to survive such events, similar to De Cordier's own Biblioteca del Sol (a high-altitude library in Spain built to preserve knowledge in case of solar storms or electromagnetic civilization collapse).

Solar cycles, climate, and cataclysm

De Cordier discusses theories linking solar activity to climate cycles and extinction events. Key points include:

  • Grand solar minima: Cycles of reduced solar activity occurring roughly every 200 years, with larger cycles every 400, 800, 2,000+ years. These correlate with climate change, crop failures, and historical upheavals (e.g., the Maunder Minimum and Little Ice Age; the Dalton Minimum preceding the French Revolution).

  • Cosmic radiation and clouds: Solar physicist Henrik Svensmark's research (confirmed by CERN's CLOUD project) shows cosmic rays create clouds, cooling the Earth. During grand solar minima, reduced solar activity allows more cosmic radiation to reach Earth, increasing cloud cover and cooling.

  • Earth expansion and primary water: Some theories propose the Earth is expanding due to electromagnetic energy influx, and water is continuously created within the Earth's crust (primary water) rather than only cycling atmospherically. This could explain why oil and water reserves replenish and why ancient oceans on Mars were vastly larger than Earth's current oceans.

  • Solar nova events and younger dryas: De Cordier suggests the Younger Dryas (12,800 years ago) may have been triggered by a solar event rather than solely by meteorite impact. Such events would cause massive electromagnetic disturbances, atmospheric loss, volcanic activity, and flash-freezing of megafauna.

  • The "black sun" prophecy: Ancient texts describing a black sun may refer to a solar nova event—a period when the sun becomes covered in sunspots and undergoes a regenerative outburst, potentially resetting civilizations.

De Cordier notes that we are currently entering a new grand solar minimum (the Eddy Minimum), with increased cosmic dust in the heliosphere, a weakening magnetosphere, and growing geomagnetic anomalies (e.g., the South Atlantic Anomaly). This cyclical pattern suggests humanity may be approaching another period of significant climate and geological change.
