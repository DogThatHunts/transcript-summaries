The quiet retreat: HSBC and major banks pull back leverage

According to reporting from the Financial Times, HSBC has begun informing select private credit borrowers that it will not renew their credit facilities, while pulling back crucial "back leverage" from higher-risk private debt funds. While private credit has long marketed itself as an independent alternative that replaced traditional bank lending following the Great Financial Crisis, Jeff Snider notes that banks never truly vacated the space; they simply stepped back one layer.

Private credit funds rely heavily on commercial banks for warehouse lines, liquidity backstops, and leverage to turn mid-single-digit loan returns into double-digit fund yields. HSBC’s retreat signals that the fees generated by providing this balance-sheet support no longer compensate for the underlying credit and collateral risk. Snider emphasizes that HSBC is not reacting to an isolated default, but rather making a wholesale risk calculation after recent corporate distress exposed deteriorating underwriting standards across private markets.

How back leverage turns asset-side marks into systemic risk

Back leverage allows a private debt fund to pledge its corporate loan portfolio as collateral to borrow fresh capital from a commercial bank, compounding its deployable assets. While this amplifies returns during an economic expansion, it becomes a structural trap during a downswing. Fund managers must not only monitor whether underlying corporate borrowers can service their debt, but also whether prime lending banks continue to trust the valuation of those loans.

Private credit typically avoids day-to-day volatility by valuing illiquid assets using internal models, periodic appraisal marks, and selective payment-in-kind (PIK) structures rather than active secondary market pricing. However, smooth marks only hold as long as funding counterparties accept them. Reports indicate that institutions including JPMorgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, and Barclays have exercised contract rights to mark down individual loans pledged as collateral. When a bank re-marks a pledged loan downward, the fund faces immediate collateral calls, forcing managers to swap in higher-quality assets, sell holdings into an illiquid market, or dilute returns. Snider observes that when banks look directly at private credit loan books and reject their collateral valuations, the funding layer has effectively confirmed a turn in the credit cycle.

The split screen: why public credit spreads remain dangerously complacent

The market currently displays a stark divergence: insiders with balance-sheet exposure to loan books are quietly running for the exits, yet public high-yield credit spreads remain near historical lows. Under normal market conditions, narrowing spreads indicate economic stability and low default expectations. In the current environment, however, Snider argues this price signal is fundamentally broken.

Citing analysis by market strategist Michael Green, Snider attributes tight junk spreads to programmatic passive investing flows rather than fundamental credit health. Inflows into equity market benchmarks and fixed-allocation retirement vehicles mechanically deploy capital across corporate debt funds. Portfolio managers competing for yield allocate this cash into high-yield paper regardless of fundamental underlying solvency risks. As long as passive allocations persist, public junk spreads project artificial stability, creating a false sense of security that masks tightening conditions in primary bank funding.

The late-cycle trap: collateral stress precedes the default wave

Credit downturns historically begin not with surging corporate bankruptcies or high-profile headlines, but with quiet liquidity freezes inside bank funding facilities. Lenders adjust terms, reduce commitment sizes, and haircuts increase long before default rates peak.

Snider cautions that retail investors often enter credit cycles at the worst possible moment, drawn in by elevated yields after rate-hiking campaigns. Investors often confuse income with safety, buying high nominal coupons while ignoring severe tail risks. Because private and public corporate borrowers operate in the same real economy—facing matching pressures from customer demand, refinancing costs, and margin compression—liquidity contraction within shadow banking eventually cascades into broader capital markets. When market participants eventually recognize that underlying collateral values have decayed, the resulting spread widening can rapidly wipe out years of accumulated coupon income.
