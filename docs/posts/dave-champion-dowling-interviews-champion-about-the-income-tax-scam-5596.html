The three statutory classes under Dave Champion's reading of tax law

Dave Champion asserts that despite the Internal Revenue Code being amended numerous times since the modern income tax was enacted in 1913, the foundational statutory scope of who is subject to the tax has never expanded. In Dave Champion's view, ordinary American citizens earning domestic income within the states of the Union do not fall under any statutory imposition of the income tax.

Instead, Dave Champion identifies three narrow statutory categories: 1. Nonresident aliens receiving income derived from U.S. sources. 2. Foreign corporations deriving income from U.S. sources. 3. U.S. citizens residing abroad who earn foreign-sourced income.

Dave Champion claims that Treasury decisions regarding Form 1040 historically targeted nonresident aliens or their domestic agents. He argues that the government relies on public ignorance and fear to induce voluntary compliance from the roughly 100 million individuals who submit Form 1040 each year.

Disinformation, fear, and the evolution of the tax honesty movement

Addressing the history of the tax honesty movement, Dave Champion recalls that activism was robust through the late 1990s and early 2000s before collapsing around the 2008 economic crisis, when financial distress made individuals less willing to risk disputes with authorities. Dave Champion argues that the movement is now positioned for greater success because many early legal theories and internet "gurus" with flawed arguments have been filtered out.

Dave Champion characterizes the official narrative surrounding income tax compliance as a highly effective government disinformation campaign that enlists accountants and corporate payroll departments as unwitting enforcement mechanisms. He claims that although he personally stopped filing tax returns and paying federal income taxes over three decades ago, federal prosecutors avoid taking fully informed non-filers to trial because doing so would risk exposing statutory definitions in open court.

Reinterpreting Taxpayer Identification numbers and information returns

A substantial portion of the discussion examines administrative tax documents, notably the Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN), Form W-9, and Form 1099. Dave Champion argues that a TIN—whether an Employer Identification Number (EIN) or a Social Security Number (SSN)—is legally required only when an individual participates in activities linked to the three statutory classes he outlines.

When opening bank accounts or conducting domestic business, Dave Champion explains that commercial institutions frequently demand TINs due to automated banking software and regulatory compliance mandates. Dave Champion describes providing an SSN to complete account creation and subsequently sending formal legal notices to the bank's corporate legal department stating the number was furnished under administrative duress and cannot be utilized as a tax reporting number.

Similarly, Dave Champion argues that Form W-9 is technically a "withholding certificate" that designates the signer as an intermediary handling U.S.-sourced income flowing to a foreign principal. He contends that many criminal prosecutions involving non-filers arise because commercial payers submit 1099s that the government treats as foreign-source pipelines, leaving defendants and their attorneys confused about the underlying legal theories being used against them.
