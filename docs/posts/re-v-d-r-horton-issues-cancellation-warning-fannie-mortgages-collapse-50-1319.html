Spikes in builder inventory mirror historic recessions

To combat slowing demand, both builders have implemented significant price cuts. Despite these adjustments, inventory continues to pile up. Historically, builder inventory has only reached these levels during severe economic downturns, specifically the 1974 recession, the 1981 double-dip recession, and the 2008 financial crisis.

This is a dramatic increase from a decade ago, when the builder financed only about 50% of its transactions.

To keep buyers qualified, the builder has shifted away from conventional Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac loans toward riskier government-backed financing. Conventional loans dropped from 58% of D.R. Rising FHA delinquency rates signal broader economic risk

The shift toward FHA lending introduces substantial risk, as these borrowers historically experience much higher default rates. Nationally, the FHA delinquency rate has climbed to 11.88% as of the first quarter of 2026, the highest level since 2011–2012 (excluding the initial pandemic lockdown spike). This rate is nearly five times higher than the delinquency rate for conventional loans.

Furthermore, data from the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) indicates that the overall FHA delinquency rate reached 13.7% in mid-2026, with purchase mortgages seeing a 15.2% delinquency rate. This distress is occurring despite a seemingly strong economy with a 4.2% unemployment rate. If unemployment rises to a historically normal 6%, these delinquency rates could climb significantly higher.

Compounding this risk is the high debt-to-income (DTI) ratio among government-backed borrowers. The average back-end DTI for FHA, VA, and USDA loans reached 44% in late 2025. This exceeds the 39% average DTI seen at the peak of the 2006–2007 housing bubble, raising concerns that these loans are taking on subprime characteristics.
