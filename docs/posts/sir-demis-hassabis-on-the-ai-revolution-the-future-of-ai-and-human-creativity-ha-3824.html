Sir Demis Hassabis on the AI Revolution, the Future of AI and Human Creativity

Summary

In this RSA lecture, DeepMind founder Demis Hassabis receives the Albert Medal for his groundbreaking work on artificial intelligence, particularly AlphaFold, which has solved the protein-folding problem and accelerated drug discovery. Introduced by David Joseph (RSA CEO) and Hannah Fry (mathematician and broadcaster), Hassabis discusses his unconventional path from chess prodigy and video game designer to neuroscientist to AI researcher, arguing that creativity and scientific rigour are inseparable. He positions AI as potentially the most transformative technology in human history—comparable to electricity or the internet but unfolding ten times faster—and emphasizes that its impact depends entirely on how society chooses to deploy it. The conversation ranges from the future of human creativity in an age of AI to education reform, urban design, and the philosophical questions that will define the next era of human flourishing.

Five key insights

  1. Creativity and science are not opposites. Hassabis argues that the greatest scientists are distinguished by creative intuition and leaps of imagination, not just technical brilliance. His own career—spanning chess, game design, neuroscience, and AI—reflects a unified pursuit of understanding intelligence through multiple angles.

  2. AI is inevitable; its direction is not. Hassabis insists that AI development cannot be stopped, particularly given competition from China and the US. The real question is not whether AI will advance, but whether society will intentionally shape it toward beneficial outcomes or allow market forces and geopolitics to determine its path.

  3. Human connection and craft will remain valuable. Even if machines eventually match human creative output, the human story behind a work—the effort, pain, and intention—will retain meaning. Hassabis uses the example of "based on a true story" to argue that audiences value knowing another human being experienced something, a connection machines cannot replicate.

  4. Education must be radically reimagined. Rather than resist AI in schools, Hassabis proposes inverting the classroom: use AI for personalized rote learning outside class, freeing teachers and students to focus on project-based, collaborative, creative work in person—closer to Montessori or Oxbridge supervision models.

  5. The next decade will determine the next century. Hassabis frames the 2020s as a critical juncture where arts, humanities, and social sciences must join technologists in defining what society values and how AI should be deployed. Without this interdisciplinary dialogue, the technology will be shaped by commercial and geopolitical forces alone.

The path to DeepMind: creativity as a Trojan horse

Hassabis explains that his early career in video games was not a detour but a deliberate strategy. In the 1990s, when AI research was unfundable, he used game design as "a Trojan horse" to sneak in AI research with collaborators like Dave Silver. Games allowed him to explore creative objectives (when will this be fun?) alongside technical challenges—a parallel he later recognized in scientific research (when will this breakthrough happen?). Both require taste, intuition, and faith in an uncertain outcome. This experience taught him how to blend cutting-edge engineering with open-ended creative goals, a lesson he carried to DeepMind.

His PhD in neuroscience, studying imagination and memory, was another angle on the same question: how does intelligence work? By the early 2000s, he felt he had pushed AI knowledge as far as it could go using only computer science. Neuroscience offered an "orthogonal source of information" about thinking, planning, and reasoning. He was fascinated by imagination specifically because it was fundamental to creativity and planning—something he had experienced viscerally as a chess player and game designer—yet almost no one had studied it, and AI had no way to do it.

On AlphaFold and the Albert Medal

David Joseph frames Hassabis's achievement in simple terms: proteins are tiny machines whose function depends entirely on their shape. For decades, determining a single protein's shape could take a scientist years or a lifetime. AlphaFold has now solved the structures of 262 million proteins and made them freely available to every researcher on Earth. Joseph draws a parallel to Roland Hill's penny postage reform: both are creative acts for the common good, solving old problems by looking at them differently.

Hassabis accepts the medal with characteristic humility, noting that his family's creative background (his father is a singer-songwriter; his sister is a professional musician and composer) gave him permission to be unconventional. He emphasizes that his formal training was scientific and logical, but he thinks of himself as "led creatively, intuitively, and emotionally"—a combination he believes separates good scientists from great ones.

AI as a revolutionary technology

Hassabis argues that AI will be at least as transformative as electricity, the telephone, the internet, and mobile technology combined—perhaps ten times the impact of the Industrial Revolution, but unfolding in a decade instead of a century. Like industrialization, AI will bring immense benefits (advances in medicine, solving diseases, new materials, clean energy) but also challenges that will require new institutions and approaches.

His primary motivation for working on AI has always been to advance science and medicine. He sees AI as "the most amazing tool for advancing our understanding of the world around us" and dreams of using it to solve fundamental questions about consciousness, creativity, dreaming, and time. However, he stresses that technology only enables possibilities; it does not define what society should do with those possibilities. That is a question for the arts, humanities, and social sciences—represented by institutions like the RSA.

The singularity and the need for philosophical debate

Hassabis describes himself as standing in "the foothills of the singularity," a few years away from artificial general intelligence (AGI). He argues that society is not yet ready for what is coming and that the debate about what to value, what constitutes purpose, and how to enhance human creativity rather than replace it must begin now. These are philosophical problems, not technical ones, and technologists are "not best placed to answer" them.

He calls for a convening of sciences and arts to define the next era, emphasizing that places like the RSA are uniquely positioned to facilitate this interdisciplinary dialogue. The questions are profound: What will it mean to be human? What will we create and value? How do we distinguish between craft and the soul of a creative piece in an age of AI?

AI and human creativity

Hassabis has worked with top artists—including filmmaker Darren Aronofsky and game designers—to develop AI tools that enhance rather than replace creativity. He observes that AI can be used for both "AI slop" (creatively mundane content designed to hook audiences briefly) and genuine creative enhancement. The best uses he has seen involve prototyping: artists can now iterate through many more ideas quickly, testing them before committing resources, or undertaking projects they could not otherwise afford.

For emerging artists, AI tools could accelerate breakthroughs into the professional world—replacing pitch decks with actual prototypes or trailers. However, Hassabis acknowledges that these tools will reshape what society values. He predicts two phases: the next decade will see an "incredible flourishing of creativity" as top artists use AI to amplify their output tenfold. After that, as tools become more autonomous, the question of what humans uniquely contribute will become urgent. He suspects that human craft, effort, and the human connection behind a work will become more valued, not less—just as we still prize original paintings over perfect copies.

The future of human identity and purpose

Hassabis notes that humans have long defined themselves by their ability to solve complex problems and create. As machines get better at these pursuits, society will need to rethink what it means to be human. He is optimistic that this reckoning will lead to a deeper appreciation of human creativity, connection, and purpose—but only if society is intentional about it.

He envisions a best-case future: AI tools carefully stewarded to advance science and medicine, solving diseases and environmental challenges, leading to an era of abundance. This abundance would unlock space travel, new forms of art, and "maximum human flourishing." However, the biggest challenges will not be technical but economic and philosophical: how to ensure everyone shares in the benefits, and how to define what we value and want to become.

London, ambition, and the role of institutions

Responding to architect Thomas Heatherwick's question about the built environment, Hassabis argues that the AI revolution offers an opportunity to rethink urban design and public health. He notes that beautiful architecture (Art Deco, Georgian) stopped being built after World War II, and that cost has become the only measure of what gets built. In an era of potential abundance, society should factor in human inspiration and health. He suggests that new infrastructure for AI (data centers, etc.) should be designed beautifully, not merely functionally.

He also defends his decision to keep DeepMind based in London rather than moving to California. He praises London as the world's most multidisciplinary city, where experts in science, architecture, art, finance, and government can meet in one place. He calls on the UK to embrace American-style ambition and optimism while maintaining European groundedness and compassion. Institutions like the RSA, Royal Society, and Royal Academy of Engineering have unique convening power and should use it to shape the AI revolution.

Education and the next generation

In response to a question from Usha Haley (independent member of the House of Lords), Hassabis proposes a radical reimagining of education. He argues that the current system caters to the median, leaving both gifted and struggling students underserved. Rather than resist AI in schools, he recommends inverting the classroom: use AI tools for personalized rote learning outside class (algebra, facts, etc.), freeing classroom time for project-based, collaborative, creative work—closer to Montessori schools or Oxbridge supervision.

This approach would allow every student to have a personalized tutor, eliminate redundant lesson planning by teachers, and focus human interaction on the skills that matter most for the future: creativity, entrepreneurialism, teamwork, and leadership. Hassabis acknowledges this is radical but necessary if the next generation is to be equipped to shape AI's future.

Da Vinci and the renaissance of science and art

Hassabis's office at DeepMind was named after Leonardo da Vinci, whom he cites as one of his scientific heroes alongside Aristotle, for their "all-roundedness." Da Vinci's ability to excel in both art and science—painting the Mona Lisa while producing anatomically precise medical drawings—fascinates Hassabis because it shows that artistic and scientific excellence are mutually reinforcing. He hopes AI will help usher in a new renaissance, a "coming together of science and the arts" that has not happened since the original Renaissance, potentially unleashing "a new golden era of scientific discovery and wonder."

On optimism and uncertainty

Hassabis emphasizes that the future is not written. No technologist truly knows what will happen, and anyone claiming certainty is either lying or has ulterior motives. However, he is optimistic because he believes in "the power of human ingenuity." He marvels at what humans have already created—from transatlantic flight to art and civilization—using brains evolved for hunter-gathering. If humanity managed that, it can adapt to and shape the AI era.

He urges everyone to move beyond focusing only on risks and negatives. Yes, things could go wrong, but they might not. The outcome is "all up for grabs," and society needs more people in "the optimistic kind of space" to ensure it goes well. This is not naïveté but a call to action: the future depends on intentional choices made now, by people across all disciplines.
