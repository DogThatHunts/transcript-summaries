BlackRock Insider Reveals What’s REALLY Happening in Private Credit

A deep dive into the opaque world of private credit, its systemic risks, and how the artificial intelligence boom is acting as the market's final credit impulse.

In this conversation, financial analyst Ed Dowd joins Jeff Snider of Eurodollar University to discuss the hidden vulnerabilities in the rapidly expanding private credit market. Dowd argues that private credit has effectively become the new junk bond market—highly leveraged, illiquid, and opaque—masking deteriorating credit quality because assets are not publicly marked to market. The two discuss how the credit loss cycle is beginning to shift, the concentration of marginal credit in the artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure buildout, and the broader macroeconomic factors, such as immigration and government spending, that have temporarily floated a fragile real economy.

Key insights

  • Private credit is the new junk bond market: Dowd explains that riskier, lower-quality credits have migrated from the public high-yield bond market into the opaque, illiquid private credit space, hiding widening spreads from public view.
  • Lack of mark-to-market pricing masks defaults: Unlike public markets where deteriorating credit declines gradually, private credit assets are often held at par value until they abruptly drop to zero, creating sudden and unexpected portfolio shocks.
  • The AI boom is the final credit impulse: The massive capital expenditure on AI data centers and semiconductors represents a late-cycle reach for yield and growth, which Dowd warns is creating a commodity-like oversupply with poor return on investment (ROI).
  • Contagion risks are rising beneath the surface: Although major indices remain near all-time highs, Dowd's contacts in fixed-income hedge funds report highly unusual "six to twelve standard deviation events" occurring weekly beneath the surface.
  • Macroeconomic indicators are heavily distorted: Dowd argues that official US employment data has been revised downward significantly, and that massive immigration and associated government spending have artificially propped up an otherwise weak real economy.

The migration of junk debt to private credit

Dowd explains that the public junk bond market appears to be of higher quality today because the riskiest borrowers have shifted to private credit. This transition has created a highly opaque environment where fund managers mark their own books, hiding the true extent of credit deterioration. Snider notes that this asymmetry leads to industry pushback claiming "everything is fine" because there has not been a public Lehman-style event. However, Dowd points out that when a loan fails in private credit, it does not decline gradually in public view; instead, it goes from being marked at 100 cents on the dollar to zero overnight, as recently seen in certain large institutional portfolios.

The credit loss cycle and systemic contagion

According to Dowd, major fixed-income managers like PIMCO are signaling that the credit loss cycle is starting to turn. When private credit flows reverse, it quietly freezes credit creation across other sectors, including consumer, commercial, and real estate loans. Snider and Dowd agree that the banking system has largely stopped lending to anyone other than private credit and private equity funds, making these non-bank entities the primary marginal credit engine of the economy. If this engine stalls due to rising redemptions and a lack of new inflows, a severe credit crunch will ripple through the broader financial system.

The AI bubble as a late-stage credit impulse

Dowd compares the current enthusiasm for artificial intelligence to the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s. He argues that hyperscalers and Fortune 500 companies are aggressively spending capital on AI infrastructure out of fear of being left behind, despite a lack of clear return on investment (ROI). This massive capital expenditure has temporarily benefited semiconductor and memory manufacturers, driving up their margins to unsustainable levels. Dowd expects a sharp correction as corporations realize the high costs of running these models do not justify the returns, leading to a pullback in spending that will severely impact both equity and private credit portfolios heavily exposed to the sector.

Macroeconomic distortions and the real economy

The conversation turns to why widely predicted recessions have not materialized in official GDP figures. Dowd asserts that his economic models were not wrong about the underlying weakness of the economy, but rather that the system was temporarily floated by unprecedented levels of immigration and government spending. He argues that the US added millions of immigrants in recent years, supported by significant federal spending that went directly into the real economy, keeping consumer demand and home prices artificially high. As these temporary stimulants fade, Dowd warns that rising auto loan and consumer delinquencies, alongside emerging foreclosures, will expose the true fragility of the real economy.
