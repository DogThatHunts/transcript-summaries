"China Is COLLAPSING And It's Much Worse Than You Think..." | Jim Rickards

A warning about China's economic fragility and the U.S. debt crisis, based on a war game simulation and economic analysis.

China's financial infrastructure and the myth of a gold standard

Jim Rickards argues that China lacks the foundational elements of a credible global reserve currency. He states that China has no significant buy market, no banking infrastructure, and no rule of law. He emphasizes that 'you can't trust the Chinese as far as you can throw them.'

Rickards references a 2009 Pentagon war game in which he participated as part of a China team. In the simulation, his team proposed a new gold-backed currency, backed by gold stored in Switzerland and issued by a London bank under English law. The plan included requiring foreign buyers to pay in this new currency and allowing gold deposits to generate currency. The white team (referees) initially ruled this move illegal, but Rickards argued that in a real war, there are no illegal moves. They agreed to let it proceed.

Over two days, the scenario gained momentum. Russia, which participated, tripled its gold reserves. By the latest data, the dollar value of Russia's gold exceeded the value of its treasury securities, and it now holds 20% of its reserves in gold. China has also tripled its gold reserves, indicating a move toward a gold-backed world economy—though Rickards notes this was all part of a hypothetical war game.

He asserts that China's monetary system is over-leveraged and investment-driven, with investment making up 40–45% of its economy compared to the U.S. at 25%. He visited ghost cities in China—seven such cities with only one or two skyscrapers, retail, golf courses, and empty land—where he observed that the buildings are abandoned and deteriorating. He claims that under generally accepted accounting principles, such structures would be written off as having no value.

Rickards draws a parallel to Zaire (now the Congo), where post-1970s skyscrapers were built but never used, with windows falling out and elevators broken. He concludes that China is wasting money through overinvestment in underpopulated, unoccupied infrastructure.

The yuan Is not a global reserve currency

Rickards explains that a reserve currency is not defined by its currency value but by the health of its bond market. Key requirements include liquid bond markets, dealers, auctions, payment systems, repo agreements, futures, options, and a rule of law. China lacks all of these.

He notes that China holds $1.4 trillion in reserves, but these are not in physical dollar bills. Instead, they are held in dollar-denominated securities. Thus, China does not possess a functioning reserve currency system.

He contrasts this with the U.S. dollar, which holds 60% of global reserves, with 25% in euros, making the U.S.-euro exchange rate the dominant global benchmark. China, he says, is a '1%' player at the bottom of the reserve currency hierarchy.

The U.S. stimulus and the failure of monetary policy

Rickards criticizes the U.S. fiscal response to the pandemic. He states that the Fed printed $4 trillion in 2020 and 2021, with an additional $1 trillion in 2024 and a proposed $2 trillion stimulus by Biden, bringing total deficit spending to $8 trillion over two years.

He argues that money printing does not cause inflation. Instead, inflation is driven by velocity—the turnover of money. He notes that velocity has been declining since 1998, dropping faster during the 2008 crisis and accelerating today.

He explains that nominal GDP equals money supply multiplied by velocity. If velocity is zero, then nominal GDP is zero. Thus, massive money printing without rising velocity has no stimulative effect.

He asserts that the U.S. economy is not recovering to 2019 levels. Output is expected to decline by 6–7%, and full job recovery will not occur until at least 2025.

Rickards also challenges the idea that stimulus checks will stimulate spending. He notes that people are not spending the checks—they are saving them. This is due to precautionary savings, especially in uncertain economic times. He cites behavioral economics: people save when they fear job loss or economic collapse.

He references research showing that the Keynesian multiplier—where spending generates more GDP—only works below a 90% debt-to-GDP ratio. Once that threshold is exceeded, the multiplier drops below one. For every dollar borrowed and spent, only 90 cents of GDP is generated, while debt increases by a full dollar.

Today, the U.S. debt-to-GDP ratio is about 135%, far above the 90% threshold. He identifies Lebanon, Greece, and Italy as other countries in this 'super-debt' category. This ratio creates a 'headwind' to growth, as people anticipate a negative outcome—such as default or hyperinflation—and adjust their behavior accordingly.

Rickards concludes that neither money printing nor increased deficits function as stimulus. He calls the current economic situation the 'new great depression.'
