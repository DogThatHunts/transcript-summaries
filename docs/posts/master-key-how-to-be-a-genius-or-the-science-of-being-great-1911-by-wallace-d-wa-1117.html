Master Key | How to be a Genius or The Science of Being Great (1911) by Wallace D. Wattles

A classic New Thought guide outlining how individuals can tap into cosmic intelligence, overcome heredity, and achieve true greatness through deliberate thought and service.

The inherent capacity for greatness

Wallace D. Wattles asserts that every human being possesses an inherent "principle of power" that enables unlimited mental and spiritual growth. He distinguishes talent—which may simply be a single, disproportionately developed faculty—from genius, which he defines as the union of the human soul with God. According to Wattles, the ultimate purpose of human life is continuous, harmonious progress. Because all people are made from the same original, thinking substance, the capacity for greatness is universal. He argues that individuals fail to realize this potential because they adopt a reactive, "football-like" attitude, letting themselves be tossed about by environment and circumstances, rather than becoming "self-active" forces that radiate power from within.

Overcoming heredity and brain structure

Wattles argues that individuals are not permanently restricted by heredity or cranial formation. While acknowledging that people inherit subconscious tendencies (such as fear or ill temper) and physical brain structures that make certain talents easier to express, he claims these can be entirely overcome.

Specifically, Wattles asserts that: * Inherited mental traits are merely subconscious habits of thought that can be replaced by consciously forming opposite habits. * The physical brain does not dictate human capability; rather, the mind constructs the brain. * By focusing the "principle of power" and willpower on a specific brain area, an individual can multiply active brain cells in that region to develop any desired talent. * External circumstances, including extreme poverty, cannot prevent a person from rising if they maintain a determined personal attitude.

The cosmic source of wisdom and power

True greatness, in Wattles' view, is based on wisdom—the ability to perceive the best ends to aim at and the best means to reach them. Because human knowledge is inherently limited, individuals must connect their minds with "cosmic intelligence" (which he identifies as God or the Father) to receive true wisdom.

Wattles describes this cosmic intelligence as a thinking, spirit-substance that permeates all nature and contains all truth and history. He points to historical figures like Abraham Lincoln, George Washington, and Napoleon Bonaparte as individuals who succeeded because they possessed a spiritual insight that allowed them to read the thoughts of God and perceive truth beyond superficial appearances.

Moral and mental preparation

To align with cosmic intelligence and perceive truth, Wattles states that individuals must eliminate negative mental states and selfish motives. Anxiety, worry, and fear distort perception and sever the connection to the universal mind.

To prepare for greatness, Wattles advises: * Banishing financial and physical anxiety: Wattles refers readers to his book The Science of Getting Rich and asserts that perfect health and material wealth are guaranteed to those who obey the laws of nature and life. * Casting out moral evils: Individuals must master physical appetites, eliminate pride, and abandon the competitive desire to rule over or outdo others. * Eradicating malice and envy: Holding ill will toward others cuts the soul off from the universal mind.

The social and individual point of view

Wattles emphasizes that a person's progress depends heavily on their worldview. He advocates for a perspective rooted in evolution, viewing the world not as a ruined, decaying wreck, but as an unfinished, perfect work-in-progress.

From this standpoint, Wattles claims that: * There is no inherent evil; things or people that appear "evil" are simply undeveloped or "off the track." * Contemporary social discords, such as labor strikes, trusts, and even vice, are perfect for the current evolutionary stage of humanity and will naturally disappear as the race advances. * Individuals must view themselves and others as perfect, advancing souls. Looking at humanity as lost or degenerate contracts the mind, while seeing it as a glorious, evolving creation expands the mind and fosters greatness.

Consecration, identification, and idealization

Once the correct viewpoint is established, Wattles outlines three internal steps to power: 1. Consecration: This means absolute obedience to the soul. The body must be subjected to the mind, the mind to the soul, and the soul to the guidance of God. 2. Identification: The individual must consciously recognize and affirm that their internal principle of power is identical to God. Because there is only one conscious substance in the universe, the human mind is of the same "stuff" as the divine mind. 3. Idealization: The individual must form a clear, perfect mental image of what they want to be (e.g., a great lawyer, musician, or artisan) and hold this thought-form steadily, ignoring all adverse external suggestions.

Realization through present action

To prevent idealization from turning into mere daydreaming, Wattles insists on immediate, localized action. While an individual cannot instantly perform grand deeds on a public stage, they can begin by doing small, everyday tasks in a "great way."

  • Action at home: Greatness must first manifest in the domestic circle through poise, kindness, and consideration. However, Wattles warns against servility, stating that individuals should not become slaves to their families or meddle in the personal habits of others.
  • Action abroad: In business and public life, individuals must act with absolute poise, deliberate calmness, and faith in their own perceptions of truth. Wattles advises treating every person—from a beggar to a millionaire—with the distinguished consideration due to a fellow god.
  • Avoiding hurry: Hurry is a manifestation of fear. Poise and power are inseparably linked; a hurried mind is weak and instantly loses its connection to the universal intelligence.

True service versus the lust for power

Drawing on the teachings of Jesus and the philosophy of Emanuel Swedenborg, Wattles contrasts true greatness with the false greatness of the Pharisees. False greatness seeks dominion, high status, and the pleasure of ruling over others for self-gratification. True greatness, as exemplified by Abraham Lincoln, is rooted in the spirit of service.

Wattles cautions against the opposite extreme of morbid, self-sacrificing altruism, which he believes is a misconception of Christ's teachings. Instead, he argues that the highest service one can render to God and humanity is to make the absolute most of oneself, serving as a living demonstration of what is possible.

A daily mental exercise for alignment

Wattles provides a structured daily routine to help students replace old, limiting habits of thought with constructive ones: 1. Physical relaxation: Lie flat on the back and consciously relax every muscle from head to toe. 2. Nerve and organ affirmation: Mentally direct attention through the body, affirming that the nerves, lungs, heart, and digestive organs are in perfect, healthy working order. 3. Mental clearing: Cast out all fear, worry, greed, and ill will. 4. Viewpoint and consecration: Affirm that the world is perfect and advancing, and surrender the body and mind to the guidance of the soul and God. 5. Identification and idealization: Affirm oneness with the Eternal Consciousness, dwell on the idealized thought-form of oneself, and mentally appropriate the power to realize it.

Wattles concludes by referencing Ralph Waldo Emerson's essay on the Oversoul, reinforcing the monistic philosophy that the human soul is an expression of a singular, universal, and perfect divine reality.
