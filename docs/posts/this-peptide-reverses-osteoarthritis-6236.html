Peptides and Osteoarthritis: evidence and caveats

In this video, a researcher discusses emerging evidence that certain peptides—particularly semaglutide—may reduce or even reverse osteoarthritis symptoms in humans.

Key insights

  • The peptide liraglutide shows promise in preclinical studies for reducing pain and inflammation in osteoarthritic animals.
  • In animal models, liraglutide reduces levels of pro-inflammatory molecules like PGE and IL-1β, and decreases the release of glycosaminoglycans, indicating less joint destruction.
  • In a small clinical trial, semaglutide—related to liraglutide—was co-administered with hyaluronic acid and showed greater improvement in pain and function (measured by Womack score) compared to hyaluronic acid alone after 24 weeks.
  • Cartilage thickness increased in the semaglutide group, suggesting potential cartilage preservation or renewal.
  • The study claims semaglutide improves osteoarthritis independently of weight loss, though this is not fully supported by the study design.

Methodological concerns

  • The assessments of cartilage in the human trial were unblinded, meaning the researchers knew which group received the treatment, introducing potential bias.
  • The study does not adequately control for weight loss, a known factor in osteoarthritis progression, making it difficult to confirm whether benefits are independent of metabolic effects.
  • The sample size is small, limiting generalizability of the findings.

Context and limitations

  • The evidence is primarily from animal studies and a single small human trial.
  • The study focuses on metabolic etiology of osteoarthritis, suggesting effects may not apply to all forms of the condition.
  • Long-term, large-scale clinical trials are needed to confirm these results.
