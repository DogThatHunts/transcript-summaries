Ido Portal, a movement coach and philosopher who has worked with elite athletes including Christian McCaffrey and Connor McGregor, sits down with Danny Jones to discuss why isolated optimization fails and why real transformation requires "assassination"—killing off old patterns to allow new ones to emerge. Portal argues that modern training has become too mechanicalized and goal-oriented, stripping away the presence and choice that make us human. The conversation ranges from breathing mechanics and peripheral vision to the philosophy of practice itself, with Portal insisting that genuine practice should have no reason, no goal, and no expected outcome.

Five key insights

  1. Isolated components fail: Portal argues that strength and conditioning, flexibility, and other specialized training domains are too narrow. Real transformation requires addressing the whole person—the athlete, the mover, and the human being beneath, including psychological and spiritual dimensions that most coaches ignore.

  2. Presence beats flow: Flow states can be dangerous because they automate and mechanicalize behavior, removing the person from the equation. Portal prefers a state that contains flow but maintains awareness—a "watchful tower" from which you observe yourself acting, preserving choice and consciousness.

  3. Observation dissolves tension: Rather than forcing change through stretching or corrective exercises, Portal advocates simply observing where tension lives in your body. The act of paying attention without trying to fix anything often dissolves the problem naturally—a principle he applies to movement, posture, and emotional states alike.

  4. Practice has no reason: Portal contends that genuine practice should not be motivated by outcomes—health, beauty, performance, or social reward. The moment you practice for something, you corrupt it. True practice is an end in itself, aligned with how evolution and natural systems work.

  5. Environment shapes neurology: Geometry, nature, and physical space directly alter how we think and move. Cities with straight lines and screens create a different nervous system than forests or deserts. Portal takes athletes to remote locations not just for novelty but because the environment itself is a tool for transformation.

On movement and training philosophy

Portal rejects the idea that skateboarding, parkour, or other "natural movement" practices are better than modern training. Instead, he argues that the word "natural" is meaningless—everything humans do is natural, including cities and technology. What matters is whether a practice maintains presence and choice or devolves into automation. He criticizes modern postural yoga as a Western invention influenced by Swedish gymnastics, not an ancient practice, and notes that traditional cultures move in curves and spirals, not the straight lines of contemporary fitness.

When asked about his work with Christian McCaffrey, Portal describes a holistic approach that includes power cleans, strength and conditioning, meditation, reading difficult philosophical texts, mathematics, and practices like rhythmicality and air sense (spatial awareness). He emphasizes that these are not separate domains but layers of the same person, and that real transformation at the elite level requires the athlete to become a different person—to de-ambition themselves and find freedom within the game rather than being enslaved by it.

On presence and observation

Portal uses a live demonstration with Jones to show how observation works: he asks Jones to notice which seat bone bears more weight. Jones says they're equal, but Portal points out that physically, perfect equality is impossible. The tension in Jones's pelvis prevents him from sensing the actual asymmetry. Portal argues that simply paying attention—without trying to change anything—gradually softens tension and reveals what's really happening. This principle applies to breathing, posture, emotional states, and movement quality.

He contrasts this with the Western approach of "sit up straight" or "breathe through your nose," which creates tension rather than releasing it. True postural capacity is not an external position but an organizational capacity—like a candle flame, alive and responsive. You can sit slouched with perfect postural capacity or upright in meditation for twelve hours. The difference is presence, not position.

On breath, vision, and peripheral awareness

Portal argues that breathing is far more versatile than popular advice suggests. While nose breathing has benefits, mouth breathing is not inherently bad—evolution wouldn't have allowed it otherwise. The real issue is adapting your breathing to the scenario: different breathing for stillness, conversation, sprinting, or combat. He criticizes "belly breathing" as an oversimplified concept that often creates distortion rather than solving problems.

On vision, Portal emphasizes that elite athletes like Michael Jordan, Tom Brady, and Kelly Slater use wide peripheral vision and trust it. Most people have the capacity for peripheral vision but don't trust the information they receive, so they revert to focused vision, which is slower. He trains athletes to trust peripheral blur and to recognize that smooth eye movement creates smooth neck movement—the two are fused together.

On genetics, limitation, and adaptation

When asked whether genetics or training matters more, Portal deflects the question, arguing that it moves people away from practice. Instead, he asks: "How shall I practice to improve?" He points to examples like blind skateboarders who rip harder than sighted ones, and a paralyzed breakdancer whose atrophied legs make him lighter and more capable of certain tricks. Limitations often motivate people and create openness to finding new ways. The standardization of humans in Olympic sports—where you must be born to the right parents and in the right skin color to compete—is a corruption that skateboarding and parkour have begun to escape.

On the "assassination" of old patterns

Portal describes his work as "assassination"—not supporting what's already there, but killing something so something else can be born. This applies to athletes and to himself. He's spent decades being a beginner, abandoning skills the moment he masters them because repetition bores him. He doesn't do one-arm handstands anymore because he can do them. This approach is risky; some athletes worry that working with Portal might make them lose interest in their sport. But Portal argues that the real edge—the thing that separates Messi from other great players—comes from a place beyond ambition, from freedom within the game rather than slavery to it.

On eastern practices and global research

Portal has spent thirty years teaching and researching globally. He's studied capoeira in Brazil for seventeen years, worked with Bollywood in India, researched Soviet methods in Russia, explored hermetic and esoteric systems in Europe, and recently spent time in Indonesia studying ancient peoples and trance states. He argues that modern postural yoga is a Western fabrication, and that many traditional practices have been lost or corrupted. Indonesia, he says, is currently the most fascinating place on Earth for him—still containing unknowns, natural trance states without drugs, and people connected to something deeper.

On supplements, drugs, and dependence

Portal takes a nuanced stance on performance-enhancing drugs and supplements. He argues that the "enhanced games" (where all athletes were allowed to use PEDs) produced poor results, suggesting that current world records are already enhanced—we just caught some athletes and not others. He criticizes the purist attitude of condemning others for using drugs while claiming independence, noting that everyone is dependent on something—air, food, human contact. The word "natural" is meaningless.

That said, he supports supplementation for practitioners who are actively working on themselves. He uses nicotine occasionally when doing deep practice, not for casual conversation. He also advocates trying periods without your usual supplements or stimulants—not to prove you don't depend on them (you do), but as a practice to observe what happens and investigate the fear of discomfort. Many people discover that their "shitty mood" or "fatigue" dissolves the moment they stop resisting it and start observing it.

On practice without reason

Portal's core philosophy is that practice should have no reason, no goal, no expected outcome. The moment you practice for health, beauty, performance, or social reward, you corrupt it. He's damaged his own health through decades in sun, wind, and salt water, and he doesn't care because he wasn't practicing for health. When people praised his movement or body, he stopped moving beautifully and stopped having a beautiful body—because he'd taken away the reason. Now he practices for no reason at all, which paradoxically makes everything work better.

This aligns with how evolution works: organisms don't practice for survival; they practice, and survival is a side effect. The same applies to humans. If you orient yourself directly toward dopamine, health, or success, you miss the deeper transformation that comes from practice itself.
