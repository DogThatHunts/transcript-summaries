Key insights

  • Target active real estate investors over retail buyers: Taylor emphasizes that retail homeowners only purchase every ten years using licensed agents, whereas active real estate investors buy monthly and routinely pay substantial assignment fees if a wholesaler locks up viable inventory at a discount.
  • Underwrite fix-and-flips with built-in pessimism: Rather than banking on peak comparable sales, wholesalers should calculate the average price per square foot across tight, realistic comps and assume their exit value will land $10,000 to $15,000 below market comps to ensure an adequate safety buffer.
  • Rental properties must deliver a 20% to 25% cash-on-cash return: Because passive stock index funds return roughly 10% without property management headaches, rental investments must significantly outperform the market. Taylor calculates net yield using the formula: (Gross Rent × 0.8 − PITI) × 12 ÷ Total Cash Invested.
  • Find distressed deals using median price filtering: In unfamiliar metropolitan markets, search for the median home price and filter listings above that baseline to isolate stable neighborhoods, then target the few anomalously low-priced, distressed homes within those boundaries.
  • Frame price reductions through curiosity, not insult: When speaking with listing agents about overpriced or rough properties, aggressively lowballing closes doors; instead, ask clarifying questions comparing the listing against turnkey neighborhood comps to encourage sellers to offer price drops or concessions organically.

Core underwriting: fix-and-flips versus rentals

Taylor establishes a clear framework for categorizing residential opportunities based on condition and end-buyer utility. Properties requiring structural or substantial cosmetic upgrades belong to fix-and-flip investors, while structurally sound, rent-ready assets must be analyzed purely as yield-generating investments.

To comp a fix-and-flip, Taylor demonstrates pulling closed transactions from the past 12 months within a three-to-four block radius, selecting properties within 250 to 300 square feet of the subject property rather than wildly disparate builds. Calculating an average price per square foot from four or five renovated sales gives a realistic after-repair value (ARV). Reviewing his purchase of a duplex at 3731 Tyler Street in Detroit, Taylor outlines buying at $65,000 with $5,000 in closing costs and a $35,000 rehab budget. By conservatively projecting a sales price of $190,000 (well below comparable sales of $205,000 to $225,000), he locks in an estimated $75,500 net profit margin after factoring in real estate agent commissions.

When evaluating buy-and-hold rentals, Taylor rejects deals that fail to generate returns well in excess of index funds. He factors an automatic 20% deduction off gross rent for operating reserves, vacancies, and maintenance, subsequently subtracting principal, interest, taxes, and insurance (PITI). If the annualized net cash flow divided by the down payment and closing costs does not exceed 20%, the asset fails the investment test.
