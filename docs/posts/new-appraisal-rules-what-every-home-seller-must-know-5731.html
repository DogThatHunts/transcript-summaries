NEW Appraisal Rules: What Every Home Seller MUST Know!

Appraisal expert Jim Finney explains the new UAD 3.6 standards, why the rollout is delayed, and what sellers and agents must prepare for.

Summary

Real estate professional Audra Lambert interviews forensic appraiser Jim Finney about the mandatory transition to UAD 3.6, the new Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac appraisal dataset that took effect November 2, 2026. The conversation covers how the standard replaces paper forms with tablet-based, highly granular data collection, why many lenders received extensions to February 2027, and practical steps sellers and agents must take — such as providing floor plans, upgrade details, solar-panel documentation, and disaster-mitigation information — to avoid delays and low valuations in a slowing market.

Key insights

  • UAD 3.6 retires the legacy Form 1004/1073 and requires appraisers to input hundreds of discrete data points via tablet, doubling typical inspection time from ~40 minutes to ~2 hours.
  • Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac granted lender extensions until end of February 2027 because underwriters, appraisers, and legacy software providers were not ready; each assignment may still use the old 2.6 format depending on the lender.
  • Sellers and listing agents must proactively supply detailed property data (room-by-room finishes, ceiling heights, above-/below-grade square footage, solar ownership, seismic water-heater strapping, fire-zone vegetation clearance) or risk reinspection fees and escrow delays.
  • In a declining market with record-low pending sales, appraisers must analyze price trends and adjust comparable sales for seller concessions (rate buy-downs, closing costs, commissions), often at 50 cents on the dollar, which can significantly lower the effective sale price used in valuations.
  • The transition coincides with a sharp market slowdown (7%+ mortgage rates, falling buyer traffic), pressuring appraiser fees and increasing the risk of errors as the industry adopts new tools and workflows simultaneously.

The new UAD 3.6 standard

Jim Finney explains that UAD 3.6 replaces the 20-year-old static appraisal forms with a dynamic, machine-readable dataset. Appraisers now use tablets with extensive drop-down menus to capture granular details: ceiling height per room, flooring type by room, roof flashing, gutter condition, disaster-mitigation features, and precise above-grade vs. below-grade square footage per ANSI standards. The new Uniform Residential Appraisal Report (URAR) is modular — sections appear only if relevant — and AI-driven underwriting reviews will flag inconsistencies between photos and entered ratings (condition/quality scores 1–6). Finney notes the scope now "bumps up against the role of a home inspector."

Rollout delays and extensions

Although November 2, 2026 was the hard deadline, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac announced extensions to February 28, 2027 for lenders who apply and are approved. Finney attributes the delay to three bottlenecks: underwriters unfamiliar with the new report layout, legacy software providers with unresolved glitches (one major provider "collapsed"), and appraisers still adapting to the data-intensive workflow. He warns that agents and sellers must confirm with the lender which format — 2.6 or 3.6 — will be used for each specific appraisal order.

Preparation checklist for Sellers and agents

  • Floor plans with ANSI-compliant above-grade/below-grade breakdowns and room counts.
  • Upgrade documentation: dates, costs, and specifics for kitchens, baths, flooring, countertops, appliances, and mechanical systems.
  • Solar/photovoltaic details: owned vs. leased vs. power-purchase agreement, panel count, age, payoff terms in contract.
  • FHA/VA requirements: seismic water-heater straps (dual, top/bottom third) and pressure-relief drain line.
  • Disaster mitigation: Zone Zero vegetation clearance (5 ft from structure), fire-resistant roof/soffits, flood/tide risks.
  • Concession transparency: disclose all seller-paid buy-downs, closing costs, repair credits, and buyer-agent commissions in MLS so appraisers can adjust comparables accurately.

Market context and valuation challenges

Finney and Lambert describe a "wonky" market: pending sales at the lowest level since 2001, refinance volume near zero, and purchase deals falling out of escrow as rates exceed 7%. Appraisers must now declare markets as increasing, stable, or declining and apply time adjustments to older comps — sometimes looking back 12 months due to data scarcity. Seller concessions (e.g., 2-1 rate buy-downs costing $80–100K) reduce net proceeds and must be analyzed at roughly 50% weight. Finney cautions that pricing above the concession-adjusted market leads to stale listings, price cuts, and buyer suspicion. He also notes the new format's AI-driven uniformity may struggle to capture non-quantifiable value drivers (e.g., proximity to family) while filtering out distressed-sale noise.

Software landscape and industry outlook

Legacy appraisal software vendors are struggling to retrofit 2.6 platforms; a new generation of "AI-oriented" tools built natively for 3.6 is emerging, enabling voice input, LiDAR sketching, and automated photo labeling. Online "shootout" demos let appraisers compare platforms remotely. Finney sees opportunity for younger, tech-fluent appraisers but warns the concurrent market downturn is suppressing fees — appraisal management companies are paying the same or less despite doubled workload. He urges patience: "It's not a fun environment to appraise in when you don't have confidence in the tools."
