Why your Roth IRA May Not Be Enough

Doug Andrew, a financial strategist and retirement planning specialist with over 50 years of experience, argues that the traditional Roth IRA is fundamentally limited and that many Americans are missing out on better alternatives.

The two advantages of a Roth IRA

Doug explains that both Roth IRAs and traditional IRAs or 401ks accumulate tax-free, and both can be funded with after-tax dollars. However, Roth IRAs come with significant strings attached:

  • Access to funds is restricted for at least five years after opening or until age 59 and a half.
  • There is no flexibility in contributions—once you miss a contribution year, you cannot make it up in future years.

Doug notes that these limitations make Roth IRAs rigid and ill-suited for modern retirement needs.

Four additional advantages of a Max-Funded IUL

Doug compares the Roth to a Max-Funded Immediate Universal Life (IUL) insurance product, which he claims offers four key advantages:

  • Higher contribution limits: Unlike Roth IRAs, which cap contributions at $7,500 annually (as of 2026), IULs allow contributions up to $300,000 per year. Clients can fund only a portion and make up the rest later.
  • Liquidity without penalties: With an IUL, you can withdraw funds without tax or penalty—unlike a Roth, where early access triggers a 10% penalty.
  • Indexing protection: IULs use indexing to track market performance, avoiding losses during downturns. For example, during the 2000–2012 period, IUL-linked to the S&P 500 grew from $1 million to $2.5–3 million, while indexed mutual funds lost up to 40% and barely recovered.
  • Tax-free death benefit: Upon death, an IUL can grow and transfer to heirs tax-free—doubling a $1 million account to $2 million. This is not available with Roth IRAs.

The hidden Tax cost of Delaying Tax payments

Doug argues that most Americans defer tax payments on retirement accounts, believing it will save them money in the future. He claims this is a mistake:

  • Tax rates are likely to rise, not fall.
  • Delaying tax payments means future tax liabilities grow on a larger, undeducted balance.

He illustrates with a 25% tax bracket example: a $1 million IRA or 401k grows to $1.333 million, but after tax, only $1 million remains. In a 33% tax bracket, the net value drops to $893,000.

In contrast, a Roth or IUL grows tax-free—resulting in a full $1 million net value.

Real-World Tax deductions and IUL funding

Doug demonstrates how real estate can be used to fund IULs indirectly:

  • A client refinanced four rental properties at $1 million each, borrowing at 7.5% interest.
  • The interest is tax-deductible, resulting in a $75,000 deduction.
  • In a 33% tax bracket, this saves $25,000 in real dollars.
  • The IUL earns a 10% return, generating $100,000 in value.

He compares this to hiring an employee: if the employee earns $100,000 more than the $50,000 cost, it's a 100% return on investment.

This creates a powerful financial advantage—$100,000 more in income from a $50,000 investment.

Retirement income comparison

With a 33% tax bracket:

  • Annual income from a traditional IRA at a 10% payout is $89,000.
  • From a Roth or IUL, it's $133,000—$44,000 more.

Over 20 years, this adds $880,000 in additional income—50% more than traditional retirement accounts.

Key takeaways

  • Roth IRAs have only two advantages and are rigid.
  • Max-Funded IULs offer four additional advantages, including liquidity, indexing, and tax-free death benefits.
  • Delaying tax payments increases future liabilities.
  • Real estate can be used to fund IULs indirectly through tax-deductible interest.
