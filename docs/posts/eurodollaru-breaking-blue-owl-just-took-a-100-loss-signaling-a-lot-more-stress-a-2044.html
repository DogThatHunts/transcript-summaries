The sudden collapse of Loporex and the private credit valuation problem

Jeff Snyder of Eurodollar University highlights a stark warning sign in the private credit market: Blue Owl Capital recently wrote down its private loan to Loporex—an adhesive liner manufacturer owned by Pamplona Capital Management—from par value to nearly zero in just a few months. Historically, senior debt is expected to show warning signs like falling cash flows or liquidity issues before collapsing. Instead, Blue Owl's highest-priority loans were marked at 100 cents on the dollar, and its second-lien debt was marked near 90, right up until Moody's declared the company in default.

Snyder argues that this "100 to zero" phenomenon exposes the fundamental flaw of private credit. Because these assets do not trade on public, liquid markets, their "fair value" is determined by internal models, assumptions, and manager judgment. Managers have strong incentives—including management and performance fees—to keep valuations high. Consequently, instead of gradually declining as credit quality deteriorates, private loans remain marked at par until reality suddenly overwhelms the model, resulting in overnight collapses that shatter investor trust.

How the AI debt boom is crowding out global borrowers

While private credit valuations face scrutiny, global credit markets are experiencing structural shifts. Technology "hyperscalers" are issuing massive amounts of debt to finance data centers, chips, and power infrastructure. This immense supply of tech-related debt is competing for rigid investor capital, crowding out other corporate borrowers and driving up dollar-denominated funding costs.

To escape this congestion, major borrowers are searching for alternative pools of liquidity outside the U.S. dollar system: * European Markets: U.S. companies like Amazon and Uber are increasingly issuing debt in sterling and euros. U.S.-domiciled entities issued a record volume of euro-denominated debt, seeking markets not yet overwhelmed by AI-related issuance. * New Zealand Kauri Market: The Export-Import Bank of Korea (Kexum) recently issued 625 million New Zealand dollars in five-year notes—its largest offering in the country in nine years. Kexum's treasury director explicitly noted that the move was designed to avoid the elevated borrowing costs caused by U.S. hyperscaler issuance.

Snyder notes that while these borrowers are not necessarily in distress, their migration to smaller, alternative currency markets demonstrates that credit is becoming highly fragmented, selective, and less automatic.

Opaque structures and the return of due diligence

During the credit boom, money moved faster than verification because everyone benefited from closing the next deal. Now, as the credit cycle turns, past negligence is coming to light.

Snyder points to recent fraud allegations involving Radiant World, an iron ore trading company. A fund run by Point Bonita (within Jefferies Asset Management) accused Radiant World of operating a fraudulent scheme involving falsified invoices, contracts, and email correspondence. This follows previous high-profile credit issues with Tricolor and First Brands.

These cases raise critical questions about the lack of independent verification performed by lenders during the boom. Snyder suggests that while a single case of fraud may be isolated, repeated instances indicate a systemic failure where lenders prioritized deploying capital over basic due diligence. Now that the cycle is reversing, investors are finally checking the math, prompting capital to migrate toward safer exits.

The Renovo precedent and the BDC feedback loop

The Loporex collapse closely mirrors the downfall of Renovo Home Partners. Renovo, a home renovation rollup backed by Audax Group, had its private debt held by BlackRock, Apollo's MidCap Financial, and Oaktree. Despite clear signs of distress—including a restructuring where lenders allowed Renovo to pay interest using payment-in-kind (PIK) debt rather than cash—lenders continued to mark the debt at par. Weeks later, Renovo filed for bankruptcy and shut down, wiping out the valuation instantly.

This pattern of hiding distress through PIK modifications and generous marks has led to deep skepticism among public investors. Business Development Companies (BDCs) and private credit providers are increasingly trading well below their reported Net Asset Values (NAV). The stock market is effectively signaling that it does not trust the valuations reported on BDC books.

This discount risks creating a destructive feedback loop: 1. Falling share prices and investor mistrust hurt BDC fundraising. 2. Weaker fundraising leaves managers with less capital to roll over or refinance existing loans. 3. Highly leveraged middle-market companies, unable to refinance in a crowded market, begin to fail. 4. These failures force dramatic write-downs, further damaging investor confidence and restarting the cycle.

Ultimately, Snyder concludes that the credit downturn is not creating bad loans; rather, it is revealing the bad loans and aggressive valuations that were swept under the rug during the boom.
