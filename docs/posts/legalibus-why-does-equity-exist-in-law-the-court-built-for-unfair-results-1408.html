How the rigidity of early English common law forced the creation of "equity," a parallel system of justice designed to prioritize human fairness over strict paperwork.

The English common law system was historically rigid, dismissing cases that did not fit pre-printed forms. To bypass this unfairness, citizens petitioned the king directly, prompting the Lord Chancellor to step in. This gave rise to "equity," a parallel legal system based on conscience and fairness that exists alongside common law.

Key takeaways

  • The rigidity of common law: In early England, starting a legal case required a specific "writ." If a grievance did not fit an existing form, the courts offered no remedy, regardless of the injustice.
  • The rise of equity: Overwhelmed by direct pleas from citizens, the king delegated these petitions to the Lord Chancellor, who judged cases based on moral fairness and conscience.
  • A parallel system: Equity did not replace common law but acted as a corrective referee when strict legal rules produced unfair outcomes, eventually leading to concepts like the trust.
