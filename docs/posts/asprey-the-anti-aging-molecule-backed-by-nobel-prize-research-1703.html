Asprey | The Anti-Aging Molecule Backed by Nobel Prize Research

Dave Asprey reveals how a Nobel‑backed NAD+ strategy could reboot your cellular power plant and slow aging.

Overview

Dave Asprey presents a monologue that frames aging as a loss of a single molecule – NAD+ – and outlines a step‑by‑step protocol to restore it. He ties his claims to Nobel prize research while warning that low‑quality supplements often fail.

The role of NAD+

  • Asprey describes NAD+ as the "fuel" that powers ATP production in mitochondria.
  • He likens the molecule to a master builder, while an enzyme acts as a demolition crew that destroys NAD+.
  • According to Asprey, the balance between builder and demolition crew determines how well cells can repair damage.

Evidence from Nobel prize research

  • Nobel prize researchers reported that brain NAD+ levels are the biggest factor separating severe cognitive decline from preserved cognition in older adults.
  • In post‑mortem studies, 20‑50% of people with full Alzheimer’s pathology retained normal cognition, a difference Asprey attributes to higher NAD+ levels.
  • Animal experiments cited by Asprey showed that restoring NAD+ rescued learning, reduced inflammation, and reversed disease markers.
  • Asprey notes that the researchers themselves called these findings a challenge to the idea that cognitive decline is irreversible.

Why NAD+ declines

  • Asprey explains that with age the “builder” (NAD+ synthesis) slows while the “demolition crew” (NAD+‑consuming enzymes) expands.
  • He estimates a 40‑50% loss of NAD+ capacity by middle age.
  • The resulting energy shortfall (lower ATP) triggers the body’s survival mode, shutting down non‑essential functions such as skin repair, muscle recovery, and brain function.

Practical protocol to rebuild NAD+

1. diet

  • Provide raw materials: niacin (vitamin B3) from beef liver, chicken, turkey, fish; tryptophan from high‑quality protein.
  • Include plant compounds that inhibit the demolition crew: quercetin (capers, red onions) and apigenin (chamomile tea).

2. fasting

  • Time‑restricted eating (14‑16 h daily) activates AMPK, the cellular alarm that wakes the builder.
  • A full 24‑hour fast once or twice a week gives a stronger NAD+ surge.
  • Constant eating keeps insulin high, silencing AMPK and flattening NAD+ production.

3. exercise

  • Regular training provides the same AMPK signal as fasting.
  • The fastest NAD+ trigger, according to Asprey, is REHIT (Reduced Exertion High‑Intensity Interval Training): two 2‑minute slow‑cycle intervals bracketing two 20‑second all‑out sprints, completed in ~5 minutes.

4. supplementation (final step)

  • After diet, fasting, and exercise are in place, add NAD+ precursors:
  • NMN (nicotinamide mononucleotide) – converts to NAD+ one step faster; Asprey recommends 600‑900 mg daily, starting low and taking in the morning.
  • NR (nicotinamide riboside) – another precursor, less emphasized.
  • Higher doses may be needed for vegans, people over 60, or those under chronic stress, as their baseline NAD+ is lower.
  • Asprey cautions that low doses or short‑term use explain why many users feel “nothing” from supplements.

Takeaway

Asprey argues that aging symptoms point to a failing NAD+ supply and that a combined approach—targeted nutrition, strategic fasting, brief high‑intensity exercise, and proper NMN supplementation—can rebuild the molecule’s production and slow the aging process.
