Sam Altman on how scaling intelligence, not just models, will reshape what's possible for startups and society.

Sam Altman on scaling, startups, and the future of AI

In this conversation with a Stanford systems design class, OpenAI's CEO Sam Altman discusses the transformative power of scaling—not just in AI models, but across organizations and products. He reflects on lessons from Y Combinator, the unexpected success of ChatGPT, and why betting against LLM scaling now feels misguided.

Five key insights

  1. Scaling unlocks emergent properties that aren't obvious at smaller scales. Altman argues that most interesting breakthroughs in his career have involved discovering what becomes possible when you push something to a scale people haven't tried before. The magic of Y Combinator, for example, emerged only when funding 100+ companies per batch—the network effects and serendipity were invisible at smaller batch sizes.

  2. Humans struggle to reason about exponentials. Organizations and individuals have difficulty imagining that scaling laws, revenue growth, and organizational complexity can continue exponentially. This requires deliberate, first-principles reasoning with teams to overcome intuitive resistance.

  3. Clear goals and conviction matter more than perfect planning. When OpenAI decided to bet everything on scaling deep learning, the clarity of that commitment—and the reasoning behind it—gave the organization alignment and momentum, even when skeptics warned it wouldn't work.

  4. Products emerge from observing what users actually do, not from assigned problems. ChatGPT wasn't the original plan; it arose when OpenAI noticed developers were using the GPT-3 API to chat rather than build products. Following the Y Combinator principle of "see what your users love and do that," the team pivoted.

  5. The inference stack is severely under-invested. While many teams focus on training better models, Altman argues the real frontier is making intelligence cheap and abundant at scale—the inference and delivery layer that will make AI a true utility.

On ambition and startup possibilities

Altman opens by noting that AI has fundamentally changed what's possible for startups. With affordable token spend, a small team can now accomplish what would have required a hundred-person engineering team five years ago. This shifts not just speed and scope, but the level of ambition founders can pursue.

He cautions against assigning startup ideas in a classroom setting: if a problem is obvious enough for him to articulate, it's probably obvious to many others. Instead, he suspects the most valuable opportunities today—multi-trillion-dollar markets that don't yet exist—are things only students might discover, because they're not yet constrained by the mental models of the AI research establishment.

Scale as a systems design principle

When asked to unpack what "scale" means as a design tool, Altman offers an observation he finds unsettling: nearly every interesting breakthrough he's witnessed has involved emergent properties that scale, or scaling continuing to deliver returns far beyond consensus expectations. He offers no satisfying theory for why this is true, but the empirical pattern is strong.

Examples span AI model scaling (where skeptics said scaling wouldn't produce interesting results), Y Combinator's network effects (which only appeared at scale), and the economy of scale in companies. Yet most people don't push scaling far enough, and when they do, systems break in unpredictable ways.

Breaking down scaling as a systems problem

When OpenAI scaled AI models, the team had to solve multiple interlocking problems:

  • Technical feasibility: Running training across 10,000–100,000 GPUs had never been attempted. It required stacks of specialized engineering talent.
  • Capital requirements: The financial risk and business model uncertainty were immense.
  • Cultural resistance: Researchers argued that concentrating all compute into one project was wasteful; better to distribute it and learn from multiple experiments.

Altman notes this pattern repeats across every domain he's examined. The key is breaking each objection into discrete, addressable problems rather than treating scaling as a monolithic risk.

Humans at scale

The hardest part of scaling, Altman suggests, is the human side. People carry mental models and expectations shaped by past systems, and these don't automatically adapt to exponential growth. Three things help:

  1. A clear goal and plan for how to get there.
  2. Clear reasoning about why the bet makes sense, even if it's counterintuitive.
  3. Willingness to reason through exponentials from first principles with the team, because humans didn't evolve to think exponentially.

When OpenAI committed to scaling deep learning, the clarity of that bet—"this is our thing; if we're wrong, we fail"—gave people permission to override their instinctive caution.

ChatGPT and Codex: two paths to product-market fit

Altman walks through how two different products emerged from the same underlying capability.

ChatGPT's unexpected success

OpenAI launched the GPT-3 API in summer 2020 hoping others would build products on top of it. The API got no traction initially. Then, about a month later, it went viral on Twitter when a few developers posted cool demos. However, the models were "shockingly bad" by today's standards—yet the excitement was genuine.

The only business that worked at scale was copywriting, which wasn't compelling. But Altman noticed something: developers were using their API keys to chat with the model rather than build products. The team had GPT-3.5 ready and had developed new post-training techniques for instruction-following.

Instead of waiting for a better model to make the API work, they built a chatbot—initially framed as a research demo to convince others to build chat products and pay for the API. But ChatGPT went "crazy viral." By the fourth or fifth day of exponential growth, Altman recognized the pattern from Y Combinator: when something is growing fast and still not very good, you have a guaranteed hit.

The team pivoted into emergency mode, building a company and product simultaneously. They charged users to cover compute costs, not yet knowing the long-term business model. That improvisation worked.

Codex's slower burn

The original plan was to go all-in on code. Altman and the team believed coding was how models would control things on computers, and robots would control things in the physical world. By combining both, they could get intelligence to "do stuff for you in the world."

Codex got really good by early 2024, but the real inflection came with GPT-4.5 (or later models), when people started doing "incredible things" with it. Unlike ChatGPT's overnight virality, Codex's success was more gradual but ultimately became the killer enterprise app.

The capabilities pipeline and future architectures

Altman confirms that the current pipeline—pre-training, mid-training, post-training, then RL and supervised feedback—is indeed the standard shape across research groups. However, he expects a major rewrite, though he doesn't know when or how.

When asked what an optimal solution would look like, he deflects: "I think that's a research problem for the AIs to figure out." He then reveals OpenAI's ambitious goal: by September 2024, to use 500,000 A100-equivalent GPUs as an "AI research intern," and by March 2028, to have AI doing the work of a "full end-to-end, very talented researcher" figuring out complete new architectures.

With the current pipeline and architectures, Altman believes they will "get over the line" of AI doing "incredible, incredible work."

Intelligence as a new utility

Altman pivots to a broader framing: AI is becoming a new utility, like electricity or the internet. There are few historical examples to learn from, so metaphors matter—but they also break down.

When electricity became a utility, companies didn't market "electricity" (which sounded scary and incomprehensible). Instead, they marketed light at night. Once people understood the benefit, they discovered all the other uses.

Altman suspects that even if OpenAI is right about intelligence becoming a universal utility, the framing "we're selling intelligence" doesn't resonate. People need a concrete, tangible benefit first. He doesn't yet know what the equivalent of "light at night" is for AI, but finding that frame will be crucial for adoption.

Compute vs. tokens as utilities

When asked whether compute (chips) or tokens should be thought of as the utility, Altman clarifies: consumers will think in terms of tokens or even one level higher—not the underlying hardware. Just as phone users don't think about which chip powers the base station, AI users will care about availability, cost, and quality, not whether their inference runs on an A100 or H100.

As the world moves toward constant AI agents running for each person, the abstraction level may rise even further. The utility will be "access to the whole system," not the infrastructure details.

Advice for frontier labs

When asked what he'd work on if he were a student in the class's "one-person frontier lab" project, Altman is direct: focus on the inference stack.

While many smart people are working on training and will produce incredible models regardless, the inference layer—how to deliver cheap, abundant intelligence at scale—is severely under-invested. He predicts that all frontier labs will eventually have to become inference companies to a significant degree.

On LLM skepticism and identity

Responding to a question about Yann LeCun's view that LLMs are a dead end, Altman notes that LLMs have already surpassed human intelligence in some domains (e.g., one of OpenAI's models recently disproved an Erdos conjecture that mathematicians had worked on for years). They're also vastly worse than humans at long-horizon, high-judgment tasks.

The key point: betting against LLM scaling at this point "feels quite misguided." Altman reflects that skeptics have been wrong repeatedly, yet some continue to argue the approach won't work. He attributes this to identity: when people tie their identity to a belief and evidence disproves it, they struggle to update. This is "a form of insanity," he suggests—and a reminder to stay epistemically flexible in both directions.
