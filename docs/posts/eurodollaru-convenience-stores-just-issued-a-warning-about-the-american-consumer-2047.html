EurodollarU | Convenience Stores Just Issued a Warning About the American Consumer

Beneath resilient headlines, declining fuel volumes, collapsing ticket resale prices, and contracting construction spending reveal an American consumer increasingly forced to choose between gasoline and basic discretionary goods.

The convenience store warning: nominal revenue vs. real volume

Jeff Snider of EurodollarU argues that while mainstream narratives champion the "resilient" American consumer, microeconomic data reveals a different reality. The starkest warning comes from convenience store giant Circle K (owned by Alimentation Couche-Tard). While the company reported a 33% surge in fuel revenue to $16.7 billion due to high oil prices, actual fuel volumes declined by 1.6% in the US and 4.3% in Europe.

Snider highlights this divergence as the classic definition of demand destruction: nominal spending rises due to price inflation, but real economic activity contracts. Furthermore, Circle K reported that consumers are making fewer store visits and becoming "increasingly intentional" with their purchases, leaving discretionary items like snacks and soft drinks on the shelves to afford necessary fuel.

Discretionary demand destruction from tennis courts to grocery aisles

This consumer pullback is spreading beyond convenience stores into broader discretionary spending:

  • The US Open: Resale ticket prices for the tennis tournament collapsed by 50% to 88% over a three-day period, indicating that marginal consumers are refusing to chase high prices.
  • Big-box retail: Walmart slashed prices on over 11,000 items, and while foot traffic remained steady, spending per visit declined.
  • Grocery price wars: Kroger, Amazon, and Aldi are aggressively cutting prices and increasing promotions to capture highly price-sensitive shoppers.
  • Dollar stores: Dollar General reported increased traffic from middle- and upper-income households trading down, while Dollar Tree's core lower-income demographic is struggling to expand purchases at all.

The labor market reality and the construction spending warning

Snider argues that the labor market is shifting from a "no hire, no fire" posture to "no hire and some fire." He points to Uber's layoff of 3,300 employees (10% of its workforce) as a sign that companies are prioritizing efficiency over growth. Official JOLTS data supports this, showing a drop in hiring to 5.1 million in July and a decline in voluntary quits, which signals low worker confidence in finding alternative employment.

The most overlooked confirmation of this weakness is construction spending. Private non-residential construction has entered a modest contraction, and residential spending has fallen sharply. Because construction requires long-term capital and confidence in future demand, this slowdown suggests that both businesses and households are unwilling to make long-term commitments. Snider notes that lower interest rates will not automatically revive construction if expected returns on projects continue to fall faster than financing costs.

Why energy shocks cause contraction, not inflation

Central bankers and mainstream economists often fear that rising energy costs will trigger an economy-wide inflationary spiral. However, Snider contends that in a fragile economy, an energy shock acts as a tax that destroys demand elsewhere.

Because consumers must pay more for unavoidable expenses like gasoline, they are forced to cut back on discretionary purchases. This strips pricing power from non-energy businesses, forcing them to offer promotions, accept thinner margins, and ultimately reduce hiring or cut hours. Rather than fueling sustained inflation, the current energy shock is driving the global economy toward contraction.
