Why investors keep getting trend following wrong

Summary

Patrick Welton, founder of Welton Investment Corporation and a pioneering trader since the Commodities Corporation era, sits down with NKL and Alan Dunn to challenge the dominant narratives around trend following. Rather than defending the strategy mechanically, Welton argues that the real problem is how the industry explains itself. Trend followers have drifted from their core edge—agnosticism across multi-asset classes—in pursuit of marketing narratives and client preferences. The conversation weaves together Welton's personal journey from medicine to trading, lessons from mentors like Paul Tudor Jones and John Henry, and a framework for understanding why trend following works that goes far beyond the usual pitch-deck explanations.

Five key insights

  1. The moral difference of managing outside money. When you take in client capital, their goals—not maximum risk-adjusted returns—become your primary obligation. This shapes everything from risk management to volatility targeting to the products you offer. Welton argues this distinction is rarely discussed but fundamentally reshapes how a manager should operate.

  2. Trend following's true edge comes from four return sources. Rather than treating trend as a monolithic black box, Welton decomposes returns into economic carry, financial carry, information diffusion, and reflexivity (feedback loops). Each operates on different timescales and in different market regimes. Understanding these sources helps explain why trend following works across diverse assets and why short-term trend following is much harder than medium-to-long-term versions.

  3. The narrative problem is worse than the mechanism problem. Managers have increasingly changed what they do in response to marketing feedback and client preferences, drifting from their original edge. Welton argues that the four S's (setup, signal, sizing, symmetry) define a disciplined trading approach, but they alone don't create conviction. Without a compelling story about why the returns exist, investors struggle to stay committed through drawdowns.

  4. Replication is mathematically valid but narratively dangerous. Welton separates the mathematical legitimacy of replicating trading returns (via regression or other techniques) from the business of selling replication as a hero's journey narrative. A $500 million institution can replicate trend-following returns more cheaply than buying a retail product, but the "beat the rapacious managers" story is more marketable than the math.

  5. Capacity constraints are real but often misdiagnosed. The key metric is execution costs and slippage, not absolute AUM. A trend follower with $200 billion managing the same way would have identical returns to one with $20 billion, provided they adjust for market liquidity. The real capacity problem emerges when managers try to add new methodologies to accommodate size without losing client trust—a reflexive trap between manager and allocator.

The Commodities Corporation years and early mentorship

Welton's path to trading began unconventionally. After studying medicine and meeting his future co-founder and wife Annette at UCLA, the couple started trading personally to make ends meet while pursuing medical careers. They were discovered by Commodities Corporation, a multi-strategy pod shop in the late 1980s that explicitly invested in developing trading talent. This environment exposed them to some of the industry's best minds.

The pivotal moment came around 1990 when Elaine Crocker, head of CC's trading, arranged a meeting with Paul Tudor Jones. Welton recalls Jones delivering two lessons that shaped his entire career. First, Jones emphasized that when trading size, "you can't only think about getting out when you want. You have to get out when the market lets you." This insight—that markets are composed of counterparties with their own edges—made Welton realize that market structure matters profoundly. Second, Jones joked about the bureaucratic overhead of scaling: "We have manuals for this, manuals for that, and now manuals for all the manuals." This taught Welton that building a professional firm required separating the trading mission from the business mission, a principle that became foundational to Welton Investment Corporation.

Later, Welton and Annette met John Henry, another legendary trader. Henry made a similar point about discipline: "The difference between our success and other people's is that we actually follow our system." He also noted that even if they printed their system on a billboard, no one would follow it because it wouldn't be theirs—a reminder that conviction and ownership are inseparable from edge.

From prop trading to managing outside money

The transition from proprietary trading to managing client capital is rarely analyzed for its moral and operational implications. Welton argues the biggest difference is not compliance or operations but a fundamental shift in whose goals matter most. A prop trader optimizes for risk-adjusted return on capital (RAROC), thinking in dollars. An asset manager must optimize for the client's goals, which may involve consistent exposure, targeted volatility, or proportional sizing based on forward return profiles.

This distinction led Welton to rethink product design. In the 1990s, Welton's main program was a diversified medium-term trend-following strategy with variable volatility (roughly 10–20 vol, averaging 15). By the early 2000s, after observing that professional allocators lost money three to four times faster than they made it, Welton realized the problem: rising volatility often accompanied rising correlation, causing net exposure to spike during drawdowns. The solution was volatility targeting, which many trend followers adopted to make their strategies more compatible with professional allocators' needs.

The four S's: a framework for systematic trading

Welton presents the four S's as a practical discipline for building a trading system:

  • Setup: Are you trading continuously or waiting for conditions that raise your probability of success (in a Bayesian sense)?
  • Signal: What makes you enter and exit? What's your decision rule?
  • Sizing: How much do you risk relative to opportunity and forward risk? This is synonymous with position sizing and risk management.
  • Symmetry: Do you enter trades with equal win-to-loss balance, or do you have an asymmetric advantage (either directly or synthetically)?

These four elements are necessary but not sufficient. The trader must deeply understand why they chose each answer and maintain conviction through market cycles. Welton notes that John Henry's insight applies here: even if you published your system, others wouldn't follow it because they lack the conviction that comes from building it themselves.

The narrative gap and why mechanism alone fails

Welton identifies a critical flaw in how trend followers market themselves: they lead with mechanism (the math, the formulas, the daily operations) rather than narrative. Managers and analysts dive into the mechanics—moving averages, volatility targeting, position sizing—but these explanations are "unemotional and unattached to any rationale for success." They are facts without reason. Welton argues that compelling matches between investors and managers rarely form based on "what do I do every single day, what math do I invoke?"

Instead, Welton proposes a layered narrative approach:

  1. The character layer: A story of a manager or team overcoming a challenge.
  2. The return-source layer: A clear decomposition of where returns actually come from.

For trend following, Welton has identified four return sources that explain its success across diverse assets and timeframes:

  • Economic carry: The benefit of holding positions through time in assets with positive expected returns (e.g., equity risk premium).
  • Financial carry: Benefits from term structure, roll premiums, and financing costs (e.g., bond roll premium).
  • Information diffusion: The gradual incorporation of new information into prices, creating trends.
  • Reflexivity: Feedback loops where buying begets buying, creating momentum.

Each source operates on different timescales and dominates in different markets. For instance, fixed-income trend returns are often dominated by term structure premium, while equities benefit more from economic carry and information diffusion. Short-term trend following relies more on reflexivity and information diffusion, which is why it's harder to implement profitably than medium-to-long-term approaches.

Welton uses a boat metaphor to explain this to investors: "A boat in the ocean moves because of currents, tides, wind, and engines. All could be in the same or opposite directions." This intuitive framing helps investors understand why trend following works across regimes and why it's not a prediction strategy.

The replication question: math versus narrative

Welton separates two distinct issues: the mathematical validity of replication and the business narrative around it. Mathematically, replication is legitimate. A trend follower doesn't trade primary cash bonds; they trade smoothed price signals and apply formulas. Replicating another trader's P&L via regression is not fundamentally different—it's just using P&L as a price signal. Index funds replicate the S&P 500 without owning all 500 stocks.

Welton's firm has studied replication and found that replicating the replicators (e.g., replicating trend-following ETFs) can actually yield better performance due to better smoothing and lower churn. For a $500 million institution, building a custom replication might be cheaper and more efficient than buying a retail product.

However, the business narrative around replication—the "hero's journey" of beating rapacious managers and democratizing access—is more marketable than the math. Welton notes this worked for Jack Bogle with index funds, but it stretches thin when applied to active ETFs and hedge fund replication. At some point, the narrative becomes about carving out a new niche rather than solving a genuine problem.

Why trend following works: beyond the usual explanations

Welton argues that trend following's resilience comes from its agnosticism. It doesn't predict; it responds to multi-asset, multi-directional price movements. As capital flows from one asset class to another, from long to short, from one sub-asset to another, trend followers capture the convexity and skew of these transitions. This is especially valuable on quarterly and semi-annual timescales.

However, Welton emphasizes that this edge depends on maintaining the original methodology. The minute managers respond to marketing feedback and client preferences by changing their approach, they dilute their edge. He cites the "four seminal questions" that investors used to ask traders:

  1. What's your edge?
  2. How and where will you exploit it?
  3. What's your risk management?
  4. How do you protect your tails?

These questions focus on edge, not on marketing narratives or product features. Welton argues that trend followers have drifted from this focus, and results have suffered as a consequence.

Regime shifts, humility, and the research process

Welton addresses a common misconception: that regime shifts require precise prediction. Instead, he argues for humility and continuous observation. Markets change—what data was important in the 1980s (money supply) is ignored today. What works in a rising-rate, supply-constrained world may not work in a demand-constrained, low-rate world.

Rather than trying to predict regimes, Welton advocates for building systems that are robust across regimes while remaining alert to changes. He distinguishes between:

  • Time-insensitive approaches: Systems that produce the same results whether you trade today or tomorrow. These are more robust to regime shifts and market microstructure changes.
  • Time-sensitive approaches: Systems that lose significant P&L if delayed by 15 minutes. These are more vulnerable to regime shifts and have less margin for error.

Welton notes that regime considerations can fool early quants. They often assume data changes drive market moves, but in reality, people see the data, interpret it, and then decide to buy or sell. The relationship between data and price is mediated by human behavior and attention, which shifts over time.

The capacity question: when is a trend follower too big?

Welton directly addresses the narrative that trend followers can become "too big" and lose their edge. He uses a thought experiment: if he bought all trend followers and CTAs (consolidating $200 billion of the $250 billion industry) but ran them independently with no consolidation, his performance would be identical. Size alone doesn't destroy edge; execution costs and market impact do.

The real capacity constraint is liquidity. A large volume of dollars moving into a small market quickly will cause slippage. The key metric is execution costs and slippage, measured objectively. If these metrics trend upward, there's a capacity problem. But this problem is market-specific and time-specific, not a blanket constraint on AUM.

Welton notes that managers face a reflexive trap: if they want to add new methodologies to accommodate size without losing clients, they must convince clients that this isn't style drift. This dialogue takes years, not quarters, and is harder when assets are flowing in rapidly.

On criticisms of trend following

Welton distinguishes between different types of criticism. Some, like Bill Sharpe's "why bother?" perspective, aren't really criticisms—they reflect a different investment philosophy. Others, like concerns about tail risk or correlation breakdowns, are legitimate but often misunderstood.

The criticism Welton most directly addresses is the claim that trend followers are "too big." He argues this narrative conflates size with capacity. A trend follower can be very large if they diversify their approaches and remain disciplined about execution costs. The real issue is whether they maintain their edge or drift into new strategies to accommodate growth.

The investor alpha problem

Welton concludes with a sobering observation: the biggest cost most investors face is not fees or market timing but "negative investor alpha"—the return gap from switching strategies based on performance. This is often larger than the cost of managing money itself. Steady handedness—the ability to stay committed to a strategy through cycles—is more important than any single return source.

This brings the conversation back to the narrative problem. If investors don't understand why trend following works, they're more likely to abandon it during drawdowns. A clear, compelling explanation of return sources—economic carry, financial carry, information diffusion, reflexivity—can help investors maintain conviction and avoid the costly mistake of switching at the wrong time.
