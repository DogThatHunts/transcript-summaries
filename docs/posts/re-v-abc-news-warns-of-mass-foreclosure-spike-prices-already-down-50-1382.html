The emerging wave of mortgage foreclosures

According to reports from ABC News, foreclosure filings across the United States are experiencing a significant upward trend. Foreclosures have risen 21% year-over-year and are up 28% from 2024. Between 2020 and 2025, foreclosure filings spiked by 71%. While these levels remain far below the peak of the 2008 Great Financial Crisis, RE-V highlights that they are already exerting downward pressure on home values, forcing distressed sellers to slash prices by up to 50% in certain pockets of the country.

Why FHA loans and recent buyers are driving the distress

RE-V points to Federal Housing Administration (FHA) loans—typically utilized by first-time homebuyers due to low down payment requirements—as the primary source of current mortgage distress.

  • Delinquency spikes: Excluding the anomalous pandemic spike, the serious delinquency rate for FHA mortgages has reached its highest level since 2014, hovering between 5% and 6%.
  • The 2022–2023 buyer trap: Active foreclosures are surging among newer vintage loans initiated between 2022 and 2026. Buyers who entered the market during this window purchased homes at peak bubble prices while taking on elevated mortgage rates of 6% to 7%.
  • Low financial resilience: Because of high purchase prices and interest rates, these buyers carry high monthly payments. RE-V notes that even minor financial disruptions, such as a job loss, quickly push these households into default because they lack sufficient savings to buffer the blow.

Extreme debt-to-income ratios rivaling the mid-2000s bubble

Data from the Fannie Mae National Mortgage Database reveals that the back-end debt-to-income (DTI) ratio for purchase mortgages surged to nearly 40% near the end of 2025. This means the average homebuyer with a mortgage is spending roughly 40% of their gross income on debt payments. RE-V emphasizes that these DTI ratios are actually higher than those recorded during the run-up to the 2005–2007 housing crash, underscoring how stretched modern buyers have become.

Geographic hotspots and the return of the 1% rule

Foreclosure activity is highly localized, concentrated primarily in lower- to middle-income neighborhoods rather than wealthy zip codes.

  • Florida leads the nation: Florida ranks first in yearly foreclosures per zip code, followed by South Carolina, Indiana, Nevada, Illinois, Delaware, Ohio, Texas, California, and North Carolina.
  • The Southeast epicenter: Map data shows deep red clusters of foreclosure activity around Atlanta, Myrtle Beach, Raleigh, and throughout central Florida.
  • Return of the 1% rule: The surge in distressed properties is creating opportunities for real estate investors. For example, a short-sale property in Decatur, Georgia, originally purchased for $300,000 in 2023, is now listed at $200,000. With an estimated rent of $2,200, the property yields a 1.1% rent-to-acquisition ratio. RE-V notes this is the first time since 2017–2018 that properties in these markets have met the investor "1% rule."

Historical context: is this a repeat of 2008?

RE-V addresses the debate between housing bulls and bears regarding how serious this foreclosure wave will become:

  • The bullish view: Conventional analysts and real estate brokers argue that because the overall serious delinquency rate (1%) and foreclosure rate (0.1% to 0.2%) are well below 2009 levels (where serious delinquencies hit 5%), the current trend is minor and nothing to worry about.
  • The bearish view (RE-V's perspective): RE-V argues that even though overall delinquency rates are historically low, they are already triggering six-figure losses for sellers in vulnerable areas. If delinquency rates rise even halfway to their 2009 levels, the resulting inventory could trigger widespread, severe price declines.
