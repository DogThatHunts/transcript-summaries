Elon Musk admits DOGE was a failure

Summary

PBoyle examines the rise and collapse of the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE), Elon Musk's brief and chaotic tenure as an unelected efficiency czar. Musk promised to cut $2 trillion from federal spending—nearly a third of the entire budget—but by May 2025, the department quietly disbanded with no savings to show. Treasury data reveals that federal spending actually increased by 4% in fiscal 2025 to $7.01 trillion, while the claimed "savings" relied on accounting tricks that counted theoretical maximum contract values rather than actual cash outflows. The project's collapse illustrates the gap between startup-culture disruption and the legal and structural constraints of government, alongside apparent conflicts of interest benefiting Musk's own companies.

Key insights

  1. The $2 trillion promise was mathematically impossible. With 65% of the federal budget locked into mandatory spending (Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid) and another 10% committed to debt service, only 25% of spending is discretionary—and half of that goes to defense. Musk was effectively promising to cut $2 trillion from a non-defense discretionary budget containing only ~$900 billion.

  2. DOGE used ceiling-value accounting to fabricate savings. Rather than counting actual cash spent, the department counted the maximum theoretical amount that could have been spent over a contract's lifetime. A migrant shelter contract claimed $2.9 billion in savings when the actual retainer being paid was only ~4% of that figure.

  3. The department was never a legal entity. DOGE was an advisory committee, not a real government department—Congress alone can create departments. Yet Musk bypassed normal separation of powers through executive orders and administrative pressure, firing staff and canceling contracts without legal authority to do so.

  4. Mass firings backfired operationally. Cutting IRS auditors to save salaries resulted in a projected $64 billion loss in tax revenue over a decade. The agency's cafeteria became a storage facility for unprocessed paper returns after digitization staff were laid off.

  5. The project may have cost taxpayers $135 billion. Accounting for paid leave for suspended workers, rehiring costs for wrongfully terminated staff, and lost productivity, the Partnership for Public Service estimated DOGE's disruptions exceeded any claimed savings.

The setup and the promise

PBoyle opens by contrasting the financial term "write-off"—a polite way to describe incinerated capital—with how Washington rebranded failure as the Department of Government Efficiency. Musk's candid post-mortem came in a podcast interview with Katie Miller, wife of Stephen Miller and a former Trump administration official who had worked for Musk in the private sector.

The spectacle began at Madison Square Garden in 2024, when financier Howard Lutnick invited Musk on stage and asked how much could be "ripped out" of the federal budget. Musk promised $2 trillion in cuts—nearly double Lutnick's off-script suggestion of $1 trillion. Trump called it "the Manhattan Project of our time," a comparison that proved prophetic: like the Manhattan Project, DOGE was shrouded in secrecy and driven by existential urgency, but unlike the physicists at Los Alamos, Musk's team discovered that the laws of statutory spending could not be rewritten on a whiteboard.

The accounting shell game

By February 2025, The Economist's data team began analyzing the Daily Treasury Statements—the federal government's daily checking account ledger. Despite DOGE's viral videos and promises of revolution, actual federal outlays averaged $30 billion per day, and spending had risen compared to the prior year. By May, the Financial Times confirmed the same finding: no drop in government spending.

When auditors examined DOGE's "wall of receipts"—a website supposedly documenting every dollar cut—they found it was load-bearing only in the sense that it held up a fantasy. Investigations by Politico and Futurism revealed that roughly $4 billion of claimed savings came from indefinite delivery vehicles (flexible framework contracts). As procurement expert Jessica Tillepman noted, claiming these as savings is equivalent to canceling a credit card with a $20,000 limit and claiming you've saved $20,000—you've simply destroyed your line of credit.

The most egregious example: a migrant children's shelter that had been empty for a year. DOGE claimed $2.9 billion in savings for canceling the contract, when the agency was only paying a small retainer to keep the facility on standby. Actual savings: roughly 4% of the headline figure.

Personnel chaos and conflicts of interest

Musk's strategy was to inject startup culture into the federal workforce by recruiting engineers and venture capitalists. Leading this charge was a 19-year-old named Edward Kurestin, who went by the nom de guerre "Big Balls." His previous qualifications included being fired from a cybersecurity internship for leaking secrets and reportedly providing tech support to a cybercrime gang that harassed FBI agents. DOGE parachuted him into the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and the Social Security Administration.

The IRS became the epicenter of DOGE's disruption. Musk installed his own acting commissioners, creating a revolving door where the agency averaged a new boss every month. The chaos culminated in a shouting match in the West Wing between Musk and Treasury Secretary Scott Besant over the firing of a MAGA-aligned whistleblower. Cutting IRS auditors to save salaries resulted in a projected $64 billion loss in tax revenue over the next decade.

PBoyle notes the apparent conflicts of interest: Musk's companies faced at least $2.37 billion in potential fines and investigations from the very federal agencies DOGE was targeting. DOGE pushed for deep cuts at the Federal Aviation Administration and National Highway Traffic Safety Administration—the specific regulators overseeing SpaceX rocket launches and Tesla's self-driving software. Meanwhile, Musk's satellite internet company, Starlink, seemed to navigate the chaos unscathed, and his team pushed to integrate private AI solutions (presumably modeled on his own technology) into the federal government's core systems.

USAID and the geopolitical cost

The one area where DOGE truly succeeded in slashing a department was USAID. Because USAID was created by Congress, DOGE lacked legal authority to abolish it, so they used executive power to fire staff and cancel contracts, effectively closing it by fiat. This move horrified foreign policy experts but was a political winner at home, driven partly by viral claims from Musk that the agency was sending $50 million in condoms to Hamas.

As it turned out, DOGE's efficiency experts had confused the Gaza Strip with the Gaza province in Mozambique, where the US funds HIV prevention programs. USAID had sent zero condoms to the Gaza Strip, but the correction came too late. Bill Gates told the Financial Times that "the picture of the world's richest man killing the world's poorest children is not a pretty one." Even Secretary of State Marco Rubio testified to Congress that the death toll from these abrupt cuts was "surely not zero."

The cancellations were so haphazard that DOGE's own website listed the details of over 3,000 canceled USAID contracts as "unavailable for legal reasons." While the cuts may have saved roughly $45 billion on paper (0.6% of federal spending), they cost the US significantly in soft power and global influence.

The silent dissolution

In November 2025, with eight months still left on its official mandate, Reuters contacted the Office of Personnel Management to check on DOGE's status. The OPM director gave a brutally efficient answer: "That doesn't exist." There was no press conference, no final report, no mission accomplished banner. The Manhattan Project of our time had become a non-centralized entity, its functions quietly absorbed back into the very HR departments it was meant to destroy. The super high-IQ small-government revolutionaries Musk had recruited were either let go or moved into the National Design Studio to work on beautifying government websites. The only thing DOGE successfully deleted was itself.

Why the mission was impossible

The premise itself was flawed. The US federal budget is not a discretionary slush fund. Roughly 65% of all spending is mandatory (locked in by law for Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid). Another 10% is interest on the national debt, which must be paid unless the US wants to default and trigger a global financial crisis. That leaves just 25% for discretionary spending, and half of that goes to the Pentagon. Since the Trump administration had vowed to increase defense spending, Musk was effectively promising to cut $2 trillion from a non-defense discretionary budget that only contained about $900 billion to begin with.

PBoyle notes that Musk could have fired every park ranger, air traffic controller, and FBI agent, shut down the Department of Justice, and turned off the lights in the White House, and he still would have missed his target by a trillion dollars.

The actual scorecard

When the fiscal year closed on September 30, 2025, the scoreboard was unambiguous. Total federal outlays reached $7.01 trillion, a 4% increase from the $6.75 trillion spent in 2024. Despite all the chainsaw waving, the wall of receipts, and the Manhattan Project rhetoric, the US government had managed to spend more money that year than the previous one.

The start of fiscal year 2026 appeared to show a breakthrough as federal spending collapsed in October, but this had nothing to do with DOGE. The decline was caused by a government shutdown that froze operations. Once the lights came back on, spending didn't just return to normal—it surged as agencies paid back pay to furloughed workers and processed delayed contracts. The November 2025 deficit spiked to $366 billion.

The Hamilton Project at the Brookings Institution built a daily tracker to monitor these flows, and their data confirms there was no durable reduction in aggregate outlays attributable to DOGE's actions. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget questioned whether the entire project was a distraction rather than an effective focus, as spending continued to climb materially.

Perhaps most damning: CBS Money Watch reported an estimate from the Partnership for Public Service suggesting that DOGE's disruptions—including paid leave for suspended workers, costs of rehiring wrongfully terminated staff, and lost productivity—may have actually cost taxpayers roughly $135 billion.

The broader context: debt and tariffs

The failure to cut the deficit is particularly painful when viewed against the backdrop of the national debt. US national debt has expanded massively and is growing as a percentage of GDP. When the government runs an unbalanced budget, spending $7 trillion while collecting far less in taxes, it has to borrow the difference. Payments on the national debt have surged to nearly $1 trillion per year—dead money that doesn't buy aircraft carriers, pave roads, or provide healthcare, but simply services the past.

In the absence of spending cuts, the administration has pivoted to tariffs. The government collected $195 billion in custom duties in full year 2025, a massive increase from the previous year. Supporters claim this windfall will pay down the national debt, but even with this historic surge, tariff revenue covers less than 3% of total federal spending. Worse still, the revenue stream may be temporary: the Supreme Court is currently hearing a challenge to the constitutionality of using emergency powers to impose these sweeping tariffs. If the court rules against the administration, the Treasury could be forced to refund up to $168 billion to businesses.

The existing mechanism that actually works

PBoyle notes that a mechanism to cut waste and fraud already exists and actually works: the False Claims Act, dating back to the Civil War. It allows private citizens (whistleblowers) to sue companies or individuals defrauding the government and keep a portion of recovered funds. This "eat what you kill" approach aligns incentives perfectly: the more fraud you find, the more you get paid.

Last year alone, the SEC and IRS paid out nearly $379 million to whistleblowers who flagged financial misconduct. This decentralized approach doesn't require "big balls" insults on Twitter—just a lawyer and a spreadsheet. But unlike DOGE, it's unglamorous, legal, and slow—qualities that don't play well at a campaign rally.

The cultural lesson: when lols meet the bond market

PBoyle suggests a broader cultural lesson. For the better part of a decade, markets and politics have been dominated by "lols"—the idea that irony, memes, and sheer audacity could override fundamentals. We saw it in Dogecoin, the meme stock mania, and in the creation of a government department named after a Shiba Inu meme. As Financial Times columnist Jemima Kelly noted recently, the joke just isn't funny anymore. When the lols meet the bond market, the bond market usually wins. The 10-year treasury yield does not care about your meme game; it cares about the debt-to-GDP ratio, and that ratio is not improving.

In his final days, Musk was asked if the project could survive without him. He replied with a koan: "Is Buddha needed for Buddhism?" A more fitting question for the bond vigilantes watching the US debt pile grow is this: "Is a calculator needed for accounting?" The answer is yes, and for now the calculator says that despite all the memes, the US government is still spending $30 billion a day.
