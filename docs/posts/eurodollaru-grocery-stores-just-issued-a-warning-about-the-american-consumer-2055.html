EurodollarU | Grocery Stores Just Issued a Warning About the American Consumer

A looming supermarket price war and plunging consumer expectations signal a fundamentally weak labor market, challenging mainstream narratives of a resilient, inflationary economy.

The looming grocery price war

Major retail and grocery chains, including Walmart, Kroger, Aldi, and Amazon, are positioning themselves for an aggressive price war. While lower food prices appear to be positive news for consumers, EurodollarU argues that this shift is driven by a weakening consumer base.

Walmart has doubled its typical discounting efforts by cutting prices on over 11,000 items. Additionally, the retail giant is deploying $2.9 billion in tariff refunds directly into lowering prices on household essentials rather than retaining the funds as profit. This aggressive pricing strategy indicates that even the largest low-cost retailer in the U.S. lacks the pricing power to pass rising costs onto its customers. Shoppers are still visiting stores but are spending less per trip, forcing supermarkets to compete fiercely for shrinking household budgets.

Plunging consumer expectations and recession signals

The Conference Board’s Expectations Index—which measures the six-month outlook for income, employment, and business conditions—dropped to 68.2 in August. Historically, any reading below 80 has indicated imminent recession risk.

This metric suggests that consumers are distinguishing between their current employment status and future security. While many still have jobs, they are increasingly anxious about their ability to replace those jobs if lost. This anxiety triggers defensive economic behaviors long before official recession data is declared: households postpone major purchases, trade down to cheaper brands, and limit discretionary spending.

The 'forgot-how-to-grow' economic model

EurodollarU challenges the traditional definition of a recession used by the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), which relies on historical patterns of sudden mass layoffs and immediate GDP collapse. Instead, the post-2007 economy frequently exhibits a 'forgot-how-to-grow' pattern characterized by a 'no-hire, no-fire' labor market:

  • Employer reluctance: Businesses are hesitant to expand or hire, but they are equally reluctant to lay off experienced workers they struggled to recruit.
  • Stagnant turnover: Job openings fall, hiring slows to near-zero, and workers stop quitting because finding alternative employment is unlikely.
  • Sideways contraction: The economy experiences a slow, fragile, sideways contraction rather than an abrupt collapse, which explains why traditional indicators have appeared to lag or conflict.

The reality of the labor market

While policymakers point to a 'resilient' official unemployment rate of 4.1%, EurodollarU argues that the underlying data tells a far weaker story:

  • Downward revisions: Initial payroll estimates have consistently overstated strength, with May and June figures revised downward by a combined 103,000 jobs.
  • Labor force dropouts: The household survey showed full-time employment falling and a significant number of individuals leaving the labor force. Because the official unemployment rate only counts active job seekers, these dropouts artificially stabilize the headline rate.
  • The effective unemployment rate: When accounting for an estimated 3.8 million missing workers who have exited the labor force, EurodollarU calculates the effective U.S. unemployment rate to be closer to 6.2%.

Retail confirmation beyond groceries

The consumer slowdown is not isolated to supermarkets. Dick's Sporting Goods recently experienced a 27% stock decline after lowering its outlook due to an increasingly 'promotional' sneaker market.

To move inventory, major footwear brands have resorted to direct-to-consumer discounting on their websites as cash-strapped shoppers reject high retail prices. This corporate struggle highlights the limits of pricing power; companies without the ability to raise prices must choose between accepting lower sales volumes or accepting lower profit margins. Neither outcome supports the mainstream narrative of sustained, demand-driven inflation.

Why supply shocks are disinflationary

EurodollarU explains that facing higher input costs (such as transportation, labor, or tariffs) does not automatically translate into sustained inflation. For inflation to persist, consumers must have the financial capacity to absorb higher prices repeatedly.

When consumers face stagnant wages and job insecurity, they respond to price hikes by cutting back. Consequently, supply shocks—like energy spikes or tariffs—ultimately act as disinflationary forces. They force consumers to spend more on absolute necessities, leaving less money for discretionary goods, which in turn triggers retail discounting, margin compression, and eventual hiring freezes.
