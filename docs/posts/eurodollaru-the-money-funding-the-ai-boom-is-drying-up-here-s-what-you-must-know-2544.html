The money funding the AI boom is drying up

Overview

EurodollarU examines the emerging cracks in the financial architecture supporting the AI infrastructure buildout. While major technology companies and specialized lenders continue to fund AI projects, the terms of that financing are deteriorating rapidly. Bond yields are climbing, private credit funds are facing redemption pressures, IPO valuations are being rejected, and an energy shock is compressing the economic case for massive data center investments. The analysis argues that the AI boom doesn't need to collapse—it only needs to slow—for markets to suffer significant repricing across technology, utilities, semiconductors, and real estate.

Key warning signs in the credit markets

SoftBank's bond offering signals tightening conditions. The Japanese conglomerate raised billions in dollar-denominated debt but had to offer yields approaching 8.9% for three-and-a-half-year bonds, 9.5% for five-and-a-half-year debt, and nearly 10% for seven-and-a-half-year tranches. These are the highest yields SoftBank has ever paid on dollar bonds with those maturities, placing the company in "junk bond territory." EurodollarU emphasizes that this represents a critical distinction: access to credit versus favorable access to credit. SoftBank found buyers, but only by offering extraordinary compensation for risk—a form of tightening that matters more than outright rejection.

Oracle's data center financing shows post-deal deterioration. Project Jupiter, Oracle's proposed 1,400-acre data center campus in New Mexico, secured approximately $18 billion in loans from a consortium of banks. However, the banks expected to distribute much of this debt into the broader market, and that distribution has stalled. Loans are now quoted around $0.89 to $0.91 on the dollar, implying investors want a substantial discount before assuming the risk. Oracle's corporate rating remains one notch above junk following an S&P downgrade, but the project debt is being priced far more skeptically than the parent company.

Private credit funds are hemorrhaging liquidity. Apollo Debt Solutions BDC, a $26 billion private credit fund, is limiting investor withdrawals for the third consecutive quarter. Investors requested redemptions of 14.7% of outstanding shares, but the fund will only repurchase 5%. While this represents an improvement from the previous quarter (when requests hit 16.8%), it still means investors asked for nearly three times the cash the fund intended to provide. Apollo received only $200 million in gross inflows during the quarter, and after paying roughly $700 million to repurchase shares, net outflows totaled approximately $500 million or 3% of net asset value. Other major funds including those operated by BlackRock, Cliffwater, and Blue Owl have also restricted withdrawals as investors continue to pull money from the direct lending market, estimated at around $2 trillion in size.

The information asymmetry problem

Conflicting default rate estimates undermine confidence. There is no consensus on the health of private credit portfolios. Fitch Ratings calculates a record default rate of 6.3% across roughly 1,300 US private credit borrowers. Houlihan Lokey, using a loan-size weighted basis, finds a default rate below 1% because the largest borrowers are still performing better. PIMCO calculates a so-called shadow default rate of 19% for roughly $500 billion held inside retail-oriented BDCs. These figures use different samples, weighting methodologies, and definitions of default, creating an "enormous range" that is itself a risk.

Maturity extensions and payment-in-kind (PIK) arrangements obscure true distress. When borrowers struggle, lenders may grant maturity extensions or convert cash interest payments into PIK arrangements, which add interest to the loan balance instead of paying the lender cash. Some observers treat PIK loans as performing because they haven't entered bankruptcy or stopped accruing interest. Others argue that "bad PIK"—introduced because a borrower can no longer handle its original cash payment—is functionally a default. This definitional disagreement explains how one analyst can report almost no defaults while another sees distress approaching 20%. Extensions and PIK don't create liquidity; they create growing mistrust and postpone recognition while keeping cash trapped inside borrowers.

The energy shock compounds the problem

Higher energy costs attack the AI investment case from multiple directions. Data centers require enormous quantities of power continuously, not only during construction but throughout their operating lives. Rising energy costs increase both the operating expenses of existing facilities and the cost of building new generation and transmission capacity. They can also increase political opposition as data centers compete with households and other businesses for electricity. All of this adds to increasingly tightened credit market conditions, particularly problematic because many AI projects were built around assumptions of endlessly abundant, cheap financing.

The yield curve signals demand destruction concerns. The difference between two-year and ten-year treasury yields has narrowed to only about 18 basis points, its flattest level since early 2025. The bond market is shifting to worrying about demand destruction. Higher energy prices function like a tax on consumers and businesses, forcing households to spend more on necessities and leaving less for discretionary spending. Corporate margins get squeezed, investment projects become harder to justify, and defaults become more likely. This macroeconomic downside is what stock investors are beginning to recognize.

The fragility of the financing structure

The scale of AI spending has created systemic dependence on external funding. Until recently, the largest technology companies could fund most AI investments using cash produced by their core businesses. But ambitions have expanded faster than internal cash generation. According to Bloomberg estimates cited by EurodollarU, Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, and Meta could collectively spend more than a trillion dollars on capital expenditures next year alone. Short and long-term commitments involving leases, energy, and other obligations may total almost two and a half trillion dollars, much of it related to AI. At that scale, AI no longer depends on only the balance sheets of four highly profitable companies. It depends on banks, bond funds, private credit vehicles, utilities, power developers, infrastructure investors, and public equity buyers all continuing to provide unlimited funds on favorable terms.

Delayed IPOs expose the exit problem. SB Energy, another SoftBank subsidiary proposing to build an enormous data center project in Ohio, reportedly planned to launch an IPO this month but has delayed it as bankers struggle to find sufficient demand at the company's desired valuation of about $50 billion or more. Holtec, which owns nuclear power assets and develops small reactors that could supply data centers, paused its IPO indefinitely, specifically citing uncertainty surrounding data center development as a factor impairing investor confidence. Holtec had hoped to raise as much as $900 million at a valuation of up to $10 billion. Agreco, a power company that has emphasized data centers as a major revenue source, has also reportedly slowed its offering schedule. These delays expose a critical vulnerability: private investors can support enormous projects on the assumption that public investors will eventually provide them an exit. If the IPO market refuses the desired valuation, that capital remains locked for longer, and the company may have to accept a lower price, raise more expensive debt, or delay construction.

Stock market repricing of private credit managers signals broader concerns. Business development companies and shares of major alternative asset managers, including BlackRock, BlueOwl, and Blackstone, are falling again, in some cases back toward their early July lows. The public markets are doing what private markets do more slowly: repricing uncertainty. If investors suspect reported loan values are too optimistic, publicly traded BDCs can trade well below the value of the company's reported assets. As investors expect managers to earn lower management fees, experience slower fundraising, or see weaker performance income, asset manager stocks can fall even when the current portfolio remains profitable. When investors see these funds appeal to repeated redemption limits, they easily conclude that future retail inflows are becoming more difficult to attract. This matters because the alternative asset management business was valued partly on expectations of persistent growth. More assets under management generated more fees; strong reported returns attracted more capital; that capital funded more loans, which generated more income and performance fees. But the same mechanism works in reverse: the stock market declines represent investors pricing the strong possibility that private credit has reached the downswing of its growth cycle.

How a credit cycle changes

The transition is gradual, not catastrophic. EurodollarU argues that credit cycles don't necessarily change through one spectacular failure or "Lehman moment," but through a sequence of increasingly expensive negotiations. Higher yields, lower prices, delayed IPOs, extended maturities, restricted withdrawals, and capital that remains technically available but only on terms that make the original investment thesis much harder to achieve—these are the mechanisms of tightening. SoftBank can borrow, but near 10%. Oracle can finance data centers, but banks may be left holding heavily discounted debt. Private credit funds can offer periodic liquidity, but not enough to satisfy every investor who wants out. AI companies can plan public offerings, but they may not receive the valuations needed to complete them. Stocks tied to private markets can report stable asset values while their shareholders drastically mark down the businesses in real time.

The biggest threat may not be technological failure. The analysis concludes that the biggest threat to the AI boom is not necessarily that the technology fails, but that the marketplace no longer provides every single dollar at the price being asked. Just a little friction from the credit cycle downturn would be enough to slow the massive AI infrastructure buildout that is "priced to beyond perfection." A slowdown in construction would reduce capital spending, lower expected power demand, and reset revenue forecasts across semiconductors, utilities, construction, cooling, networking, real estate, and technology. Because the stock market has become so concentrated around AI expectations, that reset would extend far beyond a handful of big tech companies.
