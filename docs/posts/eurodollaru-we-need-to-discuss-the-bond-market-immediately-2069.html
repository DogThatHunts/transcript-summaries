The 30-year yield surge: regime change or noise?

Jeff Snider (Eurodollar University) addresses the recent surge in the US 30-year Treasury yield, which crossed 5% to reach its highest level since 2007. While mainstream commentators interpret this as a fundamental "regime change" driven by structural inflation and economic resilience, Snider argues that nominal yields alone are highly misleading. To understand what the bond market is actually signaling, investors must look beyond nominal rates to the shape of the yield curve, inflation expectations, and derivative markets.

The 2008 precedent: inflation fears vs. deflationary reality

Snider draws a parallel to the summer of 2008, when the European Central Bank (ECB) raised interest rates and Federal Reserve Chairman Ben Bernanke warned of potential rate hikes due to surging oil prices.

  • The Fed risk: The two-year Treasury yield rose by 170 basis points off its lows as the market priced in the risk of central bank action.
  • The curve's warning: Despite nominal rate increases, the yield curve was flattening dramatically. This flattening signaled that the market did not see long-term inflation risk, but rather "Fed risk"—the danger of a central bank policy error.
  • The outcome: The Fed and ECB misread the economic landscape, focusing on transitory oil-driven inflation right before the onset of the Great Recession.

The 2018 parallel: the "nowhere to go but up" fallacy

A more precise parallel to the current market occurred in 2018, when the two-year Treasury reached its highest level since 2008 and the 10-year Treasury hit a seven-year high.

  • The consensus: Prominent figures like Bill Gross declared the end of the 30-year bond bull market, while JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon predicted the 10-year yield would reach 5%. The prevailing narrative was that a roaring economy and rising inflation meant interest rates had "nowhere to go but up."
  • The hidden signals: While nominal yields rose, underlying market indicators told a different story. TIPS break-evens remained anchored in their disinflationary range, interest rate swap spreads stopped decompressing and turned negative, and Eurodollar futures inverted in June 2018.
  • The reversal: Within seven months of hitting its multi-year high in November 2018, the 10-year Treasury yield plunged to record lows in 2019. The market had successfully priced in the Fed's policy error and subsequent pivot.

Deconstructing current market indicators

Snider applies these historical lessons to the current environment (noted in the transcript as 2026), demonstrating that the same divergence is occurring today:

  • TIPS break-evens: The TIPS market remains highly resolute about the lack of long-term inflation risk, eliminating structural inflation as the primary driver of higher yields.
  • Swap spreads: Interest rate swap spreads remain low and are compressing in the near term, which is historically inconsistent with a roaring economy or structural inflation.
  • SOFR futures "frown": Similar to the Eurodollar futures inversion of 2018, the "frown" in Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR) futures indicates that the market expects short-term rates to fall in the future.
  • Yield curve flattening: The two-year Treasury has risen significantly faster than the 10-year or 30-year yields, leading to severe curve compression. This flattening indicates that the market is pricing in near-term Fed hikes but expects them to be unsustainable.

Conclusion: pricing a policy mistake

The surge in the 30-year long bond is not a signal of structural inflation, bond vigilantism, or a fundamental regime change. Instead, it represents the market pricing in "Fed risk"—the high probability that the Federal Reserve is misreading economic indicators and raising rates into a weakening economy. Just as in 2008 and 2018, nominal yields are being temporarily dragged up by the front end of the curve, but the broader marketplace is preparing for rates to eventually fall.
