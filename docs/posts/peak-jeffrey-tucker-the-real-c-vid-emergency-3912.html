The 2005 pandemic plan

Tucker recounts how President George W. Bush convened experts in 2005 to address avian flu fears. Two camps emerged: traditional epidemiologists like D.A. Henderson, who advocated treatment and minimal social disruption, and a newer group led by computer modelers who proposed mapping out lockdowns, school closures, and six-foot distancing. Bush favored the latter's PowerPoint presentation. The resulting plan, which Tucker read at the time, struck him as "worse than anything ever conjured up in Khrushchev's Russia." He warned then that implementing such a plan would destroy society and government credibility—warnings he published but which went largely unheeded.

January 2020: early signals

Tucker says he became alert to COVID in the third week of January 2020, having spent 15 years writing about pandemic planning. He suspected the virus might be used as a pretext to implement the 2005 plan. By late February, he identified a turning point: the New York Times Daily podcast featuring virus reporter Donald G. McNeil, who arrived at the studio spraying hydrogen peroxide and warned of needing to "go medieval" on the virus, evoking the 1918 flu. The next day, op-eds by McNeil and Peter Daszak (head of EcoHealth Alliance) appeared simultaneously in the Times, signaling to Tucker that "it was a done deal."

Fauci's two-part strategy

Fauci's response: "If it's not a bad flu season, we're no worse off."

Plan B (after lab origin became public): Deny the lab leak by funding academics to publish a false zoonotic origin story in The Lancet; immediately begin vaccine development; use lockdowns to suppress natural immunity and create a vaccination window; and suppress early treatments like ivermectin and hydroxychloroquine.

The V-safe data and vaccine injuries

Tucker describes the V-safe program, a CDC-run real-time safety monitoring system that collected over 10 million reports. The CDC never analyzed the data and eventually shut down the program. After years of FOIA litigation, the Informed Consent Action Network obtained the free-text fields, revealing thousands of injury reports. Tucker worked with AI tools to reorganize the data into a 1,048-page book titled Cries for Help. He attempted to read the book live but stopped after ten hours, describing emotional and psychological disorientation from the accumulated testimony of harm.

Tucker argues the data shows the vaccines were the most dangerous ever released, with no offsetting benefit, and that the suppression of this information—including Facebook's removal of vaccine injury support groups—was deliberate and coordinated.

Hayek's "why the worst get on top"

Tucker invokes F.A. Hayek's analysis of totalitarian systems. Hayek argues that totalitarian planning fails because it defies decentralized knowledge and individual decision-making. When failure becomes obvious, rather than admit error, the system rewards those best at lying. Over time, society elevates liars and punishes truth-tellers, creating a "wicked, bloody, persistent, but compelling lie" as the organizing principle. Tucker sees this dynamic in COVID: daily lies from media, government, and academia that no normal person would tolerate, yet persist.
