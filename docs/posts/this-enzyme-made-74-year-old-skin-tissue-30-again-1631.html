This Enzyme Made 74-Year-Old Skin Tissue 30 Again

A newly engineered bacterial enzyme successfully reverses a major driver of cellular aging in human tissue, offering a promising future for longevity science.

For decades, textbooks taught that glycation—the process where sugar molecules bond to proteins and stiffen tissues—was entirely irreversible. This molecular damage accumulates over time as advanced glycation end products (AGEs), with carboxymethyllysine (CML) acting as one of the most abundant. However, a groundbreaking study has challenged this dogma by engineering an enzyme capable of stripping CML from aged tissues.

The engineering of CMLase

In a study published in Nature Communications (July 2026), researchers set out to find an enzyme that could target CML bound to proteins. They screened over 44,000 enzyme structures, eventually identifying a weak glycine oxidase from the bacterium Chalidithermos roseus that possessed a spacious active site.

Through five rounds of directed evolution and screening over 500 million modified variants, they engineered "CMLase." This optimized enzyme features 15 amino acid changes and a two-amino-acid deletion, allowing it to efficiently convert damaged CML back into normal, healthy lysine.

Promising tissue results and critical limitations

When tested overnight on preserved human tissues, CMLase yielded dramatic results:

  • Aorta tissue: CML staining in a 75-year-old donor's aorta fell by over 70%.
  • Skin tissue: CML levels in a 74-year-old donor's skin fell by over 55%, dropping below the baseline levels of a 31-year-old donor.
  • Eye lens: Direct mass spectrometry showed a 45% reduction in CML in a 64-year-old donor's lens.

Despite these exciting findings, several major caveats remain:

  • In vitro only: The experiments were conducted on preserved tissues in a dish, not in living organisms with active immune systems and blood flow.
  • Small sample size: The human tissue tests relied on single donors (N=1).
  • No functional data: The study did not measure whether the treated tissues regained elasticity or improved in physiological function.
  • Immunogenicity risks: Injecting a foreign bacterial enzyme into a living human could trigger a severe immune response.
  • Incomplete targeting: CMLase only targets CML and does not address other major cross-linking AGEs, such as glucosepane.

Practical strategies to minimize glycation today

While CMLase is not yet available as a therapy, individuals can take immediate steps to reduce the formation and accumulation of AGEs:

  • Optimize blood sugar regulation: High blood glucose accelerates glycation. Managing hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) is the most critical factor. Engaging in resistance training 3 to 4 times a week helps muscles absorb excess blood glucose, while time-restricted eating can keep baseline insulin levels low.
  • Modify cooking techniques: Dry, high-heat cooking methods (such as grilling, frying, barbecuing, and roasting) generate high levels of dietary AGEs, particularly in fatty, protein-rich animal foods. Opt instead for moist-heat methods like steaming, boiling, poaching, or stewing.
  • Incorporate protective herbs and antioxidants: Cooking meats with culinary herbs like rosemary, thyme, and parsley can inhibit AGE formation. Pairing high-heat foods with antioxidant-rich vegetables also supports the body's defense systems.
  • Utilize targeted supplements: Supplementing with glycine (or marine collagen, which is rich in glycine) has been shown to improve insulin sensitivity, lower post-meal glucose spikes by up to 50%, and reduce HbA1c levels.
