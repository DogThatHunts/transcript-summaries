The five-question Checklist from LIGO

Petro411 distills the evidence validation process used by MIT and Caltech researchers at LIGO into five practical questions that anyone can apply to strengthen their thinking.

Question 1: did something genuinely independent confirm it?

People often assume that multiple sources agreeing on a claim makes it true. But this only holds if the sources are truly independent.

  • If sources all copied the same mistake, they don’t offer independent confirmation.
  • LIGO used two detectors 3,000 kilometers apart—Livingston, Louisiana, and Hanford, Washington—to ensure observations weren’t dependent.
  • A signal detected in one location must be matched by a similar one in the other, with a delay consistent with the speed of light.
  • The key insight: trace each judgment back to its origin and assess whether interactions could have made the sources dependent.

Question 2: could I have helped create the result?

Even well-intentioned procedures can unintentionally produce the result they claim to discover.

  • LIGO tested its system with hardware injections—artificial signals inserted into the data.
  • A 2010 injection called 'Big Dog' survived months of analysis before being revealed as a test.
  • When the 2015 signal appeared, researchers first asked whether an injection had occurred.
  • This highlights a common flaw: people are good at detecting external distortion but poor at asking whether their own methods helped produce the result.
  • Examples include:
  • A survey that uses a leading language to shape opinions.
  • Monitoring behavior that alters the very behavior being measured.
  • Selecting a metric after seeing results, which builds the conclusion into the analysis.

Question 3: could the instrument or environment have created it?

All evidence passes through a measuring system that may distort or omit reality.

  • LIGO monitored 200,000 auxiliary measurements for ground movement, weather, equipment glitches, and more.
  • No disturbance could produce the signal at both sites.
  • The signal was not just detected—it was validated by checking for instrumental or environmental interference.
  • Apply this to your own tools:
  • A dashboard measuring 'engagement' may have changed definitions or recording methods.
  • Ask: What does the measurement include or exclude? When does it produce false positives?
  • Would a different instrument or metric represent the same situation differently?

Question 4: does the same conclusion appear using another method?

A single method is vulnerable to bias. Convergent results from different approaches strengthen validity.

  • LIGO used multiple search pipelines, each with different models and assumptions.
  • Different methods recovered the same event with high significance.
  • This reflects 'convergent validity'—a concept from outside physics—where a conclusion survives different, non-identical methods.
  • If four analysts use the same assumptions, their agreement just repeats the same weakness.
  • If a conclusion holds across different datasets, models, and methods, it is far more robust.
  • Ask:
  • What else could explain these facts?
  • Which assumption carries most of the result?
  • What happens when I change it?
  • What methods could I use to test this?

Question 5: how often could noise or randomness produce this kind of result?

Even if a signal looks real, it could be a statistical fluke.

  • LIGO didn’t just compare the signal to predictions.
  • They tested how often random noise could produce a coincident signal between the two detectors.
  • By shifting the timing of the data, they built a background of accidental coincidences.
  • Their analysis estimated a false alarm rate below one event in 203,000 years—equivalent to 5.1 sigma significance.
  • The key principle: How surprising would this evidence be if the explanation were wrong?
  • A person who makes many specific predictions will eventually appear prophetic by chance.
  • Count the number of opportunities for randomness to produce a success.
  • The more opportunities, the less meaningful one success becomes.

A note on the Checklist

  • These five questions are Petro411’s practical summary, not a direct copy of a MIT document.
  • The actual LIGO process was more extensive and involved multiple working groups.
  • The original checklist was compiled into a version-controlled Google spreadsheet for transparency and reproducibility.
  • The goal is not just to find truth—but to make thinking visible, inspectable, and repeatable.
