Why some people become successful: beyond the myths

The three popular theories of success

Shobhan begins by presenting three influential psychological frameworks for understanding success:

Walter Mischel's marshmallow test (Columbia University) tracked children who could delay gratification by resisting eating a marshmallow to earn two instead. Over 50 years, Mischel found that those who waited had better school performance, higher test scores, more stable careers, healthier relationships, and avoided jail, drugs, and alcohol. The theory: success requires delayed gratification and self-control—the ability to sacrifice short-term pleasure for long-term gain.

Carol Dweck's growth mindset (Stanford University) distinguishes between those who see failure as a learning opportunity and those who give up. Those with a growth mindset try harder after failure; those with a fixed mindset assume they lack capacity to improve. The theory: success requires resilience—the ability to bounce back and persist.

K. Anders Ericsson's deliberate practice identifies that successful musicians and athletes don't just work hard; they work strategically. They set goals, examine their weaknesses, create improvement plans, and adjust those plans based on results. The theory: success requires self-assessment and self-reflection—constant evaluation of your own learning.

The critical flaw: correlation is not causation

Here Shobhan introduces a crucial distinction that undermines all three theories. Just because successful people wake up early, practice deliberately, or show resilience does not mean that doing these things causes success. The causation runs the other way: if you are already successful, you will naturally exhibit these behaviors.

A poor student taught self-control, resilience, and self-assessment will not automatically improve. The reason: these traits are symptoms of success, not causes of it. Macroeconomic studies show that parental wealth matters far more than school performance. If your parents are rich, you will likely succeed; if they are poor, you likely will not—regardless of how well you do in school.

The real difference: parenting strategies shaped by class

Shobhan identifies three major differences in how rich and poor parents raise their children:

1. Vocabulary and communication Rich parents speak to their children using higher vocabulary and longer sentences. Poor parents use minimal language ("no," "yes," "go away").

2. Attitude and authority Rich parents use a friendly, explanatory approach. When a rich child touches a hot stove, the parent explains why fire is dangerous and how to avoid it in the future. Poor parents use command-based discipline: "Don't you ever do that again or I'll beat the crap out of you!" This creates fundamentally different worldviews—rich children learn the world is safe and they are respected; poor children learn the world is scary and adults are to be feared.

3. Stability vs. volatility Rich parents can afford to keep promises. They say "next week we'll go to Thailand" and follow through. Poor parents struggle with money and cannot reliably keep promises ("We'll go to McDonald's, but my paycheck isn't enough"). This teaches rich children to trust authority; poor children learn that promises are unreliable.

Reinterpreting the marshmallow test

The marshmallow test is not actually a test of self-control—it is a test of trust in authority. A child who believes the experimenter will return and keep their promise will wait. A child from an unstable background, where promises are routinely broken, rationally eats the marshmallow immediately. Poor children are not lacking self-control; they are responding logically to their lived experience of unreliability.

Similarly, resilience depends on believing the world will help you. Rich children, surrounded by support, can afford to fail and try again. Poor children, lacking that safety net, rationally conclude that failure means they should not attempt that path. And self-assessment is difficult when you live under chronic stress—looking inward only surfaces pain and trauma.

The hierarchy: two different games

Shobhan argues that society is fundamentally divided into two worlds that barely intersect. The critical difference is not intelligence or work ethic but the game each class plays:

Poor people must obey authority. They navigate police, bosses, and family by accepting commands, not questioning, and keeping their heads down. Challenging authority risks jail, job loss, or family rejection.

Rich people negotiate with authority. They debate, argue, and present evidence to determine outcomes. This teaches them to see themselves as agents who can shape their world.

Poor parents teach obedience because it is the optimal survival strategy in their world. Rich parents teach negotiation because it is the optimal strategy in theirs. Neither is wrong—each is rational for its context. But this means rich children are trained from birth to advocate for themselves and push boundaries, while poor children are trained to comply.

Why schools cannot fix this

Shobhan notes that schools have tried to teach self-control, resilience, and self-assessment to poor students. It doesn't work. They have tried to create school environments with more vocabulary, friendly teachers, and stability. It helps marginally but doesn't close the gap. The reason: children's worldviews are largely formed before school, shaped by years of parenting. And attempting to change parenting styles fails because poor parents are not being irrational—they are optimizing for survival in their actual environment. Asking them to parent like rich people would alienate them from their community and family.

The system is self-reinforcing: the rich stay rich, and the poor stay poor.

The Dunning-Kruger effect and self-awareness

Shobhan illustrates this with the Dunning-Kruger effect: when 500 university students took an IQ test and estimated their performance, those in the top 5% underestimated themselves (thinking they were in the top 20%), while those in the bottom 5% overestimated themselves (thinking they were average or top 50%). The hardest part of being a student is assessing yourself accurately. Those who lack capacity often lack the capacity to know they lack it—which helps explain why people in power are often confident despite being incompetent.

How poor kids can succeed: the exception, not the rule

When asked how poor children can escape poverty, Shobhan shares his own story: he immigrated to Canada as a poor child, but succeeded only by leaving his community and obtaining a scholarship to study in the United States. This required extreme individualism and high risk tolerance—traits most people don't have.

Other historical mechanisms of social mobility include war (get promoted if you excel), revolution, and marrying up. But these are all high-risk. Shobhan emphasizes: luck is real, and it matters. Poor people who succeed often attribute it to hard work and talent, but luck—being in the right place, meeting the right person—is essential. You can strategically position yourself to increase your chances of luck, but the odds remain against you. Success from poverty is the exception, not the rule.

Why societies collapse: elite overproduction and revolution

Shobhan shifts to explaining social change. If the system is so stable and self-reinforcing, why do revolutions happen?

The answer lies in elite overproduction. Rich people are taught to maximize their outcomes and compete for power. But power is a zero-sum game—only a few can be at the top. Over time, too many ambitious people compete for too few positions. Frustrated elites who cannot rise further align with poor people, promising to cancel debts, redistribute land, and end slavery. This is the pattern of every revolution in history.

The Chinese Revolution was not poor versus rich; it was rural elites (Mao, Zhou Enlai, Deng Xiaoping) versus urban elites. The Islamic Revolution promised debt cancellation and land distribution. Julius Caesar promised to free Romans from debt and slavery. Donald Trump's appeal rests on similar promises to Americans drowning in credit card, student loan, and mortgage debt.

The three seeds of revolution

Over time, inequality creates three problems:

  1. Indebtedness: The rich monopolize wealth, so the poor must borrow. Interest rates trap them in perpetual debt.
  2. Slavery: When debtors cannot repay, their bodies become collateral—and their children inherit the debt.
  3. Landlessness: The poor lose their land and means of survival.

When a majority has no incentive to live, an ambitious elite can mobilize them by promising debt cancellation, land redistribution, and freedom from slavery. This is the revolutionary formula.

The cycle of social mobility and closure

Shobhan concludes with a paradox: social mobility is the best form of governance. Whether a society is democratic or communist, capitalist or command-based, people work hard when they believe effort leads to advancement. America in the 1950s and China in the 1950s both prospered because both offered social mobility.

But this never lasts. The talented people who rise to power want their children to inherit their positions. They rig the system so only their children can succeed. Positions fill up, waiting lists form, and talented outsiders find the ladder pulled up. At this point, the only option is revolution—a "game reset" that restores mobility and allows the cycle to begin again.

This is the pattern of human history: either you allow people to climb the ladder, or they break the game entirely.

The rigged system today

Schools exemplify this rigging. Rich schools offer freedom and creativity; poor schools offer rigid discipline. The system is deliberately designed to ensure certain people succeed and everyone else fails. This is not a bug—it is the feature, the inevitable result of those in power protecting their children's inheritance.
