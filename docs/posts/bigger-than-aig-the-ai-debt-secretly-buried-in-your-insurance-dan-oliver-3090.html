Bigger Than AIG: the AI Debt Secretly Buried in your Insurance

In this conversation, Dan Oliver of Myrmikan Capital argues that the artificial intelligence boom is fundamentally a debt crisis rather than a stock story. He explains how zero-interest-rate policies forced life insurance companies to seek higher yields, leading private equity firms to acquire these insurers and direct their capital into structured, highly risky AI infrastructure debt. Oliver warns that because AI productivity gains are unlikely to materialize quickly enough to service these massive liabilities, a wave of defaults is inevitable, which will ultimately force another round of Federal Reserve bailouts and severe currency devaluation.

Key insights

  • The AI boom is a debt story, not an equity story: Oliver argues that hundreds of billions of dollars are being funneled into chips, data centers, and take-or-pay contracts, largely off-balance-sheet and funded by private credit.
  • Private equity has captured life insurance balance sheets: To escape low-yield environments, life insurance companies partnered with or were acquired by private equity firms, which now direct $11 trillion in insurance assets toward high-yield, structured AI debt.
  • AI productivity may not justify the debt in time: Historical precedents like the telecom and railroad bubbles show that revolutionary technologies often fail to generate the short-term cash flow required to pay back the debt incurred to build them.
  • The Fed cannot easily bail out AI debt directly: Unlike the 2008 mortgage crisis where printed money directly supported home solvency, printing money to bail out failed AI data centers does not generate natural economic cash flow.
  • Geopolitical competition with China drives reckless spending: Oliver notes that some Washington policymakers view the AI build-out as a national security imperative, arguing that winning the "AI war" against China supersedes concerns about financial stability.

The anatomy of the AI debt bubble

Oliver explains that every major technological breakthrough over the last three centuries—from canals in the 1830s and railroads in the 1870s to telecom broadband in the 2000s—follows a predictable pattern of overinvestment. Driven by artificially low interest rates set by governments, capital investment appears cheaper than it is, leading to massive overcapacity. In the current AI cycle, hundreds of billions of dollars are being poured into data centers and hardware. However, Oliver argues that the actual profits required to service this debt have yet to materialize, setting the stage for widespread defaults where equity holders are wiped out and debt holders are forced to take massive haircuts.

How private equity captured life insurance assets

A key puzzle of the AI boom is where the credit is originating, given that traditional banks are not heavily lending to these projects. Oliver traces the source back to the Federal Reserve's post-2008 zero-interest-rate policies, which squeezed life insurance companies holding long-term liabilities. Desperate for yield, these insurers turned to private equity (PE) firms. Recognizing an opportunity, major PE firms began acquiring "captive" insurance companies to gain control of their massive balance sheets—part of an $11 trillion industry. These PE firms now package risky AI infrastructure debt into structured products and direct their captive insurance arms to buy them, bypassing traditional risk-retention regulations and creating a systemic moral hazard reminiscent of AIG in 2008.

The limits of productivity and historical precedents

While proponents argue that AI is a unique technology that justifies unlimited investment, Oliver points to historical data showing that technological revolutions do not automatically translate into rapid productivity growth. For instance, US unit productivity growth actually halved during the computer age (1980–2000) compared to the pre-computer era (1950–1980). He also compares the AI hype to the catalog industry bubble of the 1910s, where overprinting by the Fed led to predictions that physical retail would vanish within a decade, only for the industry to collapse when monetary tightening occurred. Oliver suggests that while AI will ultimately benefit humanity, the current crop of investors writing the checks will likely be wiped out before the technology becomes self-sustaining.

Geopolitics and the "national security" defense of AI spending

Oliver reveals that during discussions with national security-oriented individuals in Washington, D.C., policymakers expressed indifference toward the financial risks of the AI debt bubble. According to these officials, the United States is in an existential technological war with China, and the nation that achieves artificial general intelligence first will win. Under this framework, the massive, messy financing of data centers is treated as a military necessity. Oliver criticizes this view, arguing that using state-directed capital and mass surveillance modeling to defeat China effectively mimics the communist economic model the US is trying to oppose.

Implications for the Federal Reserve, inflation, and gold

When the AI debt structure inevitably breaks, Oliver predicts the fallout will start with small, obscure insurance companies before spreading. Unlike 2008, where Fed bailouts directly stabilized mortgages because people prioritized housing payments, bailing out empty data centers will not generate organic cash flow. Oliver warns that the Fed's inevitable response—printing trillions of dollars—will trigger severe domestic inflation. This inflationary pressure will be compounded by the Biden administration's decision to weaponize the dollar system against Russia, which shrank the global pool of capital willing to absorb US dollar inflation. Consequently, Oliver views this looming crisis as highly bullish for gold, though he cautions that gold may experience short-term liquidity-driven volatility before slingshotting higher.
