EurodollarU | Japan Declared War on the Yen Carry Trade… It Won’t End Well

Why Japan's attempt to stop the yen carry trade by raising interest rates is backfiring, as domestic capital continues to flee a stagnant economy.

In this conversation, Jeff Snider and Steve Van Metre of Eurodollar University dissect the mechanics of the Japanese yen carry trade, arguing that the mainstream focus on foreign hedge funds is a distraction from the real issue: domestic capital flight. They explain that massive Japanese institutions, such as pension funds and life insurers, are systematically moving capital out of Japan in search of better risk-adjusted returns. Paradoxically, the Bank of Japan's (BOJ) rate hikes have only weakened the yen further by destabilizing the domestic government bond market and triggering a buyer strike. Ultimately, the hosts argue that until Japan addresses its structural growth deficit and creates genuine domestic investment opportunities, any policy interventions or rate hikes will fail to rescue the currency.

The myth of the hedge fund carry trade

Jeff Snider argues that the mainstream financial media presents a "cartoonish" version of the yen carry trade, depicting it as foreign hedge funds borrowing cheap yen to aggressively buy U.S. Treasuries. While opportunistic speculators do piggyback on the currency's decline, they do not possess the capital required to drive a sustained, long-term devaluation of the yen.

Instead, the true carry trade is driven by massive pools of domestic Japanese savings. Institutional giants, such as pension funds and insurance companies, are systematically shifting capital out of the country. This capital flight is a rational response to decades of failed monetary stimulus (QQE) and fiscal spending that have left the domestic economy stagnant.

Why rate hikes are backfiring on the yen

In textbooks, raising interest rates should strengthen a domestic currency by offering higher yields to investors. However, Jeff explains that the BOJ's rate hikes have had the opposite effect. By raising rates inorganically, the central bank has introduced instability and uncertainty into the JGB market.

Steve Van Metre points out that long-term investors, such as life insurers, are staging a "buyer strike." Because the BOJ continues to telegraph future rate hikes, long-term investors are choosing to sit on the sidelines or invest overseas rather than buy domestic bonds that are poised to lose value. Paradoxically, the more the BOJ hikes rates, the more unattractive JGBs become, driving more capital out of Japan and further weakening the yen.

The economic fallout for domestic businesses

Steve highlights a growing crisis among small and mid-sized Japanese enterprises. While a weak yen is traditionally expected to boost exports, many of these smaller companies are facing bankruptcy.

Unlike multinational corporations, small businesses lack direct, flexible access to U.S. dollars and rely heavily on currency hedges. The extreme volatility of the yen has repeatedly blown through these hedges, leaving them highly exposed. Steve notes that every time the BOJ attempts to address inflation by raising rates, it inadvertently pushes another wave of vulnerable domestic companies toward insolvency.

The challenge of restoring domestic investment

Both hosts agree that the Japanese government has finally identified the correct problem—domestic capital flight—but remains powerless to fix it. To reverse the carry trade organically, Japan must offer compelling, risk-adjusted returns at home.

Jeff suggests that the government might eventually resort to authoritarian or regulatory pressures, such as forcing the Government Pension Investment Fund (GPIF) to allocate more to JGBs or using tax audits to discourage foreign investing. However, both hosts conclude that these measures cannot substitute for genuine economic growth. Until Japan addresses its structural inability to grow, domestic capital will continue to seek opportunities abroad.
