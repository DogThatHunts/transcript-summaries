A wide-ranging discussion on the rapid acceleration of artificial intelligence, the rise of Chinese open-source models, and how automation will reshape human labor and geopolitics.

In this episode of the Naval podcast, host Naval sits down with Gary Tan of Y Combinator, Daniel of Able Police, and Farboud of A-List to explore the frontier of artificial intelligence and its societal impacts. The group debates the merits of AI-generated writing, the shifting dynamics between US proprietary models and Chinese open-source alternatives, and the geopolitical realities of hardware manufacturing. Looking forward, they discuss how the commoditization of software will redefine the startup ecosystem, transform white-collar employment, and potentially usher in an era of robotics-driven abundance.

Key insights

  • Inference scale is set to explode: Gary Tan predicts a massive 90,000x increase in AI inference over the next 24 to 36 months, driven by rapid hardware expansion and falling token costs.
  • The battle of open versus closed source: While US companies lead in proprietary models like Claude and Codex, Chinese open-source models (such as GLM and Minimax) are rapidly closing the capability gap, subsidized by a geopolitical interest in commoditizing software.
  • The debate over AI-generated writing: Daniel advocates for highly customized AI writing engines using lateral stochastic drift to brainstorm novel ideas, while Naval strongly opposes AI writing for human consumption, arguing it degrades the essential human muscles of writing and thinking.
  • Software commoditization shifts value to hardware: As AI makes software engineering cheap and easily replicable, durable advantages are shifting back to hardware manufacturing, where China maintains a massive supply chain lead.
  • Transitioning from workers to handlers: Rather than facing total displacement, humans who embrace AI are becoming highly leveraged "handlers" or "Pokemon trainers," guiding models and robots to fulfill human desires.

The explosion of AI capabilities and token costs

Gary Tan shares his experience of transitioning rapidly into "vibe coding," creating G Stack, one of the top open-source packages. He suggests that by spending roughly $100,000 a year on tokens, individuals can essentially live like normal citizens of 2028 today. This is driven by the expectation that compute power will scale exponentially. Gary projects a 90,000x increase in AI inference capabilities over the next two to three years, suggesting that Nvidia may still be significantly underpriced given the massive scale of data centers currently under construction.

The open-source surge and Chinese competition

The participants discuss the rapid advancement of open-source AI models, particularly those originating from China, such as GLM, Minimax, and DeepSeek. Naval points out that Chinese developers benefit from being less constrained by copyright laws, allowing them to train on vast datasets crawled from platforms like YouTube and Reddit.

Additionally, the group discusses the prevalence of model distillation—where open-source models are trained on outputs from advanced US proprietary models. Naval argues that US nationalization and regulatory capture of AI models will only accelerate this trend, as weights inevitably leak due to poor corporate security. He suggests that the Chinese government actively subsidizes open-source AI to commoditize software globally, thereby protecting and leveraging China's dominant position in hardware manufacturing.

The philosophy of writing and human thinking

A central debate emerges regarding the use of AI for writing. Daniel describes his development of "G-Brain," a system containing hundreds of thousands of personal markdown files. He utilizes a feature called "LSD mode" (lateral stochastic drift) to cross-reference disparate vector spaces and generate highly creative, novel ideas.

Naval strongly pushes back against using AI to generate text meant for human consumption. He argues that AI-generated writing is often verbose and clinical, calling it a "disservice" and a "quality drop" for the reader. Furthermore, Naval warns that outsourcing writing to machines will cause humans to lose their ability to speak and think clearly, as good writing is the ultimate output of disciplined thinking. He jokes that if AI is going to write the emails, we should just let our own AIs read them, removing humans from the loop entirely.

Geopolitical realities and the future of Taiwan

Naval offers a highly skeptical view of US-China geopolitical competition, arguing that a shooting war over Taiwan is highly unlikely. He claims that wealthy Taiwanese citizens are already systematically avoiding mandatory conscription and that many expect Taiwan to eventually assimilate into China over the next few decades, similar to Hong Kong.

Furthermore, Naval asserts that traditional military assets like aircraft carriers are obsolete in the face of modern land-based missiles and drone technology. On domestic policy, he argues that the US should focus on internal economic growth and selective tariffs rather than picking a conflict with China. In a controversial claim, Naval also attributes the creation of COVID-19 to joint gain-of-function research funded by both the US and Chinese governments.

The future of labor: universal basic robots

Looking at the future of employment, Gary Tan advocates for "universal basic robots" (UBR) rather than universal basic income (UBI), envisioning a future where affordable robotics handle mundane physical tasks like cleaning and cooking. While skeptics place this future five to ten years away, boosters believe it is only two to three years from reality.

Naval notes that while white-collar management, academic, and journalism jobs are facing severe disruption, human desire remains the ultimate unreplaceable moat. As long as humans have desires, they will need to guide and handle AI agents. Rather than being replaced, productive workers will transition into highly leveraged "AI handlers" or "Pokemon trainers," using technology to achieve unprecedented levels of creative and operational output.
