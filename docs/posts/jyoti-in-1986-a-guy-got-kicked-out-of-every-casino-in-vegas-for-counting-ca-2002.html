Probability theory fundamentals: sample spaces and axioms

This is a lecture on probability theory, delivered in Greek, that introduces the foundational concepts needed to build probabilistic models.

Why probability matters

The speaker opens by emphasizing that uncertainty exists everywhere in life. Whether you're managing risk in business, playing casino games, or making decisions in any domain, you need a systematic way to think about uncertainty. Probability theory provides the mathematical framework for handling these situations across different fields—from economics to technology to social sciences.

Building a probability model: three steps

To construct a probability model, you need:

  1. A sample space (Ω): the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment
  2. A probability assignment: assigning probabilities to events (subsets of the sample space)
  3. The axioms: rules that any valid probability assignment must follow

Sample spaces: discrete and continuous

The lecture uses two main examples:

Discrete example: rolling a die twice

When rolling a standard die twice, the sample space consists of 16 ordered pairs: (1,1), (1,2), ..., (4,4). Each outcome is equally likely with probability 1/16. From this, you can answer questions like: What is P(first roll = 2)? Or P(minimum of two rolls ≥ 2)? By identifying which outcomes satisfy the condition and summing their probabilities, you get the answer.

Continuous example: throwing a dart at a square

If you throw a dart at a unit square [0,1] × [0,1], the sample space is uncountably infinite. Here, probability is proportional to area. If you want P(x + y ≤ 1.5), you find the area of the region where this holds and use that as the probability.

The three axioms of probability

Any probability assignment must satisfy:

  1. Non-negativity: P(A) ≥ 0 for any event A
  2. Normalization: P(Ω) = 1 (the entire sample space has probability 1)
  3. Additivity: If A and B are disjoint (mutually exclusive), then P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)

From these three axioms, you can derive that P(A) ≤ 1 for any event A. The speaker walks through this derivation: since A and its complement partition Ω, we have P(A) + P(A^c) = 1. Since P(A^c) ≥ 0, it follows that P(A) ≤ 1.

Extending additivity to countable unions

The axioms above work for finite unions. However, for infinite sample spaces (like continuous ones), you need a stronger axiom: countable additivity. This states that if you have a countable collection of disjoint events A₁, A₂, A₃, ..., then:

P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ A₃ ∪ ...) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) + P(A₃) + ...

This is essential for working with continuous probability models. The speaker notes that this axiom is stronger than the finite additivity axiom and is needed to handle cases like waiting for heads on a coin flip for the first time (where the outcome is any positive integer).

Choosing the right sample space

An important practical point: when defining a sample space, you have some freedom in how detailed to make it. For example, when flipping a coin, you could track just heads/tails, or you could track the exact angle and spin. The choice depends on what information is relevant to your problem. The sample space must be:

  • Collectively exhaustive: every possible outcome is included
  • Mutually exclusive: outcomes don't overlap

Key insight: from outcomes to events

In continuous settings, individual outcomes have probability zero. This is why you must assign probabilities to events (sets of outcomes) rather than individual points. For a finite sample space with equally likely outcomes, the probability of an event is simply the number of favorable outcomes divided by the total number of outcomes.
