Biocomputing Is Here, and Investors Are NOT Ready

As tech giants face an unprecedented electricity shortage to power artificial intelligence, a massive investment opportunity is emerging in deregulated nuclear energy and grid infrastructure.

The biological signal flare

In a Melbourne lab, 200,000 living human neurons wired onto a silicon chip learned to play the video game Doom in a week without explicit programming. While this biocomputing breakthrough sounds like science fiction, Felix argues it is actually a desperate signal flare. The ultimate driver behind biocomputing research is not novelty, but extreme energy efficiency: the human brain runs on just 20 watts, whereas artificial intelligence infrastructure is consuming power at an unsustainable, city-scale rate.

The staggering math of AI power consumption

A single cabinet-sized rack of AI servers draws roughly 100,000 watts—equivalent to the power consumption of several hundred homes. Large-scale AI data centers currently being designed require up to 750 megawatts, operating like small cities. The AI industry's total power consumption is projected to double in the next few years, potentially matching the entire electrical output of Japan. This creates a critical bottleneck: while AI demand doubles every two years, building new gas turbines takes years, and constructing new nuclear plants can take decades. Research firm Gartner projects that power shortages will restrict 40% of AI data centers by next year.

The four rungs of big tech's power desperation

To secure energy, major technology firms are climbing a four-rung ladder of desperation:

  1. Efficiency: Upgrading chips, cooling systems, and software models (which ultimately increases demand as usage scales).
  2. Grid dominance: Signing long-term contracts to lock up existing grid capacity.
  3. The nuclear option: Reopening retired nuclear facilities. Microsoft signed a 20-year contract to restart the Three Mile Island unit (the site of America's worst nuclear accident), paying double the market rate. Amazon signed a 17-year deal with Talen Energy, and Meta has locked up 6.6 gigawatts of power through 2035.
  4. Biocomputing: Investing in biological computing alternatives to bypass silicon's physical limits entirely.

The deregulated nuclear "legal monopoly"

Most nuclear plants are owned by heavily regulated utilities, preventing tech companies from buying their output directly. However, a few operators exist in deregulated markets and can sign private, long-term contracts. Felix identifies only three companies that control a meaningful fleet of these deregulated nuclear assets, creating a "moat made of law":

  • Constellation Energy (CEG): Owners of the Three Mile Island plant, with contracts tied to Microsoft and Meta.
  • Talen Energy (TLN): Owners of the Susquehanna plant, backed by a major contract with Amazon.
  • Vistra (VST): Another key deregulated nuclear operator seeing interest from major political and institutional investors.

While these stocks have run up significantly and recently pulled back from their highs, Felix notes that their long-term contracts have not yet hit their balance sheets, representing a potential lag in market valuation.

The unglamorous "toll collectors" of the grid

For investors wary of individual energy suppliers, Felix suggests focusing on the infrastructure "toll collectors" who benefit regardless of which energy source wins. Companies supplying essential grid hardware are experiencing unprecedented backlogs:

  • GE Vernova (GEV): Their gas turbine backlog has reached 116 gigawatts (representing a six-year production backlog), alongside a $42 billion grid business backlog.
  • Transformer manufacturers: Facing a severe global shortage, allowing sellers to dictate pricing for these essential grid components.

Key risks to watch

Felix cautions that this thesis carries several critical risks:

  • Unrealized plans: Data center announcements are merely claims on power, not guaranteed builds. If AI spending slows, power demand curves will flatten.
  • Regulatory changes: The legal moat relies on state-level deregulation rules in roughly 11 US states; changes to these rules could disrupt private contracts.
  • Poor current fundamentals: On paper, these companies currently show low margins and poor fundamentals because future AI revenues are not yet reflected.
  • Timeline slippage: Nuclear restarts are notoriously prone to delays. For example, Constellation's Three Mile Island restart is targeted for 2027, and any delay will defer expected revenues.
