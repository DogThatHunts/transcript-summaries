As private credit defaults hit record highs, the stress is spilling over into public junk bonds and tech giants, signaling a broader credit cycle downturn.

The illusion of containment in private credit

The narrative that credit market stress is safely contained within private credit is rapidly falling apart. While investment-grade spreads remain deceptively calm, the public markets are beginning to price in significant risk. Triple-C rated junk bonds have pushed past 1,000 basis points—levels reminiscent of last year's regional banking panic or the 2024 carry trade blowout. This active refusal by investors to finance the weakest companies without crisis-like compensation shows that the downturn is no longer just a private market issue.

A record spike in private credit defaults

According to a recent Fitch Ratings report tracking roughly 1,300 American private debt borrowers, the trailing 12-month default rate reached a record 6% at the end of the second quarter, up from 5.7% in the prior quarter. Crucially, the nature of these defaults is shifting:

  • Maturity extensions have overtaken payment-in-kind (PIK) arrangements as the leading source of delinquency, meaning borrowers are struggling to repay principal rather than just interest.
  • Sector-specific stress is highly visible, with industrials and manufacturing defaults spiking to 10.4% (up from 5.9% in Q1).

The AI debt story and rising credit default swaps

The massive capital expenditure required for the artificial intelligence build-out is shifting from an equity story to a debt market story. Investors are exhibiting growing price sensitivity even toward prestigious tech names:

  • Credit default swap (CDS) spreads have risen sharply for companies like Oracle (reaching 215 basis points from 145 at the end of last year), SpaceX, Nvidia, Meta, Amazon, and Alphabet.
  • Bond issuance resistance was evident when Amazon recently sold $25 billion in bonds; after spreads were reduced, the order book fell significantly, forcing Amazon to pay an elevated new issue concession on longer maturities.

Cracks in business development companies and fundraising

Business development companies (BDCs)—which act as a public bridge to private credit portfolios—are seeing their stock prices slide toward new lows as investors demand a larger margin of safety.

  • Aries Capital, the largest publicly traded BDC, reported a 15% quarterly increase in non-accrual status investments, reaching $708 million.
  • Blue Owl experienced a dramatic slowdown in fundraising, securing only $1.8 billion for its credit business (down from $12.1 billion overall in the same period last year). This slowdown, coupled with high redemption requests that forced withdrawal limits, reduces the fund's flexibility to support troubled borrowers.

The bank transmission mechanism

The risk of contagion to the broader financial system lies in the deep connections between regulated banks and private credit funds. The Financial Stability Board tracks $220 billion in bank credit lines to private credit, though commercial estimates suggest the actual exposure is more than double that. Banks provide borrowing-base facilities, leverage, and revolving credit to these funds and their borrowers. If private loans deteriorate, collateral values fall, triggering borrowing-base deficiencies that force funds to find cash precisely when liquidity is drying up.
