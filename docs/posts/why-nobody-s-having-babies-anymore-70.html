The shift in global fertility dynamics

John Burn-Murdoch explains that historical declines in birth rates—stretching from the 1800s to the late 20th century—were slow-moving and driven by positive developments: lower child mortality, urbanization, the shift from agriculture to manufacturing, and rising female education. However, around 2010, a sudden and steep decline began, pushing birth rates in high-income countries well below the replacement rate of 2.1 children per woman.

This trend has severe economic consequences. Burn-Murdoch points to Japan as a prime example: while Japanese productivity per worker has remained highly competitive with the US, its overall economy has stagnated because the working-age population has shrunk dramatically. Furthermore, Burn-Murdoch notes that this decline is no longer just a rich-country phenomenon. Developing nations like Mexico, Tunisia, and Turkey now have lower birth rates than the US or the UK, meaning they are growing old before they grow rich.

Why smartphones and social media are the primary culprits

While housing costs and female financial autonomy are frequently blamed for lower birth rates, Burn-Murdoch argues these are slow-moving trends that do not explain the sudden post-2010 drop. Instead, he points to a landmark study by researchers at the University of Cincinnati, which tracked the rollout of 4G mobile networks across the US and UK. The study revealed that areas receiving high-speed mobile connectivity first saw their birth rates fall earlier and more steeply.

Burn-Murdoch identifies two primary mechanisms for this trend: 1. The loss of face-to-face interaction: Time-use studies show a dramatic drop in young people hanging out in person. In South Korea, face-to-face socializing has halved in ten years. This severely limits the unstructured, serendipitous interactions needed to form relationships. 2. The acceleration of individualistic narratives: Social media exposes young people globally to highly individualistic lifestyles and peer narratives that discourage early family formation, transplanting Western ideals of independence to developing countries without the accompanying economic growth.

The "curated self" and the death of commitment

David McWilliams and his co-host John discuss the psychological impact of digital platforms, contrasting "core human values" with the superficial values promoted online. McWilliams argues that platforms like Instagram, TikTok, and LinkedIn encourage users to project a highly curated, overachieving version of themselves. This creates what sociologists call "normative discontent," where young people develop unrealistic expectations of potential partners.

Because dating apps offer a seemingly infinite pool of choices, users fall victim to the paradox of choice. Rather than settling down and embracing the normal, imperfect realities of another human being, individuals remain perennially disappointed and hesitant to commit, choosing short-term hookups over long-term partnership.
