Enemy: the Germans' War, episode 1 – we Are the War

Summary

Daryl Cooper opens this episode of the Martyr Made Podcast by explaining his absence and his decision to focus exclusively on historical storytelling rather than contemporary political commentary. He then launches into a sweeping narrative of World War I, beginning with a personal anecdote about encountering a South African veteran in Tanzania, which prompts reflection on the experience of losing a war and the psychological weight it carries.

Cooper uses William Faulkner's description of the moment before Pickett's Charge to frame the episode's central theme: the moment when nations and individuals must gamble everything on uncertain outcomes. He then traces the strategic situation in 1918, when Germany, having knocked Russia out of the war and seemingly positioned for victory, faced a narrowing window of opportunity before American reinforcements arrived in overwhelming numbers.

Key insights

  1. The illusion of agency in warfare: Soldiers need to believe their actions matter, even when circumstances are largely beyond their control. In the trenches of World War I, this illusion was nearly impossible to maintain, as death seemed arbitrary and omnipresent.

  2. The environmental horror of trench warfare: Beyond combat itself, soldiers faced mud, water, cold, disease, rats, lice, and the constant presence of decomposing bodies. These conditions were not occasional hardships but the permanent reality of frontline existence.

  3. Germany's structural disadvantage: Despite fielding the war's most efficient army, Germany lacked the resources, manpower, and colonial territories of the Entente powers. The British blockade created a starvation crisis that killed approximately 800,000 Germans and undermined both military capability and home front morale.

  4. The transformation of Adolf Hitler: From a struggling artist to a dedicated soldier, Hitler found purpose and belonging in the army. His exceptional courage as a dispatch runner and his ideological development during the war would later shape his interpretation of Germany's defeat as a "stab in the back" by internal enemies.

  5. The collapse of German morale: By 1918, despite tactical successes in the spring offensive, the German army faced not only military exhaustion but ideological infection from Bolshevik propaganda, the Spanish flu pandemic, and the knowledge that they faced enemies with vastly superior resources and fresh manpower.

The trenches: a World of unimaginable suffering

Cooper devotes substantial time to describing the physical and psychological conditions soldiers endured. The trenches of the Western Front, particularly in Belgium and northern France, sat at or below sea level, creating perpetual flooding. Men stood in water mixed with urine, feces, and decomposing flesh for days at a time. Trench foot, caused by prolonged exposure to wet and cold, led to gangrene and amputation. The mud was so deep that soldiers could sink and disappear entirely.

Beyond the environment, soldiers faced constant artillery bombardment. A single barrage might involve thousands of shells per hour, creating a sensory experience so overwhelming that it induced traumatic brain injuries even in men sheltered in dugouts. The psychological impact of being hunted by unseen artillery, where death seemed to seek you out personally, broke many soldiers' minds.

Gas attacks represented another form of horror. Cooper quotes extensively from Sebastian Barry's novel depicting a poison gas attack, showing how soldiers fled in panic from an invisible, suffocating enemy that left survivors blinded or with permanently damaged lungs.

The starvation blockade

Germany's vulnerability lay not primarily on the battlefield but in her inability to feed her population. The British naval blockade, combined with poor harvests and the demands of feeding the army, created a famine that killed hundreds of thousands of civilians. By 1916-17, the "turnip winter," Germans ate sawdust bread and hunted dogs and cats for food. Middle-class women searched garbage heaps for scraps. Children displayed signs of severe malnutrition.

This starvation had profound effects on military morale. Soldiers home on leave saw their families starving. The knowledge that the home front was collapsing while they fought created a sense of betrayal that would later fuel conspiracy theories about who "lost" the war.

The German offensive of 1918

Cooper describes the German spring offensive in detail, drawing heavily on Ernst Jünger's memoir Storm of Steel. The Germans developed revolutionary new offensive tactics emphasizing small-unit initiative and infiltration rather than frontal assaults. The preliminary bombardment alone involved 1.16 million shells in five hours.

Initially successful, the offensive pushed British lines back 40 miles and inflicted massive casualties. But the German army, starving and exhausted, could not sustain the momentum. Supply lines broke down. Soldiers stopped to raid enemy food stores. The discovery that Allied soldiers were well-fed while Germans starved further demoralized the troops.

Ernst Jünger's account reveals the emotional and physical toll: the terror of artillery, the struggle between fear and duty, the horror of close combat, and the gradual realization that victory was impossible. Jünger was wounded multiple times but kept returning to the front, eventually receiving the Order of Merit at age 23.

Adolf Hitler's War

Cooper traces Hitler's journey from a struggling Munich artist to a decorated soldier. Hitler volunteered enthusiastically in 1914, found his first real home in the army, and served with distinction as a dispatch runner—a dangerous role requiring him to navigate no-man's land under fire to deliver messages.

Hitler earned the Iron Cross Second Class and was promoted to corporal. He was socially awkward and unpopular in conventional ways, but his comrades respected his reliability and courage. He adopted a stray dog named Fuchsel, which he trained and cared for, revealing a capacity for tenderness alongside his martial dedication.

During his recovery from a wound in late 1916, Hitler became convinced that Jews were disproportionately represented in rear-echelon positions and were profiting from the war while frontline soldiers suffered. Whether this represents genuine ideological development or later retconning in Mein Kampf remains unclear, but it marks the beginning of the antisemitic worldview that would define his later career.

Hitler returned to the front and participated in the 1918 offensives, continuing to volunteer for the most dangerous missions. He was temporarily blinded by mustard gas in October 1918 and was hospitalized when the war ended.

The Revolution and collapse

As the German army exhausted itself in 1918, revolution erupted at home. Strikes by radical socialists, the Spanish flu pandemic, and the arrival of fresh American troops all contributed to the sense that the war was lost. Discipline broke down. Soldiers refused orders. Red flags and Bolshevik slogans appeared everywhere.

The final blow came when the German Navy attempted to launch a suicidal attack against the British. Sailors mutinied, and the rebellion spread. Within days, revolution consumed German cities. The Kaiser fled to Holland. Officers who had bled for Germany were attacked by mobs wearing red armbands.

The German army, still holding its lines, had not been defeated in the field. This fact would fuel the "stab in the back" myth—the belief that Germany had been betrayed by internal enemies rather than conquered by superior force. For men like Hitler, who had sacrificed everything and seen comrades die by the millions, this narrative was psychologically necessary and politically explosive.

Conclusion

Cooper ends the episode with the observation that while the Armistice came on November 11, 1918, the war was far from over. Revolution consumed Central and Eastern Europe. Millions starved. The Red Army marched. The question of who had truly won remained open, and the seeds of future conflict had been planted in the hearts of men like Adolf Hitler, who believed Germany had been robbed of victory by traitors.

The episode's title—"We Are The War"—captures the way the war had become inseparable from the identity of those who fought it. For Germany's soldiers, the war was not something that happened to them; it was who they were. Its ending did not bring peace but rather transformed the conflict into a different kind of struggle, one that would consume the twentieth century.
