The progression of the credit cycle

Jeff Snyder explains that the financial system is experiencing a systematic migration of credit stress. For the past year, the primary focus was on private credit and middle-market corporate debt. Recently, this stress has crept into public markets, as evidenced by rising triple-C credit spreads and investors demanding higher yields from cash-rich tech giants like Alphabet and Amazon. The sudden collapse of UWM's stock and its dividend elimination marks the transition of this credit cycle into the mortgage space. Snyder emphasizes that this is not a subprime delinquency crisis, but rather a reflection of broad macroeconomic constraints limiting debt market functionality.

The labor market's hidden erosion

Steve Van Metre points out that credit cycles do not begin with immediate defaults. Instead, rising interest rates first restrict economic activity, leading to slower sales. Employers respond by cutting worker hours and reducing raises rather than executing immediate layoffs. This erosion of purchasing power eventually forces consumers to make hard spending choices, which ultimately triggers delinquencies. Van Metre highlights recent payroll data as validation of this trend, noting a highly negative household survey, significant job losses, and a massive drop of one million people from the labor force. Why lower rates aren't saving the housing market

According to Snyder, UWM's stock peaked in September on the assumption that Federal Reserve rate cuts would lower mortgage rates and spark a massive housing revival. However, while mortgage rates did decline, no buyers emerged. Snyder explains that rates fell because the underlying economy was weakening, not because of healthy market dynamics. Because consumers lack sufficient income and job security, lower rates have failed to stimulate demand. This lack of volume deprives mortgage originators of the profits needed to cushion their losses, creating a vicious downward cycle of tightening credit standards and declining economic activity.
