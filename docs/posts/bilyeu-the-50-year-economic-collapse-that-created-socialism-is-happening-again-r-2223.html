The 50-year economic collapse that created socialism is happening again right now

Overview

Bilyeu argues that the world is experiencing a modern version of the Engels Pause—a 50-year period during the Industrial Revolution when economic productivity rose but workers' wages fell, creating extreme inequality and the conditions for socialism's emergence. The same pattern is repeating today, driven by three factors: currency debasement through deficit spending and money printing, technological disruption that creates winners and losers, and unproductive investments enabled by market distortions. Rather than pursuing socialism, Bilyeu contends that history shows the solution requires three revolutions: democratization of ownership, education systems, and political power.

Key insights

  1. The Engels Pause is repeating. During the Industrial Revolution's Engels Pause (roughly 1760–1840), economic growth accelerated but workers' real wages declined as new technologies displaced skilled labor. A tiny elite who understood factories and mechanization became wildly successful, while the majority fell behind. Today's K-shaped economy mirrors this pattern: a small group leveraging technology and capital gains while the middle class loses ground.

  2. Socialism's appeal is obvious but its solutions are flawed. Bilyeu acknowledges that redistributing wealth from billionaires to struggling families sounds self-evident and morally obvious. However, attempting to fix inequality through redistribution doesn't address the underlying causes. It's like changing a basketball score without addressing why one team plays better—the fundamental problem remains, and productive people simply leave or hide their wealth.

  3. The real danger is loss of progress, not inequality itself. Bilyeu argues that people tolerate inequality when their own lives are improving. The danger emerges when people fall below the cost of living and experience regression. Fairness reflexes—innate to primates—trigger when people perceive injustice, and combined with social media visibility of wealth disparities, this creates a volatile situation.

  4. Technology creates power-law distributions, not bell curves. Digital technologies don't distribute benefits evenly. Early adopters and those who understand how to leverage new tools (AI, social media, cloud computing) accumulate disproportionate wealth, while others are distracted or displaced. This power-law distribution persists until the technology becomes ubiquitous and education systems adapt—a process that takes decades.

  5. The Nordic model works only under specific conditions. Sweden and other Nordic countries have created functional high-tax, high-service systems, but they succeed because of small, homogenous populations with aligned values, geographic clustering, high government competence, and a cultural emphasis on hard work and fairness. These conditions don't scale to large, diverse nations. Bilyeu emphasizes this is about values alignment, not race—shared commitment to contributing fairly to the system.

The Engels Pause and historical solutions

The Engels Pause ended through three major revolutions, none of which were inevitable or quick:

  • Revolution in ownership: Limited liability companies and new forms of asset ownership allowed ordinary people to build wealth.
  • Revolution in skills: Compulsory schooling systems trained workers for industrial economies.
  • Revolution in political power: Democratic reforms (the Great Reform Act in Britain, the Chartist movement) redistributed political voice.

These changes took 60–70 years and required both violent upheaval (France) and peaceful reform (Britain). Bilyeu argues that similar systemic changes will be necessary to address today's disruption, particularly as AI emerges as a transformative technology.

Why socialism fails

Bilyeu distinguishes between socialism as a concept and socialism as practice. While redistributing from the wealthy to the poor sounds logical, it fails because:

  • It doesn't address root causes. Inequality is a symptom, not the disease. The disease is the system that produces the inequality.
  • Productive people leave. When taxation exceeds roughly 40% of earnings, highly productive individuals and companies relocate, hide wealth, or stop creating jobs.
  • It requires coercion. Socialism ultimately depends on force—legal walls, taxation walls, and eventually physical walls—to prevent productive people from leaving.
  • It creates suspicion. Socialism makes people suspicious of whether others are working as hard as they are, especially in diverse societies where people don't share values or see themselves as part of the same tribe.

Capitalism and competition

Bilyeu is pro-capitalism, but specifically pro-competition. He argues that capitalism works because competition drives prices down and quality up. When power consolidates—through monopolies or government overreach—capitalism ceases to function. He advocates for breaking up strategic monopolies (like Amazon's combination of AWS and retail operations) and favoring small businesses through lighter regulation, which creates the dynamic competition that makes capitalism moral and effective.

The technology wedge and the K-shaped economy

Bilyeu identifies three roughly equal contributors to the K-shaped economy:

  1. Money printing and currency debasement (one-third): Deficit spending and inflation erode purchasing power, especially for those without assets.
  2. Technological disruption (one-third): New technologies create winners (those who adopt and leverage them) and losers (those displaced or distracted by them). This creates a power-law distribution where a small group captures disproportionate gains.
  3. Unproductive investments (one-third): Market distortions allow people to make poor choices (pursuing degrees with no economic value, for example) without immediate consequences, leading to massive misallocation of capital and labor.

Bilyeu argues that while technology ultimately creates more jobs and raises living standards, the transition period is brutal for those displaced. The current era—with personal computers, the internet, social media, cloud computing, and now AI arriving in rapid succession—has created a rolling series of power-law distributions, preventing the economy from settling into a bell curve distribution where benefits are more evenly spread.

The AI moment and future economic paradigms

Bilyeu views the current technological revolution as an on-ramp to AI, which will be the "lift-off moment" for a fundamentally different economy. He predicts another Engels Pause-like period over the next 10–20 years, with early adopters capturing disproportionate gains while laggards fall further behind.

However, he argues that we cannot simply apply old solutions. The scale and nature of AI disruption requires new economic paradigms that don't yet exist. He suggests several possibilities:

  • Treating data as common property: Since AI is trained on collective human data, perhaps companies should pay into a common pool when leveraging this asset.
  • Government ownership of data center infrastructure: If pension funds end up holding the bag for failed data center investments (as happened with 2008 mortgage debt), governments might bail them out and own the infrastructure as a public good, leasing it to tech companies and redistributing returns.
  • Sovereign wealth funds: Countries should treat natural resources and common assets as family silver, selling them once and investing proceeds in a sovereign wealth fund that supports education, infrastructure, and opportunity.

Bilyeu emphasizes that these solutions must be based on value and trade, not coercion. The goal is to create systems where people can earn into ownership and success, not systems where they're dependent on government handouts.

Values, homogeneity, and scale

Bilyeu argues that socialism's feasibility depends on values alignment, not racial or ethnic homogeneity (though he acknowledges that shared language, dress, and cultural practices facilitate trust). When people share values—believing in hard work, fairness, and mutual obligation—they're willing to contribute to collective systems. When values diverge, people become suspicious and resentful, especially in socialist systems where they're pooling resources.

This is why Nordic countries' high-tax systems work: small populations with aligned values, geographic clustering, and cultural emphasis on fairness. It's also why such systems struggle when immigration introduces people with different values or work ethics. Bilyeu emphasizes this isn't about excluding people but about the practical reality that systems requiring high trust and shared values don't scale across diverse, geographically dispersed populations.

The path forward

Rather than socialism or laissez-faire capitalism, Bilyeu envisions a future requiring:

  1. New ownership systems where people can earn into assets and build wealth, not just receive handouts.
  2. Education systems that reward entrepreneurship, uniqueness, delegation, and collaboration—the opposite of industrial-era schooling that punishes these traits.
  3. Political systems that distribute power more broadly, preventing both government and corporate monopolies.
  4. Recognition that geography matters less than participation in the digital economy. The future divides between those in the AI/tech economy and those in traditional economies, not between nations.

Bilyeu acknowledges he doesn't have a complete roadmap but argues that history provides a template: the three revolutions of the 1800s (ownership, education, political power) will need modern equivalents suited to a digital, AI-driven world.
