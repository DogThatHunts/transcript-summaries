Overview

Dave Asprey hosts research microbiologist Kiran Krishnan to explore how cartilage regeneration, joint repair, and age-related degeneration are fundamentally driven by vascular function and nitric oxide production. Krishnan presents evidence from clinical trials showing that even severely damaged joints—bone-on-bone cases in people aged 65–70—can rebuild cartilage when the right systemic interventions are applied. The conversation connects blood flow, inflammation, cellular signaling, and practical biohacking strategies to reverse joint and muscle decline.

Five key insights

  1. Over 90% of adults over 30 experience progressive joint degeneration, even without symptoms. The Framingham study found that asymptomatic adults showed active cartilage degradation, making joint health a universal aging challenge—the second leading cause of disability worldwide.

  2. Cartilage regeneration is possible at any age when three conditions are met: restored blood flow to the joint, reduced chronic inflammation, and reactivation of chondrocytes (cartilage-building cells) from a catabolic (breaking down) to anabolic (building) state.

  3. Nitric oxide must be sustained at functional levels for 24 hours, not spiked briefly. Most nitric oxide supplements create a 2–3 hour spike above the functional threshold (~200 mmol/L), then drop below effectiveness. Sustained levels are required for systemic repair.

  4. Strength, not size, correlates with longevity. Muscle recruitment capacity—the ability to activate muscle fibers—predicts lifespan better than muscle mass. Thigh muscle size is the only muscle group directly linked to longevity, due to high GLUT4 glucose receptors.

  5. The vascular system is the limiting factor for nutrient delivery, amino acid uptake, and satellite cell recruitment to muscles. Even with adequate protein and exercise signals, poor vessel compliance and low nitric oxide prevent the body from utilizing these inputs effectively.

The root cause: Vascular dysfunction and inflammation

Krishnan argues that joint degeneration begins with vascular endothelial dysfunction—a breakdown of the glycocalyx, the carbohydrate-based protective lining inside blood vessels. Between ages 20 and 70, people experience a 50–70% reduction in nitric oxide production, causing vessels to lose compliance (flexibility). This is especially critical in joints, where cartilage relies entirely on diffusion from tiny microvessels in the subchondral bone below.

When these microvessels collapse due to insufficient nitric oxide: - Blood supply to the joint becomes compromised - Pro-inflammatory mediators accumulate in the synovium (joint space) - Chondrocytes (cartilage-building cells) switch from anabolic to catabolic mode - The cells begin actively degrading cartilage rather than repairing it

Asprey notes this happened to him at age 14 due to his Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (a genetic collagen disorder), but Krishnan emphasizes it occurs in virtually everyone after age 20—just later and more gradually.

Practical strategies: Timing, movement, and the cephalic response

Timing Nitric oxide with meals

Asprey asks about the apparent contradiction: post-meal squats lower blood sugar (protecting the glycocalyx from AGE damage), but squats divert blood from the gut during digestion. Krishnan resolves this:

  • The cephalic response (triggered by seeing, smelling, and anticipating food) perfuses the gut with blood before and during eating
  • After ~30 minutes, the gut no longer needs maximum blood supply
  • This is the optimal window for movement to manage blood sugar

Hack: Take Vasconox ~3 hours before your first meal so nitric oxide peaks during eating, ensuring the gut has maximum blood supply. Then walk gently (not intense squats) 30 minutes post-meal to manage glucose without severely redirecting blood.

Strength vs. size: what actually predicts longevity

Krishnan emphasizes that muscle strength (recruitment capacity) correlates with longevity far more than muscle size. Only thigh muscle size shows direct correlation with lifespan, because thighs have high GLUT4 glucose receptors and burn significant glucose during walking.

Why strength matters more: - Strength reflects the ability to recruit muscle fibers - Poor recruitment (due to inadequate blood flow, nitric oxide, or neural signaling) means you cannot activate all available muscle - A large but poorly recruited muscle is metabolically inefficient and does not confer longevity benefits

Why size can be a liability: - Large muscles without corresponding strength require high cardiac output to perfuse - Bodybuilders often have poor longevity compared to power lifters or functional strength athletes - Kobe beef cattle are kept immobilized to marble their muscles with fat—movement and recruitment prevent intramuscular fat

Asprey's approach: maintain functional strength, low intramuscular fat, and high muscle fiber recruitment through movement and adequate nitric oxide, rather than chasing size.

The three pillars of physiological improvement

Krishnan frames anti-aging interventions as three interdependent systems:

  1. Signals: Exercise, movement, and mechanical stress that trigger adaptation
  2. Building blocks: Amino acids, minerals, and nutrients
  3. Delivery system: Vascular compliance, nitric oxide, and blood flow

Most people focus on signals (exercise) and building blocks (protein), but neglect the delivery system—which progressively degrades with age. This is why people cannot gain muscle as easily in their 40s despite lifting the same way as in their 20s: the vascular system cannot deliver amino acids and growth signals to muscle tissue.

Conclusion

Krishnan and Asprey argue that joint degeneration, muscle loss, and cognitive decline are not inevitable consequences of aging—they are symptoms of progressive vascular dysfunction and chronic inflammation.
