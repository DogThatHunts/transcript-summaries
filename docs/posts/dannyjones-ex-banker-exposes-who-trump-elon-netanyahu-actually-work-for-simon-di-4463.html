The architecture of power: understanding the FIC, MIC, and TIC

Dixon outlines his analytical method: disregarding public political rhetoric and instead following monetary allocations, debt ownership, and capital sponsorship. He breaks down modern Western governance into three dominant, overlapping interests:

  1. The Financial Industrial Complex (FIC): Spearheaded by commercial banks that create credit out of thin air, giant asset managers like BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street that control voting shares in virtually every public corporation, and central banks that act as guarantors of the debt.
  2. The Military Industrial Complex (MIC): Sustained by institutional defense contractors and intelligence apparatuses that rely on perpetual conflict cycles and post-war reconstruction contracts to guarantee continuous balance-sheet expansion.
  3. The Technical Industrial Complex (TIC): Big tech platforms, AI firms, and defense-tech startups (such as Palantir) that build cloud infrastructure, capture behavioral data, and prepare digital surveillance architectures.

Dixon argues that politicians are groomed and funded by these interest blocs, functioning primarily as transactional brokers rather than sovereign decision-makers.

The mechanics of the debt-based monetary Ponzi

Tracing the development of modern banking from goldsmith receipts to the creation of the Bank of Amsterdam, the Bank of England, and the US Federal Reserve, Dixon explains how fractional reserve models legally codified the creation of money as debt. Because banks issue principal while demanding interest payments that do not yet exist in the broader money supply, the entire global economy relies on endless credit growth:

  • Asset owners vs. labor: Those close to the money-creation spigot purchase equities and scarce real estate that appreciate faster than consumer goods inflation, driving the "K-shaped" economic divide.
  • Securitization and risk dumping: Dixon highlights how structures like mortgage-backed securities allow commercial banks to profit off loan creation while offloading default risks onto public pensions and ordinary retail savers.
  • Fiscal dominance: As debt servicing costs surge, governments are compelled to spend ever-larger amounts directly into markets to prevent systemic collapse, accelerating currency debasement.

The petrodollar sunset and the rise of west asia

Dixon contrasts the fully privatized American capital model with sovereign wealth models seen in Norway, Singapore, and the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states. Unlike Western powers where public debt backs private assets, nations such as Saudi Arabia maintain state ownership of underlying natural resources through national firms like Saudi Aramco.

Following the 1970s oil shocks and the end of the gold-convertible Bretton Woods system, the US petrodollar framework mandated that global crude trade settle in US dollars, with petrodollars recycled into US Treasuries. Dixon asserts that this pact is unraveling: the US has become a domestic energy producer, while China has emerged as the primary consumer of Gulf energy. Consequently, GCC capital is shifting away from US government bonds and moving directly into equities, artificial intelligence infrastructure, and local-currency settlements via China's CIPS messaging rail.
