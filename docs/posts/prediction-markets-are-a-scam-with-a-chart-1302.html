Prediction Markets Are a Scam With a Chart

An investigation into how modern prediction markets rebranded gambling as financial innovation, inviting quants and insiders to extract wealth from retail bettors under the guise of truth-seeking.

The rebranding of gambling as financial innovation

Modern financial markets traditionally exist to allocate capital to productive uses, but the definition of "productive" has expanded to include betting on pop culture, weather, and geopolitical events. Through a process of legal rebranding, what authorities once classified as gambling is now referred to as trading "event contracts." On platforms like Kalshi and Polymarket, retail participants are framed not as gamblers, but as liquidity providers contributing to a "truth machine."

In this analysis, the creator behind the channel a Chart explores the legal history, structural flaws, and societal impacts of these platforms, arguing that they function less like objective forecasting tools and more like highly sophisticated wealth transfer mechanisms.

The regulatory loophole and the onion anomaly

To understand how these platforms operate legally in the United States, one must look to the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC). Originally established to oversee agricultural futures (like wheat and cotton), the CFTC historically applied an "economic purpose test" to ensure contracts served a genuine hedging or price discovery function. Over decades, the definition of a commodity was expanded to include interest rates, stock indices, and eventually Bitcoin.

However, American financial law contains a bizarre exception: the 1958 Onion Futures Act. Passed after two traders successfully cornered the Chicago onion market in the 1950s, the law strictly bans futures trading on onions. Consequently, while Americans can legally use platforms like Kalshi to bet on congressional control or geopolitical conflicts, they are federally prohibited from hedging their financial exposure to onions.

Legal battles and political shield laws

Historically, the CFTC banned event contracts involving war, terrorism, assassination, and gaming to avoid acting as an "election cop." However, prediction platforms successfully challenged the regulator in federal court, establishing their right to list election-related contracts. Following this victory, Kalshi began self-certifying sports contracts for major leagues like the NBA and NFL, arguing that sports matches are simply "events."

This shift has created intense friction with individual U.S. states. Since the Supreme Court struck down the federal ban on sports betting in 2018, nearly 40 states have built highly regulated, heavily taxed sports betting frameworks. Tech platforms now offer functionally identical sports bets while claiming complete exemption from state taxes and gambling laws, arguing their offerings are technically federal commodity swaps.

This has led to severe legal pushback: * Arizona has filed criminal charges against Kalshi for operating an unlicensed sportsbook. * Ohio is seeing a lawsuit against Kalshi utilizing the Statute of Anne—a 1710 British law allowing third parties to sue to recover other people's gambling losses. * The federal government has taken the unusual step of deploying the CFTC and Department of Justice to block Arizona from enforcing its state laws against Kalshi.

The myth of the truth machine

Advocates argue that prediction markets provide highly accurate forecasts because participants must back their opinions with capital, stripping away personal bias. This is the efficient markets hypothesis applied to everyday life.

However, a Chart argues that because these markets are thinly traded, they are highly vulnerable to manipulation by wealthy actors seeking to manufacture public perception: * The 2012 Romney campaign: A single trader spent approximately $7 million systematically buying contracts on Mitt Romney on the platform Intrade. The goal was not to win the bet, but to artificially inflate Romney's perceived chances to boost voter enthusiasm and secure favorable media coverage. * The 2021 London mayoral race: Candidate Brian Rose was accused of having associates place bets on his victory on Smarkets, allowing him to point to the betting odds as proof of political momentum.

When a wealthy individual or campaign can alter market odds for a few million dollars to secure positive press coverage, the platform ceases to be a truth machine and becomes a public relations tool.

Quants, retail bettors, and the sharks and fish problem

The rise of prediction markets coincides with what Demetri Kofinas terms "financial nihilism"—the tendency of younger generations to reject traditional, slow wealth-building strategies in favor of high-risk crypto tokens and event contracts because traditional paths to financial security feel out of reach.

However, retail traders face a severe structural disadvantage. Major quantitative trading firms, such as Susquehanna and DRW, have established dedicated prediction market desks. These firms pay quants six-figure salaries to build algorithms that systematically identify and exploit mispriced contracts 24 hours a day.

This dynamic mirrors the online poker boom of the early 2000s. When professional players ("sharks") deployed methodical strategies and automated bots against casual players ("fish"), the survival time of recreational players plummeted. Eventually, the casual players realized they were donating money to server farms, stopped logging in, and the ecosystem collapsed. Because event contracts eventually resolve to an absolute true or false outcome, retail traders betting on "feelings" are structurally guaranteed to lose capital to quantitative algorithms over time.

Insider trading as a market feature

In traditional financial markets, trading on non-public material information carries severe criminal penalties. In prediction markets, however, proponents openly defend insider trading as a "feature" that drives rapid price discovery.

Recent events demonstrate how this works in practice: * Middle East military strikes: A Polymarket user under the pseudonym "Rico Suave 666" made highly lucrative, precisely timed bets on military actions. The Israeli government later arrested two individuals, including an army reservist, for allegedly using classified military intelligence to place those bets. * The capture of Nicolas Maduro: Immediately following the U.S. announcement of the operation, massive, highly confident bets were placed on Polymarket predicting his removal, yielding hundreds of thousands of dollars for an unknown bettor with clear insider access.

While advocates argue that allowing insiders to trade produces more accurate real-time forecasts, a Chart points out the ethical hazard of compromising military safety for the sake of "market efficiency." Furthermore, if ordinary participants realize the system is rigged by insiders, they will stop participating, destroying the market entirely.

The societal cost of frictionless gambling

Since the widespread legalization of sports betting in 2018, the U.S. has served as a massive case study on the impact of mobile gambling. Academic research highlighted by The Economist indicates that the introduction of online sports betting in a state is associated with an average 12-point drop in credit scores, alongside a rise in personal bankruptcies and loan delinquencies.

While proponents argue that adults in a free society should have the right to spend their money as they please, a Chart emphasizes that widespread financial distress is not a private issue. When millions of individuals default on loans and mortgages, the costs are absorbed by the broader financial system, and bankrupt individuals ultimately rely on taxpayer-funded social safety nets.

Ultimately, prediction markets function as a highly efficient wealth transfer system: the platforms collect transaction fees, quantitative algorithms and military/political insiders extract capital from retail bettors, and society is left to pick up the tab for the resulting financial fallout.
