Champion dismisses this promise as a political stunt, pointing out that similar bills are introduced in nearly every congressional session but never make it out of committee. Sales tax as an excise on privilege

To evaluate the constitutionality of a national sales tax, Champion categorizes how taxes are classified in U.S. law. He notes that the federal government's taxing power is limited by the U.S. Constitution to direct and indirect taxes. Because direct taxes are historically limited to taxes on real property and capitations, a national sales tax must be categorized as an indirect tax—specifically, an excise tax.

To support this classification, Champion references state-level tax codes, specifically citing California Revenue and Taxation Code Section 6051, which levies a tax "for the privilege of selling tangible personal property at retail." He also cites the federal court case American Airways v. Wallace, which held that "excise tax" and "privilege tax" are synonymous terms. Based on these references, Champion argues that a sales tax is legally defined as a tax imposed on the exercise of a government-granted privilege.

The constitutional argument against taxing natural rights

Champion argues that Congress lacks the constitutional authority to enact a national sales tax because buying, selling, and owning personal property are natural rights rather than government-granted privileges. He cites several historical court decisions to support this legal theory:

  • Corn v. Forth (1936): To argue that an individual's right to live and own property are natural rights upon which an excise tax cannot be imposed.
  • Butcher's Union Co. v. Crescent City Co. (1884): To argue that a man's labor is his personal property and a sacred right.
  • Coppedge v. Kansas (1915): To argue that personal liberty includes the right to make contracts for the acquisition of property and personal employment.

According to Champion, because a legislature cannot legally redefine a constitutional right as a privilege for the purpose of taxation, any federal retail sales tax levied on ordinary commerce would be unconstitutional.

Champion's unorthodox claims on federal income tax and labor

Champion extends his constitutional arguments to the current federal income tax system, presenting several highly contested claims regarding its legality and application:

  • Taxation of labor: Champion asserts that the federal government has no constitutional authority to tax personal labor and claims that Congress has never actually passed a law taxing the labor of ordinary Americans. He argues that citizens pay income tax out of fear or ignorance rather than legal obligation.
  • Income tax as an excise: He claims the Supreme Court has designated the income tax as an excise tax, meaning it must be levied on a privilege rather than a right.
  • The definition of withholding agents: Champion points to 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(16) and related provisions (Sections 1441, 1442, 1443, and 1461) to argue that statutory tax withholding is legally restricted to foreign corporations and nonresident aliens. He claims that ordinary American workers do not legally qualify as subjects of federal tax withholding.

Champion concludes that political leaders maintain the current tax system through what he characterizes as a long-running public disinformation campaign, knowing they cannot easily replace it with alternative constitutional models.
