EurodollarU | You Won’t Believe What McDonald’s Just Revealed About U.S. Consumers

McDonald’s declining sales and plunging consumer confidence reveal a fragile U.S. economy where forced spending masks a deeper crisis of stagnant incomes and dwindling job opportunities.

The McDonald's warning sign

McDonald's, historically a resilient haven during economic downturns due to its affordability, is struggling. Jeff Snyder of EurodollarU highlights that the company is lowering its outlook, expecting negative U.S. sales, and preparing an $8.5 billion support plan for squeezed franchisees. This struggle indicates that the low-income consumer is tapped out, unable to afford even basic fast food as franchise owners face rising labor, ingredient, and operating costs that prevent them from offering deep discounts.

The illusion of the August spending surge

While August data showed a surge in consumer spending, Snyder argues this is not a sign of economic resilience. Instead, it represents "panic buying" or demand pulled forward. Facing energy shocks and fearing future price hikes, consumers made major purchases early. Crucially, real disposable income remained flat (and negative when excluding transfer payments), forcing households to draw down their savings rate to finance this temporary spending burst.

Labor market stagnation and the JOLTS data

The underlying weakness of the economy is reflected in the August JOLTS report. Job openings fell significantly, hiring remained flat at recessionary levels, and the quits rate hovered near its lowest point since 2020. Snyder explains that a low quits rate demonstrates a lack of worker confidence; employees are holding onto their current jobs because finding a replacement is increasingly difficult. Rather than expanding, employers are taking a defensive posture—retaining existing staff to handle temporary order spikes rather than committing to new hires.

Why energy shocks destroy demand rather than fuel inflation

Snyder challenges the conventional economic view that rising energy prices are inherently inflationary. For an energy shock to cause sustained, economy-wide inflation, it must be accompanied by robust income growth that allows consumers to absorb higher costs. Without this income mechanism, higher gas and utility prices act as a tax, forcing consumers to cut back on discretionary spending like dining out. This dynamic compresses business margins and ultimately destroys demand rather than fueling a durable inflationary spiral.

The divergence between economic models and consumer reality

The Conference Board’s September Consumer Confidence Index plunged to its lowest level since 2014, with consumers expressing deep pessimism about jobs, income, and business conditions. Snyder criticizes mainstream economists who dismiss these sentiment surveys simply because the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) has not declared a formal recession. He asserts that the lived experience of everyday consumers—facing stagnant wages, disappearing job vacancies, and high costs of living—is a far more accurate gauge of the economy's health than aggregate GDP figures.
