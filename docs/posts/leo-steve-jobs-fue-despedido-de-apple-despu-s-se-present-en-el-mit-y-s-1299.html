In an unvarnished lecture at MIT, Steve Jobs discusses NeXT's software strategy, the battle over enterprise computing, and key management lessons learned from leaving Apple.

Executive summary

Speaking to business students at MIT in 1992, Steve Jobs outlines the strategic thesis behind NeXT Computer, the evolution of corporate computing, and lessons learned from his career. Jobs argues that personal computers historically improved management productivity but failed to enhance operational productivity. NeXT's objective was to capture the enterprise market by enabling rapid, custom application development through object-oriented software (NeXTStep). Jobs also shares candid insights on why software holds a longer competitive moat than hardware, the limitations of management consulting, the competitive threat of Microsoft and Sun, and how his leadership style matured after leaving Apple.

Operational productivity and the next computing revolution

  • Management vs. operational productivity: Citing Paul Strassman’s book The Business Value of Computers, Jobs notes that top-performing companies spend the same percentage of revenue (~2%) on IT as underperforming ones. The key difference is that successful companies invest in operational applications (e.g., custom trading platforms or hospital operations) rather than simple management productivity tools like desktop spreadsheets.
  • The custom software bottleneck: Enterprise front offices previously waited years for IT departments to build mission-critical tools on mainframes or Sun workstations. NeXTStep aimed to compress development cycles from two years to 90 days.
  • The Trojan horse strategy: Just as desktop publishing became the unexpected killer app that brought the Macintosh into corporate America, rapid custom application development became NeXT’s primary growth engine for enterprise accounts.

Software moats and the business model of hardware

  • Transient hardware advantage: Hardware innovations cycle every 18 months, making hardware-based competitive advantages short-lived (lasting roughly six months). In contrast, software platforms take competitors four to five years to replicate.
  • Why NeXT sells hardware: Despite software being the true moat, software-only companies cannot support a direct sales force with $500 software packages. Selling $5,000 hardware systems provides the margin required to maintain 130 direct field salespeople to educate and create market demand.
  • Porting NeXTStep to Intel: Jobs highlights the strategy to port NeXTStep to Intel 486 chips, expanding market access while maintaining NeXT's high-end hardware line.

The competitive landscape

  • Sun Microsystems: Jobs views Sun as lagging in software innovation, noting that Solaris lacks object-oriented architecture.
  • Microsoft: Windows NT is categorized as better operating system plumbing for Windows, but Jobs criticizes its development environment as non-object-oriented and difficult to build custom enterprise applications on.
  • Taligent (Apple/IBM joint venture): Recognized as NeXT's first true potential competitor in object-oriented software, though Jobs predicts Taligent will take until mid-decade to deliver what NeXT already offers.

Leadership, consulting, and manufacturing lessons

  • The limits of consulting: Jobs critiques management consulting, arguing that advisors who lack multi-year operational ownership never accumulate the necessary "scar tissue" or three-dimensional understanding to truly learn from mistakes.
  • Reflections on Apple: Addressing his departure from Apple, Jobs expresses regret over the split but notes Apple chose to become the "Sony of computers" by focusing heavily on portables (PowerBooks) while abdicating the high-power desktop user market.
  • Evolution in management style: Jobs reflects that his leadership matured toward taking a longer-term view on people—focusing on coaching and developing team members through mistakes rather than immediately stepping in to fix problems himself.
  • Consensus at the top: At NeXT, executive decisions were managed through an eight-person policy team focused on reaching genuine consensus on major strategic issues rather than forcing top-down compliance.
  • Lean manufacturing: Jobs emphasizes the importance of Just-in-Time (JIT) manufacturing and avoiding high-tech automated warehouses, which he realized were merely expensive places to hold defective inventory.
