Europe's economic cracks are revealing a warning for America's financial future.

The four cracks in Europe and America

Felix Nicholas Preen, an economist and former investment banker, outlines four structural weaknesses in Europe that he argues are already affecting America.

Crack one: promises nobody can pay for

For about 40 years, European governments promised that hard work and taxes would secure pensions and healthcare for retirees. However, these systems were never funded in advance.

  • The money paid today goes directly to retirees, not saved in a growing pot.
  • With people living 20 years longer and fewer children, the number of people retiring exceeds the number of people working.
  • This imbalance means pension systems are unsustainable.
  • France attempted to fix this by raising retirement age from 62 to 64, but the move triggered widespread unrest.

This is not a French problem—it is a mathematical one. When a government promises more than it can pay, there are only three outcomes: - Break the promise (as France nearly did) - Tax people more (which the UK is attempting) - Print money to cover the gap

The third option—printing money—is the one that quietly affects personal portfolios.

Crack two: the Young generation locked out

In most European cities, young people under 35 cannot afford homes that their parents had at their age.

  • House prices have outpaced wages.
  • Rents consume half of a typical paycheck.
  • Secure, well-paying jobs are becoming rare.

This sense of being excluded from the economic ladder fuels social unrest.

  • Young people feel the ladder was pulled from beneath them.
  • This lack of stake in society makes it unstable.
  • It directly feeds into the pension crisis, as fewer births mean fewer future workers.

This pattern is already emerging in America, with similar trends in major cities.

Crack three: making things has become too expensive

Germany, once the 'workshop of the world,' is now shutting factories for the first time in 89 years.

  • Energy costs in Germany are 3 to 4 times higher than in America or Asia.
  • This makes it uncompetitive for manufacturers, even if they are world-class.
  • Companies like Volkswagen and BASF are relocating operations abroad.

This trend affects entire supply chains, reducing wages, tax revenue, and industrial capacity.

The cost of energy is the hidden killer in Europe’s industrial base.

Crack four: Money and talent are leaving

Wealthy individuals and companies are moving out of Europe.

  • Britain is now one of the top five places where the wealthy are leaving.
  • Norway raised taxes on the rich, prompting many to move to Switzerland.
  • Companies like Wise and Flutter have moved their stock listings to New York.

  • CRH, a major building company, did the same.

  • These moves are not publicized—they happen quietly.

  • Money flows to where it is treated best, and away from where it is treated worst.
  • When money and talent leave, the country becomes poorer, its currency weakens, and savers lose value.

This crack is the one that directly affects personal portfolios.

America’s position: not yet in crisis

America is not yet experiencing the fourth crack.

  • The U.S. still has a strong advantage: cheap energy and global trust in its financial system.
  • Factory decisions are still favoring America due to lower energy costs.

  • The U.S. is currently the destination for global capital.

  • The American pension system is also under strain, projected to run short by the mid-2030s.

  • Wage growth has not kept pace with asset prices, and young people face the same housing crisis as in Europe.

However, the U.S. still holds two key advantages: - Cheap energy - The trust of the world’s money

  • Step two: Evaluate how your assets will be affected by inflation from money printing.
  • Step three: Learn about how money moves in the market—this is where compounding benefits begin.
