The Basement: Julia Mossbridge | The Future Is Sending Us Information, and Science Can Prove It

Julia Mossbridge discusses precognition, time travel, and her childhood experiences in government programs.

Dr. Julia Mossbridge is a cognitive neuroscientist who spent her early career cutting the heads off rats under a red light before they started showing up in her dreams. She eventually left her PhD program to study time, from whale songs to brain waves to beams of light. Today, she discusses the science of precognition, how the body reacts to events half a second before they happen, and what she's building in a closet in her office.

Key insights

  • The soul and mental illness: Mossbridge argues that viewing mental illness as a broken machine is less compassionate than viewing it as a possession or a soul being pushed aside.
  • Psychophysics and subjective experience: She explains that psychophysics was developed to measure subjective experience during the era of behaviorism, which denied its existence.
  • Pre-sentiment and precognition: Mossbridge distinguishes between pre-sentiment (unconscious physiological response, 100 milliseconds to 10 seconds) and precognition (conscious, potentially years out).
  • Two mechanisms of precognition: She proposes two ways information might reach the present: a "stone in a pond" mechanism where the brain sends signals both forward and backward in time, and a "universal consciousness" mechanism where information is sent from the universe itself.
  • Gender differences in intuition: Mossbridge notes that men and women seem to have different intuitive targets, with men often intuiting victory or sex and women intuiting protection or fear.

The Science of precognition

Mossbridge categorizes precognition on a spectrum of consciousness and delay. At one end is pre-sentiment, an unconscious physiological response on a 100-millisecond to 10-second timescale. In the middle is unconscious behavioral precognition, like Daryl Bem's "Feeling the Future" experiments, which operate on seconds-to-minutes delays. At the other extreme is conscious precognitive remote viewing, where the viewer is aware they are accessing future information.

She believes that the unconscious, shorter-delay experiments are more replicable than the conscious, longer-delay ones. Mossbridge also discusses her own physiological experiments, which found brain activity differentiating 550 milliseconds before a choice was made, suggesting a form of precognition about one's own body's actions.

A time machine in a closet

Mossbridge describes an experiment she developed to test if photons could interfere with their future selves. She hypothesized that if a photon is emitted in a group, the group's "identity" or "event" is defined by the duration of the emission. She built a device, Amelia, to test this by measuring photon absorption at different points in a five-minute interval to see if the pattern of absorption could predict the future duration of the interval.

She reports that the device, built with her friend Wynne Williams, can now predict the random number generator's choice at a rate higher than chance, with 60% accuracy. She says this technology could be used to predict binary events like whether a hurricane will be a category 7 or not, and that it is being developed for both the intelligence community and the private sector.

Childhood experiences and government programs

Mossbridge recounts being pulled out of class as a child for "hearing tests" that involved tones, clicks, and vision tests, and later being placed in a separate "Gate" program. She describes these experiences as secretive and unethical, with no consent forms. She believes the program may have been a continuation of MKUltra, testing children for psychic abilities or to interact with aliens.

She also shares a story about being recruited by the federal government after filing a FOIA request, and being offered a job and security clearance within days. She declined the job but kept the clearance, which she says is still active.

Non-speaking autistic people and telepathy

Mossbridge discusses her work with non-speaking autistic people, whom she believes have access to a different kind of cognition. She argues that they are not "authorship" cases where a communication partner is secretly spelling for them, pointing to experiments where the partner is out of the room and cannot see the stimulus. She believes these individuals have a form of telepathy and are accessing the "informational substrate" of the universe.

She also discusses the "right hemisphere release hypothesis," suggesting that the left hemisphere's inhibition of the right hemisphere is less effective in non-speaking autistic people, allowing them to access more intuitive and emotional information.

Unconditional love as a practice

Mossbridge believes that unconditional love is a human response to a physical force in the universe. She has developed a survey to measure this feeling and has found that people in a high state of unconditional love are statistically more likely to be able to do precognition. She suggests that practicing unconditional love is like practicing flying—it's not something you do, but something you ask for and allow yourself to receive.
