Warren Buffett on investing, character, and business wisdom (1998)

Overview

In this 1998 talk to MBA students, Warren Buffett opens with a provocative thought experiment about character and personal integrity, then fields wide-ranging questions on investing philosophy, business selection, and life choices. He emphasizes that success depends less on IQ or energy than on behavioral traits—honesty, humility, and discipline—that are entirely within your control. Throughout, he stresses the primacy of understanding what you invest in, avoiding leverage, and staying within your "circle of competence."

Five key insights

  1. Character is more important than intelligence. Buffett invites students to imagine buying 10% of a classmate's lifetime earnings. Most would not pick the highest IQ, but rather someone with integrity, humility, and leadership qualities. These traits are habits you can choose, especially when young.

  2. Never risk what you need for what you don't need. The Long-Term Capital Management collapse—involving 16 brilliant people with 350+ years of combined experience—illustrates how smart people make dumb decisions when they leverage heavily. Buffett argues that if you already have enough money, risking it for more is irrational.

  3. Stay within your circle of competence. Buffett avoids macro predictions and industries he doesn't fully understand (e.g., electric utilities post-deregulation). He only invests in businesses he can understand with confidence, even if that limits his universe.

  4. Buy wonderful businesses at fair prices, not fair businesses at wonderful prices. A business with a durable competitive "moat"—like Coca-Cola's brand and distribution—compounds value over decades. Cheap cigar-butt stocks (low-return businesses) rarely deliver long-term wealth.

  5. Work in a job you love, not one that looks good on your resume. Buffett argues that if you're already wealthy, optimizing for salary or prestige is like "saving up sex for your old age." Choose work that would excite you even if you were independently wealthy.

Character and personal integrity

Buffett frames success not as a function of IQ or energy—which most MBA students already possess—but of behavioral traits: integrity, honesty, generosity, and freedom from egotism and greed. He cites Pete Kiewit's hiring principle: look for integrity, intelligence, and energy, but if someone lacks integrity, "you want them dumb and lazy" rather than smart and energetic.

The key insight: the qualities that make you admirable or off-putting are not innate talents but habits of behavior and temperament. At the students' age, these habits are still malleable. Buffett references Ben Graham and Ben Franklin, who deliberately chose to emulate admirable people. He urges students to write down the qualities they admire and make them habitual, because "you already own 100% and you're stuck with it."

Investing discipline and leverage

Buffett recounts the Long-Term Capital Management crisis (September 1998). Despite the fund's extraordinary talent—including two Nobel Prize winners and decades of combined experience—it nearly collapsed because its partners risked their substantial personal wealth for returns they didn't need. Buffett argues this is "plain foolish" regardless of odds: if you hand him a gun with a million chambers and one bullet, no sum will convince him to pull the trigger at his temple.

He invokes Walter Gutman's book title: You Only Have to Get Rich Once. Once you have enough, the downside of leverage (humiliation, loss of capital, damage to reputation) vastly outweighs the upside of an extra 10% or 20% return. Buffett has never borrowed money, even when he had only $10,000, because it made no difference to his lifestyle or happiness.

The circle of competence

Buffett refuses to predict macro conditions (interest rates, currency movements, geopolitical events) because he views them as unknowable and unimportant to his decisions. Instead, he focuses on what is knowable: the economics of specific businesses.

He illustrates this with Japan in 1998. Berkshire could borrow at 1% for 10 years in yen, but Buffett found few Japanese businesses earning more than 1% on equity. Rather than guess whether the yen would strengthen or the economy would recover, he simply waited. He notes that Japanese companies earned very low returns on equity—a structural problem—and that "time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the lousy business."

Buffett also declines to invest in electric utilities post-deregulation because he cannot confidently predict who will profit in a deregulated market. He understands the product and the industry but not the future competitive structure.

Business selection: moats and pricing power

Buffett seeks businesses with a durable competitive advantage ("moat") that protect them from rivals. He contrasts Coca-Cola with Eastman Kodak. Thirty years ago, both had wide moats; Kodak's brand promised that photos would last decades. But Fuji eroded Kodak's moat by sponsoring the Olympics, narrowing the perceived gap. Coca-Cola's moat has widened: every new bottling plant in an emerging market strengthens its distribution.

He emphasizes "share of mind" over market share. Coca-Cola owns a space in consumers' minds worldwide. Disney similarly commands pricing power because mothers trust Disney videos without having to evaluate alternatives. See's Candy, which Buffett bought in 1972 for $25 million, had untapped pricing power: the emotional association with romance and gift-giving allowed price increases without volume loss.

Buffett's test: "Give me $10 billion and tell me to hurt Coca-Cola. I can't do it." That's a moat.

Valuation and long-term ownership

Buffett does not buy stocks with price targets. He buys businesses he would be happy to own forever. If the New York Stock Exchange closed for five years, he should be content with his holdings based on their business fundamentals, not daily price quotes.

He illustrates this with Coca-Cola's history. The stock went public in 1919 at $40, fell to $19 within a year (a 50% loss), and faced numerous headwinds: sugar price increases, bottler rebellions, the Great Depression, World War II, sugar rationing. Yet if you bought one share for $40 and reinvested dividends, it would be worth about $5 million by 1998. "The timing part is a very tricky thing," but if you're right about the business, timing matters far less.

Mistakes and omission

Buffett's biggest investment mistakes have been mistakes of omission, not commission. He passed up opportunities in healthcare stocks (when the Clinton health plan collapsed), Fannie Mae, and other businesses he understood but didn't act on. These represent "billion-dollar mistakes" that accounting never captures.

He also made mistakes of commission: buying US Air Preferred (an attractive security in an unattractive business) and Salomon (same error). He now has an "800 number" he calls when tempted to buy airline stocks, joking that he's an "air-holic."

The lesson: stay within your circle of competence. If you can't state a clear reason for buying a stock—beyond a tip or a chart pattern—don't buy it.

Diversification: for professionals vs. amateurs

Buffett draws a sharp distinction. For non-professional investors, extreme diversification through low-cost index funds is appropriate. But for those who commit to understanding businesses, diversification is "a terrible mistake."

If you can identify six wonderful businesses, that's all the diversification you need. "Very few people have gotten rich on their seventh best idea." Buffett himself does not diversify and notes that most successful investors he knows (except Walter Schloss, whom he calls "Noah" for owning two of everything) concentrate their capital.

Coca-Cola and the Asian crisis

When asked about Coca-Cola's recent earnings warning amid the Asian financial crisis, Buffett expresses enthusiasm. Short-term pain is irrelevant if the long-term business is sound. Coca-Cola's per capita consumption will grow faster internationally than in the US over the next 20 years.

He notes a unique property of cola: it has no taste memory. Unlike cream soda or root beer, which accumulate and become cloying, cola can be consumed multiple times daily indefinitely. This drives per capita consumption to levels impossible for other beverages. Over 1.8 billion 8-ounce servings of Coca-Cola products are sold daily worldwide, and this will grow.

Buffett also reflects on Coca-Cola's pricing power. In 1936, he bought six bottles for a quarter and sold them for a nickel each. Today, a 12-ounce can costs roughly 20 cents in bulk—not much more than twice the per-ounce price of 1936, despite inflation. Yet the product is more valuable than ever.

Wall Street vs. omaha

Buffett explains why he avoids Wall Street. The environment overstimulates activity. "Wall Street makes its money on activity. You make your money on inactivity." If everyone in a room traded portfolios daily, all would end up broke and brokers would capture all gains. Conversely, if a group owned average businesses and sat still for 50 years, they'd be wealthy and brokers would be broke.

He compares brokers to doctors paid per pill change: they profit from activity, not health. Buffett's goal is one good idea per year, fully exploited. That's nearly impossible in an environment of constant price quotes and reports.

Berkshire hathaway's philosophy

Berkshire does not pay dividends because every dollar retained has historically turned into more than a dollar of value. The company is run for owners, not for size or prestige. Buffett and Charlie Munger judge themselves solely on whether retained capital generates returns above the cost of capital.

Berkshire has only 12 people at headquarters and 45,000 employees across its subsidiaries, in 3,500 square feet. Buffett promises this will never change. The company is measured by performance, not by the size of its home office.

Real estate and REITs

Buffett notes that securitization of real estate has created opportunities but also challenges. REITs avoid double taxation (corporate income tax plus shareholder tax) but typically charge 1–1.5% in operating expenses. On an 8% yield, this leaves only 6.5–7%, which is unattractive compared to owning property directly if you have sufficient capital.

He observes that people often misvalue land companies by extrapolating small annual sales to total asset value, ignoring illiquidity. Real estate is far less liquid than stocks, and moving large quantities of property is difficult.

Buffett remains open to large real estate transactions if they offer compelling value, but the field has generated few opportunities.

The ovarian lottery and social design

In closing, Buffett asks students to imagine designing the world 24 hours before birth, knowing they'll draw one ball from a barrel of 5.8 billion—not knowing their race, gender, intelligence, health, or wealth. What system would they design?

He argues this thought experiment illuminates social policy. You'd want a system that produces abundant goods and services (so everyone lives well) but also provides for those unlucky enough to be poorly wired for that system. Buffett acknowledges his extraordinary luck: born in a wealthy country, with parents who nurtured him, and with a mind suited to capital allocation in a market economy.

He concludes that if he could live again, he'd do almost exactly what he's done—except he wouldn't have bought US Air. He works only with people he likes, choosing fulfillment over money. "I only work with people I like. If I could make $100 million by buying a business with some guy that caused my stomach to churn, I'd say no."
