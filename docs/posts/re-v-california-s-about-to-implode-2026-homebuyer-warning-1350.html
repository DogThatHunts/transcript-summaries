California's housing market in 2026: demand collapse and hidden cracks

Overview

California is experiencing its deepest homebuyer recession in recent history. The California Association of Realtors reports home sales down nearly 30 percent below pre-pandemic norms—the longest and largest demand recession on record. Yet despite this collapse, statewide prices have declined only 1.7 percent year-over-year through February 2026, creating a paradox that masks significant regional deterioration and affordability crises.

Key dynamics

Population exodus and demand collapse

California lost 229,000 people to outbound migration in 2025, continuing a multi-year trend. Los Angeles County alone shed nearly 54,000 residents between 2024 and 2025—the largest population decline of any U.S. county. This exodus has created demand so weak that RE-V argues it has "never been seen before" in California's recent history. Yet despite losing over a million people in four to five years, the housing market remains expensive, creating a fundamental mismatch between supply, demand, and price.

The mortgage lock-in effect

The primary reason prices haven't crashed is a massive mortgage rate lock-in. Nearly one-third of California's mortgage holders (28 percent) have rates below 3 percent, while only 16 percent have rates above 6 percent. This is the highest concentration of sub-3% mortgages in the nation. Homeowners with cheap mortgages have little incentive to sell, especially at a loss. By contrast, Florida shows the opposite pattern: 25 percent of mortgage holders there have rates above 6 percent, creating more selling pressure and price declines.

This lock-in effect artificially constrains inventory and prevents price discovery. Sellers refuse to list or cut prices because they're comfortable holding their homes. RE-V argues this dynamic will eventually break, but only as current owners age, relocate, or face financial pressure—a process that could take years.

Buy-versus-rent differential signals danger

A critical metric RE-V highlights is the buy-versus-rent differential. In many California zip codes, the monthly cost to buy (including mortgage, taxes, insurance) is now more than double the rental cost. One San Diego example shows an $8,700 monthly mortgage payment versus a $4,000 monthly rental estimate for the same property—a 150 percent differential. Historically, buying has been slightly more expensive than renting in California, but since 2021 the gap has become "out of control." This signals that renting may be the rational choice for many buyers, further suppressing demand.

Regional bifurcation: winners and losers

California's market is deeply fragmented. San Francisco County is booming, driven by AI industry demand. The LA Times reports median home prices above $2 million, up 18 percent year-over-year, with inventory down nearly 30 percent. RE-V forecasts a +2.4 percent appreciation for San Francisco County.

Elsewhere, the picture is dire: - Oakland: Down 25 percent in three and a half years; a townhouse listed at a 23 percent loss to 2024 purchase price. - Los Angeles and Hollywood: Values down 16 percent in three years; downtown LA and Hollywood showing consistent declines. - Pacific Palisades: Down 11 percent despite fire damage in early 2025. - San Diego: Multiple pockets down double digits; the overall market showing "a sea of blue" on RE-V's forecast map, signaling imminent downturns.

Orange County remains relatively resilient, with some areas up 27 percent since mid-2022, but even there houses are staying on the market longer than any point in the past decade—a buyer-friendly signal.

Forecasts and outlook

RE-V claims its 2025 forecasts were six times more accurate than Zillow's. The overall prediction: no statewide crash in 2026, but continued downward pressure in specific zip codes and counties. The reason is insufficient inventory; too many sub-3% mortgage holders will continue to resist listing or cutting prices.

However, demand is so weak that sellers will gradually become more realistic. This process will likely take years as older owners move, relocate, or face financial pressure. In the meantime, pockets of opportunity exist for buyers willing to search: areas with negative forecasts offer more negotiating leverage, especially if a property has been on the market longer.

The fundamental problem is simple: California is too expensive relative to rents, affordability is broken, insurance costs are rising, and outbound migration continues. These headwinds will sustain record-low buyer demand regardless of price declines.
