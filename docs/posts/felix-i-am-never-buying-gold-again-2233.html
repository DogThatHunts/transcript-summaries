I am never buying 'gold' again

Overview

Felix examines four distinct ways to gain gold exposure, arguing that the most popular method—gold ETFs—silently shrinks your holdings and fails as insurance. He presents a nuanced verdict: physical metal as foundation, miners for leverage, and royalty companies as a superior alternative to mining stocks.

The problem with gold ETFs

The largest gold fund on the planet, GLD, holds over $150 billion in assets. Felix identifies a critical flaw buried in its prospectus: the amount of gold backing each share declines every year. The fund charges a 0.4% annual fee by selling off gold to cover its costs. When GLD launched in 2004, one share was backed by one-tenth of an ounce; today it's backed by approximately nine-hundredths of an ounce. This erosion continues indefinitely.

Felix also highlights three structural risks:

  1. No redemption for retail investors. Only authorized participants (Goldman Sachs, JP Morgan, Morgan Stanley, HSBC, etc.) can swap shares for physical metal, and only in blocks of 100,000 shares—roughly $40 million at current prices. Ordinary investors own a claim on gold, not gold itself.

  2. The custody chain. You own a share of a trust that owns a claim on a custodian bank, which may hand the gold to subcustodians. The fund's own documents admit its ability to inspect or take legal action down that chain is limited. The actual bar doesn't know you exist.

  3. Paper gold outruns physical metal. COMEX registered gold (metal available for delivery) totals about 14.5 million ounces, down 23% in the past year. Yet there are roughly 40 million ounces of open claims—less than half could ever be delivered in theory. As Felix's mentor advised: "Follow what can't be printed." Gold can't be printed, but claims on gold can be printed all day long.

The insurance paradox

Felix argues that if you buy a gold ETF as insurance against institutional collapse, you've made a logical error: you've bought your insurance from the institutions and stored it with the institutions. It's like buying a lifeboat bolted to the ship. For price exposure alone, ETFs work fine. But for genuine insurance, they fail.

Method 1: Physical metal (the foundation)

Gold coins and bars have no counterparty risk, no CEO, no bankruptcy clause, and no ability to suspend redemptions. If you own it and know where it is, that's it—no one's terms and conditions apply.

Key considerations: - Buy from large, reputable dealers, never corner shops - Larger pieces carry smaller markups; you can negotiate them - You pay a small premium over the paper gold price for the privilege of holding it - Past a certain amount, proper storage is essential

The critical word: allocated. This means specific bars with serial numbers, a bar list with your name against it, sitting outside the storage firm's balance sheet. If that firm goes bust, no one can claim your gold. Central banks understand this—they bought over 1,000 tons in the past year alone, and they took numbered bars into vaults they control, not index fund units.

Method 2: mining stocks (leverage with risk)

Gold miners have mostly fixed costs (payroll, equipment). When gold prices rise 20%, the gap between production cost and selling price can expand far more than 20%—this is leverage. Conversely, on the way down, the same leverage shreds profits.

Felix and his co-host have made good money on miners, but the channel has been honest about the downside: strikes, floods, management blunders, and operational risks cut both ways. Miners also carry business risk beyond metal price movements.

Basket funds like GDX offer sector exposure without picking individual stocks. Felix recommends doing homework on what they own, where mines are based, and which countries you're exposed to.

Method 3: royalty and streaming companies (the elegant model)

These firms hand miners upfront cash to build mines, then receive either: - A royalty: a small slice of revenue (1–5%) forever, or - A stream: the right to buy a chunk of production at a fixed discount (typically ~20% of market price)

The business model is lean: no trucks, no diesel, no 10,000-person payroll—just a small team in a low-tax jurisdiction collecting payments on everything the mine produces.

Franco-Nevada Corporation exemplifies this. With ~40 employees and a $50 billion market cap, it keeps over 70 cents of every revenue dollar after costs, compared to miners who keep roughly 40 cents. When mine costs blow out, it's the mine's problem, not theirs. You get gold leverage with far less operational risk.

The catch: These companies are not free money. They're still exposed to gold prices and mine production. A royalty on a shut-down mine is a royalty on dust. They must execute smart deals perpetually, and the market knows how good this model is—you rarely get them cheap.

Sizing your allocation

Gold should be an insurance policy, not a wealth-building bet. Serious investors typically allocate 5–15% to gold; some family offices go to 20%. Central banks are buying 1,000+ tons per year. But if you're at 90% gold, you've joined a cult.

The allocation structure: - Foundation: Physical metal (largest slice) - Leverage: Miners and royalties (smaller slices on top) - Never: 50% or more in gold

Verdict

Felix has abandoned gold ETFs entirely, not out of fear of imminent collapse, but because he refuses to call a promise "insurance." For price exposure, ETFs work. For genuine protection, own allocated physical metal. For leverage, consider royalty companies over miners—they offer similar upside with less operational risk. Do your homework, don't follow hot tips, and remember: gold is insurance, not a get-rich scheme.
