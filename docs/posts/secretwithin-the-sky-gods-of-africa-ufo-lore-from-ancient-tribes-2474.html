The Kikuyu and Mount Kenya

Mount Kenya, known as Kirin-Yaga to the Kikuyu, is the sacred dwelling place of Ngai, their supreme god. The Kikuyu have maintained rich oral traditions despite colonial-era Christianization. Colonial administrator C.W. Hobley documented Kikuyu elder testimonies describing villagers vanishing without trace, taken by sky beings in floating craft. Some abductees returned days or weeks later with fragmented memories, while others claimed encounters with otherworldly realms or gained uncanny abilities. In rare cases, the abducted never returned.

The Dogon and Sirius b

The Dogon, living along the Bandiagera Escarpment in Mali, are renowned for their astronomical knowledge and intricate cosmology. In the 1930s–40s, French anthropologists documented that the Dogon possessed awareness of Sirius B—a white dwarf companion to Sirius A—long before Western telescopes confirmed its existence. The Dogon described it as a small, incredibly dense, invisible star, details that align with modern astronomy. The Zulu and the Chitauri

According to Zulu shaman Kredo Mutwa's teachings, the Zulu remember a time when beings called the Chitauri descended from the stars in great shining vessels that "thundered across the sky, filling the air with fire and smoke." These sky visitors were described as tall, reptilian, and physical—not mere spirits. They brought knowledge of fire, metal, and earth's secrets, but demanded obedience and set themselves up as rulers. Zulu oral tradition preserves these memories in stories of thunder and lightning, ancestor veneration, and rituals honoring sky spirits. Some contemporary Zulu elders report witnessing strange lights in the night sky and dreams of the old gods' return.

The Yoruba Orisha and celestial realms

In Yoruba cosmology, the sky is alive with the Orisha—divine beings who travel between celestial and earthly realms. Olodumare, the supreme creator, dwells high above but rarely intervenes directly. Orunmila, the deity of wisdom and prophecy, descended from the sky bearing Ipha, a divination system revealing hidden cosmic patterns. Obatala, father of the Orisha, was sent down by Olodumare with earth, a white hen, and a palm nut to create the land and first humans. Oshun travels on beams of sunlight, and Shango, the Thunder God, lights the sky with fiery bolts. While not explicitly UFO lore, these myths involve sky-dwelling beings descending to shape the earth and humanity.

The San, the kalahari, and shamanic journeys

The San people, among Earth's oldest cultures, have gazed at the night sky for millennia. Their ancient rock art depicts not only animals and landscapes but also strange, otherworldly figures with elongated limbs, large eyes, and halos or rays of light. Some researchers interpret these images as records of shamanic journeys or encounters with non-terrestrial beings. In Kagan mythology, the trickster mantis Kagan created the moon and stars and could transform and travel between earth and heavens. The San believe stars are their ancestors' campfires and shooting stars are messages from the spirit world. In trance dances—powerful healing rituals—shamans enter altered states, journeying through tunnels of light and meeting luminous beings, returning with wisdom. Some of these visions speak of visitors who do not belong to this world.

The Egyptians believed in the Duat, a mysterious afterlife realm reached only by traveling through the sky. Pharaohs and priests mapped the heavens with precision, believing the souls of the dead would become stars themselves. Some ancient texts and temple carvings describe strange luminous objects and beings descending from the heavens—imagery that has inspired modern speculation about ancient extraterrestrial contact. The pyramids, aligned with the stars, stand as witnesses to the Egyptians' obsession with the sky and its secrets.

The Ashanti and Nyame

The Ashanti people of Ghana look to the sky for guidance and divine connection. Nyame, the great sky god, is their supreme creator and ruler of the universe, dwelling high above the clouds and controlling the sun, moon, stars, and rain. According to Ashanti legend, the sky was once much closer to earth, and people could speak directly to Nyame; the gods would visit and share wisdom and power. Human carelessness or disrespect caused the sky to retreat higher and higher until it became unreachable, yet the connection was never fully broken. The Ashanti honor Nyame through prayers, rituals, and festivals. They also revere Anansi, the clever spider and trickster, who serves as a messenger between earth and sky. In one famous tale, Anansi journeys to the heavens to bargain with Nyame for the world's stories, bringing them back to humanity—a reminder that the sky is a place of wisdom, stories, and endless possibility.
