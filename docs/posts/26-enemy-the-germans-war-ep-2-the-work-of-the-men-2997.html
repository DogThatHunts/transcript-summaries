#26 Enemy: The Germans' War, ep. 2 - The Work of the Men

How the collapse of empires, mass starvation, and communist revolution transformed ordinary Germans into soldiers of a new and darker cause.

Enemy: the Germans' War, episode 2 - the Work of the Men

Overview

Daryl Cooper narrates the second episode of his series on World War II from the German perspective, focusing on the period immediately following World War I's end in 1918. Rather than ending with the armistice, the episode traces the chaos, revolution, and violence that consumed Central Europe in the months that followed, and how those events shaped German consciousness and politics in ways that would prove catastrophic.

One-Paragraph summary

Between November 1918 and January 1919, Germany and Austria descended into revolutionary chaos while their populations starved under a continued Allied blockade. The episode opens with the false belief among German soldiers that the war had merely paused, then documents the shock of revolution—the abdication of the Kaiser, the rise of communist movements backed by Bolshevik Russia, and the emergence of Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg as revolutionary leaders. Cooper provides extensive context about the Bolshevik terror in Russia, the starvation policies imposed on Germany despite the armistice, and the formation of armed militias (Freikorps) by returning soldiers. The narrative culminates in the January 1919 Spartacist uprising in Berlin, where Liebknecht and Luxemburg were killed by Freikorps soldiers, and establishes how these traumatic events—particularly the perceived betrayal of the army and the specter of Bolshevism—would radicalize an entire generation of German men.

Five key insights

  1. The war did not end in November 1918 for most Germans. The armistice was signed, but the Allied blockade remained in place, starving the German and Austrian populations. Hundreds of thousands died of hunger after the fighting stopped, creating a sense of betrayal that would poison German politics for years.

  2. The Bolshevik Revolution in Russia was far more brutal and chaotic than commonly understood. Cooper documents how the Bolsheviks deliberately encouraged chaos, executed tens of thousands without trial, used starvation as a weapon against peasants, and created a system of terror that shocked even hardened observers. This reality—not theoretical communism—is what Germans feared when they saw Liebknecht and Luxemburg calling for "world revolution."

  3. Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg were a tiny minority, but they were extraordinarily effective at inserting themselves into major events and claiming leadership. The Spartacist League never had more than a handful of delegates to the Workers' Councils Congress, yet through aggressive public demonstrations and willingness to use force, they became the face of the revolution in German minds.

  4. The Freikorps soldiers were not inherently fascist, but they were traumatized men of war who had been humiliated and betrayed. These were front-line veterans who had survived the trenches, returned home to see their flags burned and their sacrifices mocked by civilians, and were then asked to defend a government they blamed for losing the war. Their violence was not ideological at first—it was personal.

  5. The pattern of Jewish leadership in communist movements, as perceived by Germans at the time, became a central organizing principle for right-wing politics. Cooper notes that more than half of the Russian Bolshevik delegation at peace negotiations were Jewish, that many prominent communist leaders in Germany were Jewish, and that this observation—accurate as far as it went—became the basis for a conspiracy theory that would define the next era of German history.

The collapse of empires and the illusion of peace

Cooper begins by contrasting the official narrative of WWI's end with the reality on the ground. While the Allies celebrated victory, the defeated nations descended into civil wars, revolutions, and territorial conflicts that would rage for years and claim millions more lives. Bulgaria collapsed first, followed by Austria-Hungary's disintegration, and finally Germany itself erupted in revolution.

The German soldiers on the front lines did not initially understand that they had lost. The German army still held territory deep in enemy lands, and no enemy boots stood on German soil. The shock came gradually through rumors: Bulgaria had stopped fighting. Then Austria-Hungary was breaking apart. Then the Kaiser had fled. Then revolution had come to Germany itself.

The starvation blockade and betrayal

One of the episode's most powerful themes is the continuation of the Allied hunger blockade after the armistice. The armistice terms explicitly promised that the Allies would provision Germany, but instead the blockade remained in place to hold the German population hostage until they agreed to whatever peace terms were imposed.

Cooper emphasizes that many Germans, including patriots of the old empire, initially supported or acquiesced to the revolution because they believed it meant the blockade would be lifted and they could finally feed their children. Instead, the blockade remained, and hundreds of thousands of Germans and Austrians died of starvation after the war had ended.

This betrayal—the promise of food in exchange for laying down arms, followed by continued starvation—created a wound in the German psyche that would never fully heal. Cooper asks listeners to imagine what they would feel if their child died of starvation after being promised the war was over.

The Bolshevik terror and its impact on German consciousness

A substantial portion of the episode is devoted to documenting the Bolshevik terror in Russia between 1917 and 1919. Cooper details:

  • The deliberate encouragement of chaos and violence by Bolshevik leaders
  • Mass executions without trial (6,185 in just two months of 1918, compared to 6,321 by the Tsarist regime over 92 years)
  • The use of starvation as a weapon against peasants who refused to surrender their grain
  • Atrocities in places like Tashkent, where 14,000 people were massacred
  • The massacre of the Russian imperial family
  • The creation of concentration camps and the secret police (Cheka)

Cooper argues that this context is essential for understanding German reactions to Liebknecht and Luxemburg's calls for "world revolution." Germans could see exactly what that meant: not theoretical socialism, but the chaos, starvation, and mass murder they were witnessing in Russia. Refugees fleeing the Bolshevik terror were arriving in Germany with stories that seemed almost unbelievable in their brutality.

The German Revolution and the rise of the Freikorps

The German revolution began with sailors mutinying in Kiel and spread rapidly to every major city. The Kaiser fled, the old dynasties fell, and a new government under Friedrich Ebert and the Social Democrats attempted to establish order.

Cooper emphasizes the genuine fear among ordinary Germans that their country would follow Russia into chaos and terror. This fear was not irrational—it was based on observable reality. Liebknecht and Luxemburg were openly calling for the same kind of revolution that had brought terror to Russia, and they had the backing of Bolshevik money and professional revolutionaries.

The Freikorps emerged as a response to this fear. These were volunteer militias formed by returning soldiers, often elite storm troopers from the front lines. They were not initially ideological—they were men who had survived the trenches and now saw their country being torn apart by what they perceived as a communist takeover backed by foreign powers.

The Spartacist uprising and the killing of Liebknecht and Luxemburg

The episode culminates in the January 1919 Spartacist uprising in Berlin. Cooper provides a detailed narrative of the events:

  • The firing of Berlin's police chief, Emil Eichhorn, sparks massive demonstrations
  • 700,000 people take to the streets, with Liebknecht calling for armed insurrection
  • The communist leadership decides to attempt to overthrow the government
  • The Freikorps are unleashed and systematically crush the uprising
  • Liebknecht is captured, beaten, and executed in Tiergarten Park
  • Luxemburg is captured, beaten, shot in the head, and her body dumped in a canal

Cooper notes that the uprising itself was not particularly bloody—only 150-200 people were killed in Berlin—but the psychological impact was enormous. The Freikorps had proven they could defeat the communists, but in doing so, they had also demonstrated a capacity for violence and a willingness to execute prisoners that would characterize their actions in the months and years to come.

The formation of a radical generation

The episode ends by emphasizing how these events transformed an entire generation of German men. These were soldiers who had survived the trenches, who had seen their friends die for the fatherland, who had returned home expecting to be heroes, only to be spat upon and humiliated by mobs wearing red armbands.

They had been asked to defend a government they blamed for losing the war. They had seen their imperial flags burned and their sacrifices mocked. They had witnessed what they believed was a communist takeover backed by foreign (and, they noted, often Jewish) revolutionaries. And they had proven, through their actions in January 1919, that they had the power to stop it.

These men were not yet fascists, but they were radicalized. They had tasted violence, and they had found it effective. They would not lay down their arms and return to civilian life. Instead, they would turn east toward Germany's porous frontier, where they would continue to fight in wars of annihilation that would make the trenches look like a training exercise.

Historical context and attribution

Cooper emphasizes throughout that he is presenting this history from the perspective of Germans living through it, not offering an objective account. He notes that:

  • Hitler's anti-Semitism before the war was largely abstract and intellectual
  • The observations about Jewish prominence in communist movements were factually accurate, even if the conclusions drawn from them were not
  • The fear of Bolshevism among ordinary Germans was not paranoia—it was based on real events happening in Russia
  • The Freikorps violence, while brutal, was not initially ideological but rather the product of traumatized men seeking revenge

Cooper is careful to note that understanding how Germans perceived events is not the same as endorsing those perceptions or the actions that followed from them.

Themes and implications

The episode establishes several themes that will presumably continue throughout the series:

  1. Trauma and radicalization: The experience of the trenches, combined with the shock of revolution and the humiliation of defeat, created a generation of men capable of extraordinary violence.

  2. The power of perceived betrayal: The belief that Germany had been "stabbed in the back" by communists and Jews on the home front would become a central organizing principle of right-wing politics.

  3. The visibility of communism: Unlike in Germany, where communists remained a minority, in Russia the Bolsheviks had actually taken power and were implementing their vision. This made the threat seem real and imminent to Germans.

  4. The inadequacy of liberal democracy: The Social Democrats' attempt to maintain order through compromise and legality appeared weak compared to the Freikorps' willingness to use overwhelming force.

  5. The role of foreign intervention: The Bolsheviks' support for German communists, the presence of Russian POWs in Germany, and the continued Allied blockade all contributed to the sense that Germany was under siege from external enemies.
