A philosopher argues that humanity's survival depends on recovering individual integrity and common sense, not on scientific progress divorced from ethical foundations.

The secret of the 33 degree freemason

Opening: the broken teapot

Manly P. Hall opens with a Rudyard Kipling epigram about a neglected teapot that breaks—a metaphor for civilization's decay. The lecture's central claim: throughout history, a small minority (perhaps ten percent of humanity) administers the vast majority, with little direct contact to their actual needs. Yet the greatest resource for human survival is the human being itself, and this "tremendous common-sense majority" has almost no say in the causes and procedures of civilization.

The isolation of leadership

Hall argues that professional elites—scientists, politicians, intellectuals—have isolated themselves in "ivory towers of intellectual superiority," becoming incapable of the jobs they claim to manage. Despite unprecedented advances in science and education over the past fifty years, civilization is in worse condition than ever. The reason: leadership has lost basic contact with the essential humanity it is supposed to guide.

A new humanism is needed, one rooted in "the dignity, right power and authority of the folk"—the great mass of ordinary people. The average individual wishes to be a good parent, citizen, and child; to live in a happy environment; to avoid war and meaningless labor. Yet leaders and followers have become mutually estranged. The people no longer accept their leaders, and the leaders are out of touch with their followings.

The crisis of thought

Hall contends that modern education systematically prevents active, independent thinking. Children are taught to accept textbooks and arrive at approved answers; those who refuse are penalized and excluded. The result is an inferiority complex: the average person bows helplessly before the wisdom of the elect, afraid to express himself for fear of ridicule.

Yet within each individual lies a "mysterious faculty" that might be called common sense—the basic quality of mind given to all by a power greater than ourselves. The mind is an instrument to be used, not abused. Its proper use solves problems; its abuse tears them down. Modern society, however, is actively cultivating the mind into a robot, training it to serve false situations and prevailing policies, even when those policies lead nowhere.

Small groups and the monopoly of ambition

Hall identifies a partnership between military-minded intellectuals and the "psychosis of wealth." Small groups of ambitious persons play chess with human destiny, interested only in specialized warfare and abstract theories that benefit limited groups. They have convinced humanity that independent thought leads to poverty, while obedience to the elect brings wealth and fame—though such rewards ultimately destroy individual creativeness and authentic life.

Hall illustrates this with a personal anecdote: his uncle spent forty-five years in faithful corporate service, received a grandfather's clock, and died at eighty-three without having thought anything through for himself. He had no idea what kind of world he lived in. Meanwhile, the elite's wealth has multiplied (from sailboats to yachts, from modest to million-dollar incomes), yet nothing is being solved. The wealthy descend into sickness and death just as surely as the poor.

Rebellion and the refusal to conform

More and more, people are tired of being mistreated by those claiming superiority. Yet conformity is enforced: those who break the pattern face penalties not only from leaders but from indoctrinated family members and neighbors. Advocating world peace is branded as treason. The whole situation is out of hand—yet inside each person remains a power that is the only possible solution.

Hall argues that each child has an inalienable right to become a person, to think and use the faculties gained in previous embodiments. But the system forces individuals to have their individuality killed, to recognize that individuality is dangerous to material advantage. The comforting thought: the advantages people suffer to maintain are themselves failing, being wiped out by corresponding disadvantages that threaten the survival of the race.

Historical precedent: codes and discipline

Hall turns to history for lessons. Civilization's great foundations—codes of law, ethics, healing, art—did not come from privileged overclasses but from recognition of survival necessities. Hammurabi created Babylon's great code not by copying something earlier but by observing ordinary injustices: theft, broken promises, shoddy work. He established ethics to meet these injustices.

Lycurgus of Sparta found his people rich, powerful, and morally corrupt. He restored strict discipline, cutting crime to virtually nothing. Self-discipline and curtailment of luxuries became the basis of national strength. For five hundred years, Sparta remained well-ordered and low-crime. Today, Hall observes, we suffer from all the ailments Lycurgus cured.

The Mosaic Code and the teachings of Jesus—neither originating from academic credentials—established the moral foundations of the West. Moses was a wandering shepherd; Jesus never attended university. Yet their principles were greater and more enduring than two thousand years of accumulated intellectualism. Both were simple persons who discovered the power of the individual to be right and what that power could do in human destiny.

The failure of utopian schemes

Hall cites the People's Republic of China's "Great Leap Forward" as a cautionary tale. The regime decided to cut off all relations with the past, to live only for the future under dictatorial politicians. The result was tragic failure. Confucius—the sage of old—eventually returned as one of the most powerful forces in modern Chinese communist policy. The lesson: attempts to conquer or destroy in the name of political theory produce only distress.

The governing power within

Hall introduces a spiritual dimension: inside each person is a governing power that, if given a chance, will carry us through life with reasonable security. This is something we are born with. If previous karma does not make this obvious, then an incarnation in which we cannot control negative factors bears witness to unsolved problems of the past. A person with a difficult or unstable disposition must work harder—it is evidence of previous mistakes and compromised living across multiple embodiments.

Yet wherever a person faces a natural problem, he is also inwardly aware of the natural solution, though he may not want it. Proper childhood training in constructive thought can help. The new generation must become aware of its own self-individuation early, learning that the small despotisms of this planet have no permanent significance. Truth must survive and conquer all forms of untruth; it will ultimately become the basis of an enduring way of life.

The Ten Commandments and natural law

Hall argues that nature's rules cannot be broken successfully. The most important rules we can recognize are the Ten Commandments. No scientist has created something to replace them. One can ignore, deny, or write against them, yet they operate and the violator fails.

These commandments, along with the teachings of Christ, Buddha, Pythagoras, Plato, and other sages, are basic and available to everyone. None came from academic sources; all came from individuals internally enlightened and dedicated to serving their fellow humans in trouble. There is no evidence that great scientific achievements will ever take the place of the Ten Commandments or create a civilization that can endure without them.

Yet modern religion, pressured by science, has modified its own beliefs and gone lightly on the Commandments, allowing greater individual freedom and hoping for vicarious attainment—that individuals will be saved by membership rather than virtue. As long as this continues, Hall warns, there will be little progress.

The problem of living the commandments

Hall acknowledges a common objection: people say they believe in the Ten Commandments and the Sermon on the Mount but fear that living them will make them poor. Instead of worrying about being poor in spirit, they worry about being poor in worldly goods.

Yet a person unable to keep all commandments is not justified in breaking all of them. He has the right to improve what he can and to prove he is conscious of his society's needs. He can recognize the importance of quiet living and not intentionally break rules to gain unnecessary luxuries. Nature has tricks: the luxurious individual with excessive money and little thought destroys himself more quickly and effectively than one with less means. Money can become the basis of complete character degeneration. Wealth is not a reward for wisdom but usually for selfishness, and nature penalizes those who break the rules.

Family, honor, and the collapse of ethics

Hall emphasizes the commandment to honor father and mother—practically ceased in intellectual civilization. Yet when the family fails, the entire empire is ready to collapse, as Confucius pointed out. As more homes fail today, troubles accumulate: juvenile delinquency, crime, corruption. When ethics fails, evil moves in, and everything corrupted ethically will ultimately corrupt the society to which it belongs and fall in dismal failure.

Reincarnation and personal responsibility

Hall introduces reincarnation as a framework for understanding suffering and responsibility. It is becoming more reasonable to assume that the human being is a reincarnating creature, having lived before and made progress and mistakes, destined to live again to make more progress and correct mistakes.

The individual will never escape the weaknesses of his own nature except by correcting them. Death is not an escape; the inner life is a stream flowing from embodiment to embodiment. To the degree one unfolds and strengthens it now, one will have a better time in future lives. The achievements made in dedication and integrity will be with us forever. Because we have lived better and are better people, a better world will slowly emerge from this confusion—a world depending not on scientific manipulation but on the integrities of people coming into birth with firm resolution to get along with each other.

The problem of psychology without transformation

Hall critiques psychology's half-baked approach. An individual who dislikes others goes to an analyst, receives a definition of why (usually rooted in submerged incidents from previous life), and is told that finding the cause will help him correct it. This is optimism. Generally, the individual does nothing of the kind; he finds an excuse for his condition and works it for all its worth.

A person with problems must face them. He can use memory as a tool, remembering situations from early life that caused present difficulties. By performing an "alchemistical transmutation" of his own remembrances, cleaning the mysterious stables of his memories, he can greatly improve his present disposition. No matter what happened, he can get over it if he really wants to. He must ask himself: why did this have to happen to me in the divine plan? The substance is that the condition has been allowed to control life for sixty or seventy years, and the individual was here to get over the grievance. Only his own integrity will do it; science can clarify the problem but cannot solve it without the individual's willpower, common sense, and integrity.

Life as schooling, not vacation

Hall reframes life: it is not a vacation but a period of schooling, an educational process with opportunities to be happy but fundamentally aimed at learning and correcting our own mistakes. We must become individuals in a new humanistic society, each assuming responsibility for his own conduct until life is devoid of intemperances that cause trouble.

The alcoholic and drug addict have flunked an examination. Hall recounts a family ruined for a generation because one child wished to think for himself and was cast out by parents who demanded obedience to ancestral instruction. Such problems recur, causing obscuration at a time when the whole world is in trouble.

Learning from present conditions

Yet if we understand life, we can learn from current situations and gain new strength. We can observe what intolerance in religion does—millions murdering each other in the name of divine love. We can see how addiction to profit with no consideration for values or moralities moves us inevitably toward bankruptcy. We can understand that health problems are largely controlled from within; most ailments begin through corruption of natural law. A good disposition is invaluable to health; the nag and critic, constantly on the ragged edge of unhappiness, will suffer physically.

Courts are buried in cases representing ulterior motives; hospitals bulge with patients paying exorbitant fees for failure to use common sense. Industries are in trouble, competition destroys corporations, and great leaders do nothing. Meanwhile, scientists debate what is smaller than a nut on a nut's back, or dream of communicating with the Milky Way, while Earth is neglected. We are gradually failing to recognize the importance of sewerage—both literal waste disposal and mental-emotional sewerage. We build great skyscrapers and neutron machines while forgetting what to do with nuclear waste, polluting everything in sight.

The purpose of embodiment

Hall insists that the only successful solution is to leave this world a little wiser and a little better than when we came in. If this achievement is not there, the real purpose of embodiment has not been achieved. We must constantly try to leave the world better and leave behind a better world than the one we entered.

This is against political ambitions and the great power plays we live with. Why not take vast sums spent on weird projects and instead make life safe for humans on Earth? Why not use research to clean up our own dirt instead of wandering in space where we have nothing but trouble?

The rise of the people and the science of humanism

After periods of misapplied authority, the people rise to solve their own problems. Today, more and more are concerned with these issues and determined to act. The whole purpose of human existence is to make this world safe for humanity. The great science of humanism is the science of how it is done: how to ensure that poverty, crime, unemployment, corruption, and exploitation of natural resources are corrected.

What level of intelligence must we establish to save life from corruptions of selfishness, superstition, and fear? If science turns to explain the reason for humanity, the aims for which we were intended, the plan to which we belong, and provides a working schedule of self-improvement, cooperation, and integrity, gradually weeding out dishonesty, we will all have a much better chance to live.

The killing of potential genius

Hall laments that potential genius in the human being is mostly killed out. Individuals are not permitted to become great scholars, philosophers, or scientists as in ancient times, nor great artists. They can only think of painting something someone will buy, and because of low customer standards, art and music deteriorate. All these declines in values stem from great educational institutions that should perpetuate values but instead fail to give individuals a "trestle board of achievements and plans and programs and projects" by which each can live constructively in conformity with natural law.

Until these things are achieved, we will have trouble. But a new system is arising. Everywhere, people are becoming conscious that the first problem to solve is humanity itself. When humanity is safe and sound, we can speculate. Until then, attention directed elsewhere is dangerous. We must solve the human problem first by bringing it into harmony with natural law. When we departed from natural law, we got into trouble. When we return and find where mistakes were made, getting the ship of state back on the proper course of life, things will work out reasonably well.
