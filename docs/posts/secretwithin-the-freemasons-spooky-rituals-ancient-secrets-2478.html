The Freemasons: rituals, secrets, and contested origins

The entered apprentice initiation

The narrator describes the first-degree Masonic ritual in vivid detail. A blindfolded candidate, with a noose around his neck and his chest and left leg exposed, is led into the lodge. An inner guard holds a dagger to his bare chest. The candidate kneels before the worshipful master and places his right hand on a Bible while pressing a compass point against his chest. He swears a binding oath to conceal all secrets of Freemasonry, invoking terrible penalties: "my throat be cut across, my tongue be torn out by its root, and that I be buried in sand a cable's length from shore."

The candidate is then shown three sacred objects—the Bible, square, and compass—and taught the secret handgrip and word, "Boaz." He receives a Masonic badge and symbolic tools (gavel and ruler), then returns after changing clothes to receive final charges: he must respect God, obey the law, avoid political or religious discussion in the lodge, and never reveal the brotherhood's secrets.

A society with secrets, not a secret society

Many Freemasons object to being called a "secret society," preferring "a society with secrets." Yet the narrator argues that Freemasonry fits the definition perfectly: selective recruitment, promise of special knowledge or status, and oaths of loyalty and secrecy. The illusion of secrecy, the narrator suggests, is more important than the reality. Initiation details have long been publicly available to anyone who bothered to look—those who seek such knowledge without joining are called "Cowans."

Freemasonry has never been monolithic. There is no single authority; different jurisdictions admit women or men only, set varying ages for membership (18, 21, or 25), and follow different rituals. American lodges are split between two Grand Lodges, plus separate jurisdictions in South Carolina and Washington, D.C., all tracing their origin to the Grand Lodge of England.

Symbolism and the myth of light

Formally, Freemasonry is defined as "a system of morality veiled in allegory and illustrated by symbols." The initiation ritual is laden with symbolic meaning. The candidate enters from the west and travels toward the worshipful master in the east, representing the Masonic myth that wisdom and light come from the east. The blindfold symbolizes ignorance before receiving the light. The exposed breast proves the candidate is not a woman; the rolled-up pant leg shows he is able-bodied. The gavel, square, and apron are based on actual stonemason tools, yet no one in the lodge actually uses them for real work—they are purely symbolic.

Free and accepted: the operative-to-speculative shift

The terms "free" and "accepted" have specific meanings. "Free" originally meant a man who was not enslaved or bonded, and thus able to travel. Later it came to mean someone unencumbered by obligations conflicting with Masonic ones. An operative Mason is someone who actually practices the trade; an accepted (or speculative) Mason is admitted without any intention of laying bricks or stones—he is a symbolic Mason.

The widely accepted theory is that stonemason guilds began accepting non-working members, mostly wealthy aristocrats and tradesmen, who eventually became dominant and the real masons dropped out. However, the narrator notes, no one can really explain how, when, or why this happened. Why would rich men want to pretend to be common workmen? Why would guilds admit dilettantes?

Higher degrees and esoteric Freemasonry

No Mason is required to advance beyond the three craft (or blue) degrees: Entered Apprentice, Fellowcraft, and Master Mason. Higher degrees are bestowed by so-called Rites, which are mostly regarded as honorary or ornamental—like merit badges for an Eagle Scout. These higher degrees are where mystical and occult doctrines come into play.

Over time, there have been about 20 Masonic rites. The best known are the Scottish Rite (which goes up to 33 degrees) and the York Rite (a combination of three Masonic bodies). The Memphis and Misraim rites awarded a remarkable 99 degrees, including titles such as "Knight of the Rose Cross," "Knight Hermetic Philosopher," and "Pontiff of the Mystic City." Whether such degrees were taken seriously or were merely for show depended on the individual initiate.

Esoteric interpretations: Pike, Hall, and the inner brotherhood

The American esoteric scholar and 33rd degree Mason Manley P. Hall argued that Freemasonry is "a fraternity within a fraternity, an outer organization concealing an inner brotherhood of the elect." The visible society is described as a camaraderie of free and accepted men devoted to ethical, educational, fraternal, patriotic, and humanitarian concerns. The invisible society is a secret brotherhood dedicated to "the Secret of Secrets" (Arum Arcanorum).

The 19th-century American Masonic scholar Albert Pike pronounced Masonry a search for the light that led back to the occult mysteries of the ancient Jewish Kabbalah, which in turn connected Freemasonry to Hermeticism, alchemy, and anti-papal thinkers of the Middle Ages. Pike viewed Freemasonry as the successor or revival of ancient mystery cults and even regarded regular Blue Lodge Freemasonry as merely the entrance to the temple. Pike's esoteric ideas, however, were not intended for ordinary Masons; most brethren were probably unaware of them. His book Morals and Dogma was merely his learned opinion, not sacramental or obligatory.

The modern history: 1717 and the Grand Lodge of England

The history of modern Freemasonry begins in 1717 in London with the creation of the Grand Lodge of England, which grew out of four existing lodges. By 1800, more than 300 affiliated lodges existed in England alone. Separate Grand Lodges appeared in Ireland and Scotland, creating a franchise model.

In the 1730s, the Grand Lodge was hit by a schism between factions dubbed the Ancients and the Moderns. The Moderns, influenced by Enlightenment rationalism, wanted to abandon ritualism. The Ancients were determined to preserve it. The underlying question was whether Freemasonry was to be a social and philosophical order or a mystical religious one. This same schism affected American colonial lodges. The rift finally healed in 1813, when the Moderns yielded on rituals and the Ancients became more tolerant of politics.

In 1799, both factions were forced to work together to avoid being banned under the British Parliament's Unlawful Societies Act, which targeted secret societies suspected of spreading French revolutionary ideas. The Freemasons received an exemption so long as they maintained records of their members—a testament to the prestige and influence they had attained, particularly given that members of the royal family were initiates.

Contested origins: Euclid, Solomon, and the Templars

If Freemasonry went public in 1717, it certainly did not start then. It had been slowly evolving for at least 300 years. The 18th-century Grand Lodges grew out of older ones, supposedly meeting "from time immemorial." The 18th-century lodges based their rituals and rules on a collection of documents called the Old Charges, most dating to the 15th century. The oldest, the Regius or Hallowell Manuscript, may date to 1390.

These documents invoke multiple origin myths. One claims that the Greco-Egyptian mathematician Euclid "counterfeited geometry and thereby invented Freemasonry"—though how or why is left a mystery. The Cook Manuscript from 1450 mentions Euclid but also brings in King Solomon and traces things back to Adam. The Dawn Manuscript from the mid-1500s elaborates the connection to Solomon's temple and its legendary master mason Hiram Abiff. Hiram's death at the hands of three unworthy apprentices became a centerpiece in Masonic mythology.

The blank canvas of uncertain origins made it possible to paint in almost anything. A widely accepted theory links Freemasonry to the Knights Templar of the Crusades era, yet there is no documentation to back it up. The possible exception is the 15th-century Kirkwall Scroll from Scotland, an illustrated tapestry in which some claim to see Templar and Gnostic symbols—though it may be a later forgery.

Historian John Robinson argues for a genuine link in his 1989 book Born in Blood, focusing on the 1381 Peasants' Revolt in England. Robinson believes a shadowy great society behind the revolt was a conspiracy hatched by clandestine Templars, aimed at revenge against Church and Crown. Robinson's evidence is intriguing but ultimately thin. He does raise interesting points: medieval Mason guilds were really employer associations run by master masons who treated lesser brethren as common laborers and indentured servants. Robinson speculates that Crypto-Templars did not take over genuine guilds but formed their own pseudo-masonic guilds as a cover, which would explain their obsession with secrecy—something not typical among ordinary masons.

Interestingly, the Knights Templar had their own internal sect of Brother Masons. Since most Templar organizations survived suppression unscathed, presumably the Brother Masons did as well. There are other examples of political conspiracy disguised as labor association, such as the revolutionary Carbonari (charcoal burners) of early 19th-century Italy and France, many of whom were Freemasons who co-opted the name and terminology as a cover for revolutionary conspiracies.

British Freemasonry and imperialism

British Freemasonry became the secret society of the establishment in England. The 300 lodges of 1717 grew to 1,000 by the 1860s, 2,000 by the 1880s, and 3,000 by the start of the 20th century. Hundreds more sprouted throughout the empire. The poet laureate of British imperialism, Rudyard Kipling (a Mason), popularized the Brotherhood in his writing, notably in The Man Who Would Be King.

Yet the estimated 250,000 British Freemasons in 1900 were a small fraction of a population of almost 40 million, and downright insignificant in a larger imperial population of 400 million. Membership was heavily concentrated at the upper end of society among men who already enjoyed wealth and power.

For some, however, Freemasonry was not exclusive or serious enough. Arch-imperialist Cecil Rhodes joined a Masonic lodge in the 1870s because he saw "the wealth and power they possess, and the influence they hold." But Rhodes became disillusioned with what he called the Lodge's "most ridiculous and absurd rites" and thought the order was "without an object, and without an end." Rhodes' grand idea was to bring the whole uncivilized world under British rule, recover the United States, and make the Anglo-Saxon race one empire. Along with plotting the British conquest of South Africa's gold and diamonds, Rhodes formed his own secret organization, the Society of the Elect, selectively recruiting statesmen, financiers, and industrialists. Part of his scheme was the Rhodes Scholarship, designed to turn worthy Americans into right-thinking Englishmen. Freemasonry gave Rhodes his starting point, model, and inspiration.

Irregular and clandestine Freemasonry

Not all British Freemasons were inclined toward imperial loyalty. The Grand Lodge of England was always picky about who and what it regarded as legitimate. Masonic bodies like the Scottish Rite were branded irregular or clandestine. Irregular Freemasonry tended to attract dissidents, both moral and political.

A notable example is the occultist Alistair Crowley, once branded "the wickedest man in the world." The Grand Lodge denied any association with Crowley, even that he was a Freemason at all. But Crowley boasted a bevy of Masonic degrees from French, Mexican, and other lodges. The esoteric organizations Crowley was associated with—like the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn and the Ordo Templi Orientis (OTO)—all had Masonic roots. The OTO was initially envisioned as a Masonic academy that would unify Masonic rites with mystical orders to create a unified occult Freemasonry.

Another clandestine branch was co-masonry, which initiated men and women alike. The practice originally started in some 18th-century French lodges and resurfaced in 1893 with the founding of the Parisian lodge Le Droit Humain (Human Right). Co-masonry was later hijacked by English occultists connected to the mystical Theosophical Society, which sought to combine Western and Eastern mysticism into a universal occult doctrine and would inspire much of what we now call the New Age movement.

One prominent Theosophist was the Englishwoman Annie Besant, who was largely responsible for turning co-masonry into an appendage of Theosophy. Besant's politics were definitely radical: she was a socialist, a militant proponent of Indian nationalism, and later flirted with communism. Her Freemasonry was definitely not the same as Rudyard Kipling's.

French Freemasonry and revolution

While politics was officially forbidden within the Lodge, it was practically impossible to keep out. Masonic lodges were selective; a single dissenting vote could blackball a candidate. The frequent result was a lodge full of men sharing more or less common views, all sworn to secrecy. Connections formed inside the lodge could take on a different dimension outside of it. Simply put, Masonic lodges were like petri dishes for political conspiracy.

This was most obvious in French Freemasonry and the continental lodges it spawned and influenced. Interestingly, even French Freemasonry was of English origin. The first lodges were set up by British merchants in Dunkirk in 1721. In 1733, a Grand Lodge of France was formed, mostly English-speaking and an extension of the London Grand Lodge. Forty years later, however, the French Masons formed a new Grand Lodge, the Grand Orient de France, strongly influenced by the radical ideas of the Enlightenment.

While English Freemasonry became the mainstay of the social order, the Grand Orient became an incubator of revolutionary sentiments. It passed this radical flavor on to lodges in Italy, Germany, Turkey, and Russia. It is no coincidence that the motto of the Grand Orient—"Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity"—became one of the mottos of the French Revolution.

Nowhere was this radical streak stronger than in the Parisian Nine Sisters Lodge, formed in 1776, the same year as the Bavarian Illuminati and the beginning of the American Revolution. American revolutionary Benjamin Franklin was an initiate of the Nine Sisters, as was his friend and anti-religious French philosopher Voltaire, and many other men destined to play a part in France's coming upheaval.

The papacy's opposition and the Taxil hoax

Fears that Freemasonry nurtured and encouraged dangerous ideas, while exaggerated, were not baseless. No one looked on the Freemasons with greater displeasure than the Papacy. In 1738, Pope Clement XII issued the first edict against Freemasonry, saying it had caused "the greatest suspicion in the mind of the faithful" and that "all prudent and upright men had judged them depraved and perverted." He condemned and prohibited Freemasonry "now and forever." As Clement saw it, the Freemason obsession with secrecy condemned them, for "if they were not doing evil, he reasoned, they would not have so great a hatred of the light."

The Catholic Church also figured in an anti-Masonic brouhaha that infected France in the 1890s: the so-called Taxil hoax. A journalist, ex-Freemason, and sometimes pornographer named Joseph Joanny Boullan decided to exploit public anxiety over Freemasonry and Satanism by combining the two. Writing under the names Leo Taxil and Dr. Bataille, he churned out sensational books such as The Mysteries of Freemasonry and The Devil in the Nineteenth Century. His potboiler story was that Freemasonry was run by a satanic cult, the Palladians, based in Charleston, South Carolina, with American Freemason Albert Pike as the head devil worshipper. Taxil even conjured a Masonic high priestess, Diana Vaughn, who spun lurid tales of black masses and child sacrifice. She was supposedly the descendant of the 17th-century British alchemist and Rosicrucian Thomas Vaughan.

In 1897, Boullan shocked everyone, especially the Vatican, when he revealed that the whole thing was a hoax and Diana Vaughn a figment of his imagination. Nevertheless, that did not and has not stopped some from insisting the story was true.

Conclusion: many things, not one

The most important takeaway is that Freemasonry is not one thing but many, and it always has been. What was true in one's grandfather's lodge is not necessarily true for others. Much of this vagueness and variety stems from the fact that Freemason origins are lost in time. So what was not known was invented. As ever, people see what they want to see and believe what they want to believe.
