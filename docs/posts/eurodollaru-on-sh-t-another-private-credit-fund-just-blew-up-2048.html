As the credit cycle shifts from expansion to contraction, rising redemption gates, collapsing fundraising, and international insolvencies reveal deep cracks in the private credit market.

The illusion of private credit stability

Jeff Snyder of Eurodollar University argues that despite the lack of a sudden, dramatic collapse over the past year, the private credit cycle continues to deteriorate. While strong stock market performance and AI-driven indexes suggest economic resilience, the underlying credit market is experiencing a persistent retreat. The industry has not absorbed its losses; instead, it is slowly locking down liquidity to manage a massive backlog of redemption requests.

Blackstone's B-Cred and the BDC fundraising collapse

Blackstone’s flagship B-Cred fund, the largest non-traded Business Development Company (BDC) with approximately $77 billion in assets, has limited investor redemptions for the second consecutive quarter. Shareholders requested to withdraw roughly 10% of their shares, but the fund capped repurchases at the standard 5% limit.

Snyder highlights that this liquidity pressure is compounded by a severe drop in new capital: * Fundraising collapse: Fundraising for non-traded BDCs fell to $2 billion in the second quarter, down 82% from $11 billion a year prior—the lowest level since the 2020 pandemic lockdowns. * Withdrawal backlog: Across all tracked private credit funds, investors attempted to withdraw a record $23 billion in Q2, leaving an estimated $15 billion withdrawal backlog. * Flow reversal: During the boom, inflows funded redemptions and new loans. Now, with inflows dried up, managers must rely on borrower repayments, asset sales, and bank credit lines to meet liquidity demands.

Public credit warnings: distressed spreads at the bottom of the market

Snyder points out that the stress in private credit is mirrored in the public high-yield market. Triple C (CCC) corporate junk bond spreads have surged to 1,053 basis points, matching extreme levels seen during the August 2024 Japanese carry trade liquidation and the April 2025 tariff turmoil.

This high spread indicates that investors are demanding a 10.5% premium over government debt to hold highly leveraged, vulnerable corporate debt. While investment-grade and stronger high-yield spreads remain tight, the distress at the bottom of the credit market directly impacts the smaller, heavily leveraged companies that typically rely on private credit.

The Australian real estate crisis: the collapse of Bathla group

The private credit downturn has spread internationally, highlighted by the insolvency of Sydney-based home builder Bathla Group. Bathla accumulated approximately $3.3 billion AUD in debt from more than 40 private credit funds, with some loans reportedly promising returns near 15%.

As Australian developers face rising construction costs, higher interest rates, and falling property prices, Bathla's collapse has rattled the local market. Because up to 60% of Australia's $200 billion AUD private credit market is tied to real estate (compared to just 15–20% in North America), several Australian private credit funds have been forced to cap investor withdrawals.

The trade finance cockroach: Radiant World and collateral fraud

Snyder highlights a new "cockroach" emerging in trade finance: Radiant World, a major iron ore trader that once handled 5% of global trade. Lenders and counterparties are investigating whether the invoices and receivables used as collateral for financing represented genuine transactions.

  • Historical warnings ignored: Rabobank investigators reportedly concluded as early as 2020 that certain trades involved fraud, yet Radiant continued to expand due to loose underwriting standards during the credit boom.
  • Legal and financial fallout: A fund linked to Jefferies has filed court applications in London against Radiant World and Sapphire Minmetals. Meanwhile, commodity giant Glencore has recorded a $480 million provision to cover its exposure to the entities.

Conclusion: a slow-moving shift from expansion to contraction

Snyder concludes that the absence of a sudden 2008-style crash does not mean the private credit market is safe. Private credit structures are specifically designed to delay and absorb stress slowly through valuation adjustments, payment-in-kind (PIK) interest, maturity extensions, and redemption gates. However, the broader trend is clear: capital is retreating, portfolios are contracting, and the credit cycle has officially shifted from expansion to cleanup.
