LegaliBus | Isn't "fairness" just a mood? The maxims of equity, explained

How historic courts of chancery moved past subjective rulings by establishing the maxims of equity to govern trust and fairness.

Historically, critics mocked courts of equity for delivering subjective justice that varied based on the whims of individual chancellors. To establish legitimacy, equity developed a structured set of guiding principles known as maxims. These short, memorable rules transformed fairness from an unpredictable mood into a rigorous, arguable legal framework.

Key takeaways

  • The "chancellor's foot" problem: LegaliBus notes that early critics joked that equity decisions varied as much as the physical size of each chancellor's foot, making the law feel like a subjective mood rather than a predictable system.
  • The introduction of maxims: To counter this, equity established guiding maxims to provide a standardized, repeatable framework for arguing fairness.
  • Opening and closing the doors of equity: Some maxims grant remedies where strict common law fails (e.g., "equity will not suffer a wrong to be without a remedy"), while others restrict access to those who act in bad faith or delay action (e.g., the "clean hands" and "vigilant" maxims).
  • The foundation of trusts: LegaliBus highlights the maxim "equity regards the beneficiary as the true owner" as the core concept behind modern trust law, separating legal paperwork from true beneficial ownership.
