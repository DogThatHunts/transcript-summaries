Doomberg and James Lavish examine the intersection of energy markets, sovereign debt, and geopolitical risk in a world where central banks have lost control of long-term yields. They argue that all roads lead to printing—and that Bitcoin, gold, and energy assets are pricing in inevitable monetary debasement. Europe faces a perfect storm: diesel shortages, Russian refinery attacks, potential US export restrictions, and the collapse of its energy infrastructure after decades of poor policy choices.

Five key insights

  1. The debt regime has broken. Long-term Treasury yields have broken a 40-year downtrend. Buyers no longer believe 5% protects purchasing power over 30 years, forcing governments to eventually intervene and print—a structural shift that favors hard assets.

  2. Private credit is a hidden time bomb. Private equity firms have bought insurance companies and stuffed them with their own junk bonds. When forced asset sales occur (e.g., mass insurance redemptions), mark-to-market losses could cascade across financial markets, echoing 2008.

  3. Europe's energy vulnerability is acute. The EU consumes 11 million barrels of oil per year but produces only 300,000. Diesel prices at record highs, Russian refineries under attack, and potential US export curbs create a "four horsemen" scenario: Trump limits exports, sanctions hit India's refining capacity, Middle East escalates, and overheated global refineries fail.

  4. Markets have different purposes and structures. Oil markets exist to ensure steady refinery supply at profitable prices. Stock markets assemble risk capital. Bond markets price duration risk. Conflating them leads to false conclusions about "manipulation"—the 10-year yield is one of the world's most sophisticated, liquid markets and deserves respect.

  5. Energy is the foundation of all value. Currencies exist to shuttle energy between producers and consumers. When treasuries yield 5% but energy scarcity looms, rational actors sell bonds to buy oil. This explains why Japan, China, and Russia have been dumping US debt—they're choosing BTUs over interest coupons.

The structural debt problem

Lavish argues that the US faces an unsustainable fiscal position: $40 trillion in debt, turning over 25–33% annually, while adding another 5% yearly. At 5% yields, debt service becomes punishing. Congress structurally cannot cut spending (Social Security and Medicare dominate the budget), and off-balance-sheet liabilities exceed $100 trillion. This forces the Treasury to eventually step in and buy bonds—i.e., print.

Doomberg adds that fiscal dominance has arrived. Old yield-based models no longer apply when debt-to-GDP exceeds 100% and deficit financing is 6–7% of GDP. In an era of energy scarcity, rational actors sell treasuries to buy energy. The reserve currency's entire purpose is to be held "in reserve when you need it"—but if you're energy-short, today's BTUs beat tomorrow's coupon.

Private credit and contagion risk

Doomberg flags a dangerous dynamic: private equity owns insurance companies, which buy PE firms' own debt at inflated valuations. Mark-to-market accounting allows these firms to set prices via "chip-shot marking"—I buy your deal at a new valuation, you buy mine, and both books appreciate. This works until forced selling occurs.

The trigger could be mass insurance redemptions (boomers cashing whole-life policies) or natural disasters. When insurance companies must sell private loans on the open market to raise cash, mark-to-market losses cascade. Lavish notes that in 2008, the concept of valuation—present value of expected future payments corrected for default probability—was made on Excel spreadsheets. Banks today face similar mark-to-market losses on treasuries (Porter Stansberry's Bank of America thesis), but the Fed allows them to hold losses off-balance-sheet.

Neither speaker predicts imminent collapse, but both flag this as a black swan risk that could trigger correlation-to-one-event selling across all asset classes.

The four horsemen of Europe's diesel crisis

Doomberg's forthcoming piece identifies four synergistic threats to European diesel supply:

  1. Trump export restrictions. Political pressure may force limits on US diesel exports, crushing global prices and starving Europe.

  2. Sanctions on India. The Lindsey Graham sanctions bill forces Trump to sanction China and India—major crude refiners and diesel exporters. India supplied 60% of Red Sea diesel to Europe in August. Loss of Indian exports devastates Europe.

  3. Middle East escalation. Houthi attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure and potential broader conflict could destroy refining capacity and choke supply.

  4. Refinery failures under stress. Global refineries run at 97–108% of nameplate capacity. Maintenance is deferred; one breakdown (like the Joliet refinery in the Midwest last week) cascades into regional shortages and increases Trump's incentive to restrict exports.

These four threads are synergistic: each increases the likelihood of the others. Higher diesel prices embolden Houthis; India questions whether to stockpile rather than export; overheated refineries fail faster.

Europe's predicament is acute. The 27 EU members consume 11 million barrels annually but produce only 300,000. They are "wildly susceptible to the whims of the rest of the world" yet fund and cheer the destruction of Russian refineries—without a democratic mandate. Lavish notes that Germany's Friedrich Merz polls below 5% yet claims a mandate to lead Germany back into war with Russia (for the third time in a century).

Energy as the basis of all value

Doomberg articulates a foundational principle: energy is life. Currencies exist to efficiently shuttle energy between people who can afford to buy it and people who can produce it.

After Nixon closed the gold window in 1971, the US struck a "grand bargain" with Saudi Arabia: the dollar would be "as good as gold for oil." The US would maintain fiscal discipline, and oil exporters could recycle excess profits into dollar-denominated assets, assured of purchasing power preservation. This relationship broke down in the 2008 financial crisis. China opened its gold window; Russia sold treasuries; geopolitical conflict intensified.

Today, the purpose of all markets—energy, credit, equity—is to ensure energy reaches where it needs to go. When yields don't compensate for energy scarcity, rational actors sell bonds to buy oil. This explains why Bitcoin, gold, and energy are rising together: they are all pricing in the certainty of monetary debasement and energy competition.

Bitcoin and the "all roads lead to printing" thesis

Lavish notes that Bitcoin's rise to $86,000+ is not a "flight to safety" (equities and bonds are also rising). Instead, it reflects recognition that all roads lead to printing. Nobody will technically default; governments will debase instead. Bitcoin is the asset that prices this certainty.

Doomberg cautions against timing the market but advocates for disciplined, rules-based allocation. He dollar-cost-averages gold monthly, holds all retirement accounts in gold (given his long time horizon), and treats dips as opportunities. The key is emotional discipline: if you believe Bitcoin reaches $1 million, dips are buys and rallies are opportunities to trade around your core position.

Lavish adds that perpetual futures (leveraged short bets) have created "fake liquidity" at certain price points (e.g., $81–82k). Once Bitcoin breaks through, an air pocket forms and it rallies to the next resistance level. This explains recent $5,000 overnight moves—not unusual for an asset that trades 24/7 with thin liquidity at certain hours.

Global yields and the absence of flight-to-safety

The only exceptions are China (yields down, backed by gold reserves) and Argentina (yields down after a Bessent bailout). This suggests that investors no longer view US debt as a safe haven. Instead, they're pricing in energy scarcity and monetary debasement.

Doomberg's final advice: have a set of rules you can emotionally follow without breaking during panic.
