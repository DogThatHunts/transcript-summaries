Real Estate Insider “Banks Are Now Dumping Buildings At 80 Cents On The Dollar!”

Real estate investor Ken McElroy details how surging costs, high interest rates, and localized oversupply are forcing lenders to liquidate commercial real estate at heavy discounts.

Real estate investor Ken McElroy joins host Jeff to discuss the structural pressures reshaping commercial real estate, explaining why banks and lenders are offloading distressed properties at 60 to 80 cents on the dollar. McElroy attributes the market distress to a combination of softening rents driven by recent oversupply, rapidly inflating operating expenses like insurance and property taxes, and rising debt service costs on floating-rate loans or maturing fixed-rate mortgages. While owners with long-term fixed debt are staying afloat, borrowers with immediate maturities are being forced to sell or return assets to lenders. This cycle is creating significant distress and deferred maintenance, but it also presents high-yield opportunities for experienced investors with dry powder who are prepared to acquire, renovate, and hold properties until supply contracts and rent growth resumes around 2027 or 2028.

Key insights

  • Commercial real estate is squeezed by three distinct pressures: Landlords face stagnant or falling rents due to heavy supply, sharp increases in operating expenses (utilities, taxes, insurance), and higher mortgage payments as floating or maturing debt resets.
  • Distressed debt is forcing severe lender haircuts: Lenders are increasingly taking over failed assets and quietly selling them off at massive discounts—sometimes below land replacement cost—because former equity has been entirely wiped out.
  • Deferred maintenance is rapidly accumulating: Properties burdened by high debt service are running out of cash flow, forcing landlords to skip essential maintenance and causing physical property values to deteriorate quickly.
  • Supply freeze creates an entry point for a 2027–2028 recovery: A 50-year high in apartment deliveries in 2024–2025 has frozen new project starts, setting up a sharp drop in future supply and strong rent growth by late 2027 or 2028.
  • Geographic location dictates severe market bifurcation: Heavily built-out submarkets like Austin, Nashville, and downtown Phoenix face severe concession pressure, whereas supply-constrained markets like Tempe and parts of the Midwest remain stable.

The triple threat squeezing commercial property owners

Ken McElroy explains that commercial real estate distress is not isolated to single one-off deals, but reflects a broader market repricing driven by three compounding factors: - Rent softness and concessions: A massive wave of new building triggered by past low interest rates created an excess supply of apartment units, forcing landlords in many markets to offer concessions (such as two to four months of free rent). - Rising operating costs: Utilities, property taxes, and especially property insurance have escalated dramatically, shrinking Net Operating Income (NOI). - Loan maturity resets: Floating-rate borrowers are facing immediate defaults, while fixed-rate borrowers facing near-term maturities will see interest rates reset higher (e.g., resetting from 4% to over 5.5%), increasing monthly payments even if property income remains unchanged.

Distressed assets, debt haircuts, and deferred maintenance

Property owners holding floating-rate debt or facing imminent loan maturities are often forced to list assets or forfeit them to lenders. McElroy notes that when an asset's Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) falls well below 1.0 (e.g., to 0.6 or 0.7), the asset is functionally trading at 60 to 70 cents on the dollar.

When cash flows fail to cover debt payments, owners sacrifice routine maintenance (painting, roofing, landscaping), leading to widespread deferred maintenance and visible physical deterioration. Lenders taking back these non-performing assets are taking substantial losses. McElroy cites making an offer on a 100% vacant lender-owned asset in Texas for $8,000 per unit—below land replacement cost—where the previous buyer had paid $90,000 per unit four years earlier.

Macroeconomic headwinds, demand destruction, and renter dynamics

Host Jeff points out that recent Consumer Price Index (CPI) data suggests broader economic weakness rather than persistent inflation. As energy costs and general expenses rise, consumer demand pulls back, placing further pressure on household budgets and job stability.

This economic environment makes homeownership increasingly unaffordable for many households. McElroy notes that rising home maintenance costs, property taxes, and elevated interest rates are driving higher homeownership costs regardless of base mortgage rates. As a result, the median first-time homebuyer age has pushed to 40, keeping a structural wave of households in the rental pool longer and sustaining demand for multi-family units.

Market timeline and the 2027–2028 recovery window

Describing the cycle in baseball terms, McElroy believes the commercial real estate market is currently in the first few innings of a long turnaround: - Innings 1–3 (Present): Capital positioning and acquiring distressed or lender-owned assets at low cost bases. - Innings 4–6 (2025–2027): Operational stabilization, completing renovations, and maintaining tenant quality amid peak supply absorption. - Innings 7–9 (2027–2029): Value creation phase. High interest rates have halted new ground-up construction starts in 2024 and 2025. As current inventory gets absorbed, a severe supply deficit will trigger renewed rent growth and asset price appreciation by 2027–2028.

Regional divergence and strategic market selection

McElroy emphasizes that recovery will vary significantly by location: - Overbuilt markets: Downtown Phoenix, Austin, and Nashville are experiencing heavy rent concessions and price drops due to rapid recent building booms. - Resilient submarkets: Areas with natural geographic or zoning constraints, such as Tempe, Arizona, or stable Midwestern metros, maintain high occupancy and steady demand. - Urban core challenges: Governance, political environment, and safety issues continue to hamper full recovery in downtown centers like Seattle, Portland, and San Francisco.
