Ep 200: Freedom With Equity with Andrew William Macdonald

Andrew William Macdonald discusses using equity jurisprudence to settle debts, navigate legal disputes, and shift the burden of proof without contentious courtroom battles.

In this conversation, host Alec sits down with educator Andrew William Macdonald to explore equity jurisprudence as an alternative to combative legal strategies often seen in sovereign citizen or "patriot" movements. Macdonald shares how decades of confrontational tactics against government agencies landed him in serious legal trouble—including massive tax liens and criminal charges—before he pivoted to studying classical equity. Together, they examine the nature of modern fiat banking, the legal mechanics of contracts and debt discharge, and how asking precise questions in an honorable manner can compel institutional performance and achieve resolution without warfare.

Key insights

  • Inquiry shifts the burden of proof: Instead of filing contentious motions or asserting disputed rights, equity relies on asking structured questions that place the burden of proof entirely on the opposing party.
  • Modern money operates on discharge, not payment: Macdonald contends that since the removal of the gold standard, circulating currencies are debt instruments, meaning obligations can only be discharged dollar-for-dollar rather than paid in lawful money.
  • Contracts supersede statutory defaults: Consenting to agreements, whether through direct signatures or silent acquiescence, creates binding obligations that equity can only undo through specific remedies like equitable rescission or settlement.
  • Equity views the individual as equal to the court: Unlike standard commercial and administrative procedures where an individual accepts a subordinate role, invoking equity allows a person to stand on equal standing with a judge or administrator.
  • Honor replaces confrontation: Macdonald argues that "patriot warfare" fails because it generates administrative dishonor, whereas equity focuses on giving commercial energy back to the system to balance accounting records peacefully.

Macdonald's transition from patriot warfare to equity

Macdonald recounts his early experiences in the 1980s and 1990s as a firefighter paramedic, which eventually led him into alternative legal movements after observing regulatory crackdowns on alternative medicine. For over two decades, he pursued conventional "common law" and secured party creditor approaches—tactics he characterizes as "patriot warfare mode." This approach culminated in severe conflict: high six-figure IRS liens and, following an attempt to acquire abandoned property through administrative processes, felony charges carrying potential prison time.

After discovering equity jurisprudence, Macdonald altered his approach entirely. Rather than arguing statutory validity or claiming he was exempt from taxation, he engaged with the IRS and court systems through equitable principles of honor and administrative settlement. He reports that this change enabled him to secure certificates of release on seven figures worth of tax liens and led a state judge to dismiss his felony criminal case on the record while discharging all associated bonds.

The mechanics of equity, contracts, and currency

According to Macdonald, equity functions as a legal bridge connecting natural or divine law to the commercial realm of admiralty and maritime law. Historically administered through chancery courts, equity prioritizes fair dealing and substance over strict procedural technicalities. Central to Macdonald's worldview are equitable maxims—chief among them being that "equity will not aid a volunteer" and that obligations must be dissolved in the same manner they were formed.

Macdonald and Alec discuss the premise that modern transactions operate entirely within commerce and negotiable instruments. Macdonald asserts that Federal Reserve notes and equivalent international fiat currencies are debt obligations rather than lawful money under traditional definitions. Under this model, consumer debt, mortgages, and bank loans do not represent banks lending their own capital; rather, banks leverage the borrower's signature to create credit that is subsequently securitized and tracked using Committee on Uniform Security Identification Procedures (CUSIP) numbers.

Navigating claims, presentments, and institutional disputes

Macdonald explains that institutional communications generally arrive in one of two categories: an offer (a non-threatening communication or reminder) or a presentment (a demand, citation, or formal charge). In equity, remaining silent constitutes acquiescence, which legally operates as tacit consent. Therefore, every communication requires a response, but that response should avoid debate, counter-claims, or conditional acceptances that make compliance contingent on difficult-to-prove accusations of fraud.

Instead, Macdonald advocates for accepting and returning presentments for settlement and closure, coupled with formal notice. If an individual must appear before an administrative body or court, the strategy involves a special appearance to reserve rights and posing targeted questions to the bench. By inquiring about the accounting, the presence of court bonds, or the administrator's financial interest, the individual avoids entering testimony or acknowledging fictional status while compelling the official to resolve the underlying matter.

Real-world applications and limitations

While describing how students have used these methods to address tax notices, SBA loans, credit card balances, traffic citations, and even custody disputes, Macdonald stresses that equity is not a magical "silver bullet" or a way to shirk legitimate ethical duties. Discharging an account involves permanently settling and closing it, rather than continually racking up new debt in dishonor.

Macdonald cautions against recklessly risking essential property, advising that foreclosures and asset seizures are complex matters best avoided whenever conventional settlement or short sales remain viable. He emphasizes that navigating institutions requires personal responsibility, precise study of commercial dictionaries, and emotional composure, rather than adopting scripts found indiscriminately on the internet.
