The illusion of a resilient labor market

The household survey and the reality of labor force dropouts

While the Federal Reserve points to a falling official unemployment rate as proof of economic strength, EurodollarU asserts that this decline is an accounting artifact. The official unemployment rate is falling only because a massive number of Americans are dropping out of the labor force entirely.

According to the household survey, employment has fallen by over 900,000 since January, while the labor force itself has contracted by a staggering 1.4 million. When adjusting the unemployment rate to hold the labor force participation rate steady, EurodollarU calculates that the true number of unemployed Americans is closer to 11 million, compared to the officially reported figure of under 7 million.

This contraction in the labor force closely mirrors private-sector real income data (excluding government transfer payments), which has been rolling over since the previous autumn. EurodollarU suggests this alignment points to a fragile, deteriorating economy that may have entered a traditional-style recessionary phase as early as late last year.

The Federal reserve's predictable "Pringles can" cycle

EurodollarU explains that the global economy has been characterized by "mini cycles" of ups and downs since 2008. Because mainstream economists and Fed officials only extrapolate the upside of these cycles, they repeatedly fall into a predictable policy trap: 1. Early Summer Hawkishness: Reassuring initial payroll data and temporary economic bumps (such as energy or tariff shocks) convince the Fed that the economy is solid and inflation is the primary risk. 2. The Feedback Loop: Hawkish Fed commentary validates stock market optimism, boosting equity prices and reinforcing the Fed's circular reasoning. 3. Late Summer Sour Note: By August and September, weak complementary data and downward payroll revisions expose the underlying economic fragility. 4. The Autumn Pivot: Shocked by "unexpected" weakness, the Fed quickly abandons its hawkish stance and retreats to rate cuts—a cycle EurodollarU colorfully refers to as returning to the "Pringles can."

This exact pattern played out in 2024, repeated in 2025, and is currently repeating in 2026, demonstrating that policymakers are consistently operating with the wrong economic frame of reference.

What complementary data like JOLTS reveals

To understand the true state of the labor market, EurodollarU urges observers to look beyond headline payrolls to complementary macroeconomic indicators, specifically the Labor Turnover portion of the Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS).

While the mainstream media focuses almost exclusively on job openings, the actual labor turnover data for June showed net turnover was flat at zero, following a negative net turnover in May. This flatlining turnover directly contradicts the positive job growth initially reported in the establishment surveys.

Furthermore, EurodollarU challenges the mainstream narrative that declining labor participation is driven by benign, non-economic factors like aging demographics or immigration enforcement. Historically, whenever the hiring rate falls below roughly 2%, the participation rate drops in tandem. EurodollarU argues that American workers drop out of the labor force simply because they recognize that businesses are not hiring, making the collapsing participation rate a highly reliable macroeconomic distress signal.
