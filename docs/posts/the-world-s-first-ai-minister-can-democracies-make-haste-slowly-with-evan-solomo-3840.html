The world's first AI minister: Can democracies make haste slowly?

Overview

David McWilliams interviews Evan Solomon, Canada's newly appointed Minister of Artificial Intelligence, in a wide-ranging conversation about AI regulation, sovereignty, and democratic governance. Solomon outlines Canada's "AI for All" strategy—built on trust, opportunity, and sovereign control—while addressing public anxieties about job displacement, uncontrolled AI systems, and geopolitical power shifts. The discussion moves beyond apocalyptic scenarios to explore how middle-power democracies can build AI infrastructure domestically, maintain democratic values, and establish international regulatory norms without surrendering to either tech monopolies or authoritarian control.

Five key insights

  1. AI is a tool, not destiny. Solomon emphasizes that artificial intelligence must serve people, not the reverse. The choice between building AI "right" or "wrong" remains open; there is no predetermined outcome, only the urgent need for democracies to shape development through education, regulation, and values-driven policy.

  2. Canada's unique position stems from foundational research. Geoffrey Hinton, Joshua Bengio, and Rich Sutton—three Turing Prize winners—pioneered modern AI in Canada, yet the dominant AI companies (OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic) are based in the US because of capital and compute access. Canada now aims to retain talent and harvest innovation at home rather than exporting it.

  3. Regulation must keep pace without crushing innovation. Solomon proposes four regulatory buckets: transparent model testing, capability evaluation, timed release schedules, and incident reporting mechanisms. He cites the EU AI Act and Canada's domestic rules as models, while noting the US is gradually moving from voluntary accords toward more formal oversight.

  4. The time mismatch between democracy and technology is real but not unprecedented. Drawing on the Aldine printing press's motto "Fasima Lente" (make haste slowly), Solomon argues democracies must move faster than before while preserving deliberation. The challenge is not to stop or blindly accelerate, but to build with ancestral wisdom and community input.

  5. AI is now a theater of geopolitical realignment. Mark Carney's coalition of middle powers—Canada, Germany, France, Ireland, Japan, South Korea—seeks to establish rules-based AI governance independent of US or Chinese dominance. This reflects a broader shift: democracies can no longer rely on US protection and must build sovereign capacity to ensure their values shape the technology.

What is AI? definitions and types

Solomon begins by defining AI as "a tool to serve people," not the reverse. He distinguishes three main types:

  • Generative AI: Algorithms trained on large datasets of language that predict and generate the next sentence (e.g., ChatGPT). These are called large language models.
  • Agentic AI: Systems that not only predict but autonomously perform tasks on behalf of users, like a butler executing commands.
  • Recursive self-improvement (RSI): The frontier concern—AI systems that teach themselves and other AI systems, potentially accelerating beyond human oversight.

Solomon notes that AI is already working beneficially in hospitals, manufacturing, and diagnostics. The public anxiety centers on the frontier: systems so intelligent they escape control, become misaligned (doing something other than intended), or break out of safety "sandboxes."

Why Canada appointed a minister for AI

Canada has legitimate claim to inventing modern AI through three "godfathers": Geoffrey Hinton (neural networks), Joshua Bengio (deep learning), and Rich Sutton (machine learning). All won the Turing Prize and worked in Canadian labs (Vector in Toronto, Mila in Montreal). Yet the dominant AI companies are US-based because they had access to capital and compute—the two resources that attract talent.

Solomon frames the appointment as a response to geopolitical realignment. As Mark Carney has argued, the world has changed: supply-chain integration with the US is no longer an unambiguous strength. Canada must now retain its scientists and entrepreneurs by providing domestic capital and compute access. Otherwise, it will "plant the seed, water the seed, grow the plant, and then someone comes along and harvests it."

This is not isolationism but sovereignty as partnership: keeping critical elements at home while collaborating internationally. The three guiding questions are: If we don't build domestically, we'll have to buy it. If we don't innovate, we'll have to rent it. If we don't make the rules, we'll have to follow someone else's.

Addressing the apocalyptic scenario

McWilliams raises the fear that AI will displace workers and concentrate wealth among AI owners, exacerbating inequality and triggering political backlash. Solomon acknowledges this as a real scenario but rejects both passivity and determinism.

Two traps to avoid: - Passivity: "It's so big, there's nothing I can do." - Determinism: Either we must stop AI entirely (impossible) or we must chase utopia (naive).

The real question is build right or build wrong. Solomon offers concrete examples of building right: - Data centers designed with communities, using closed-loop water systems and recycled heat. - Pro-worker AI policies: subsidizing compute access for small companies (50% cost, 80% if domestic), funding 90,000 job placements, and launching free AI literacy courses. - Legislation banning non-consensual deepfake imagery and political deepfakes during elections.

Solomon notes that past technologies (vacuum cleaners, cars) did not eliminate work but redistributed it. The key is ensuring the benefits are shared democratically, not hoarded by the few.

Safety, regulation, and the role of government

Solomon identifies three levels of AI risk: 1. Loss of control: Building something that can no longer be controlled. 2. Misalignment: The AI does something other than intended. 3. Sandbox escape: Systems break out of testing environments (e.g., the Hugging Face incident with OpenAI).

He argues that governments cannot abdicate regulatory responsibility to the companies that built AI. This is a democratic failure. Instead, regulation must proceed on four fronts:

Technical approaches: - Joshua Bengio's "Law Zero" project aims to redesign AI systems to be "safe by design," avoiding alignment issues. Canada and Germany have each funded it with $150 million. - Drawing an analogy to car safety: Ralph Nader's Unsafe at Any Speed led to seatbelts and airbags. Similarly, AI can be engineered for safety from the start.

Regulatory approaches: - Transparent model testing and capability evaluation. - Timed release schedules (don't rush models to market). - Incident reporting mechanisms. - The EU AI Act is already in force; Canada has domestic rules; the US is moving from voluntary accords to more formal oversight. - Mark Carney proposes a Technology Stability Board (modeled on the Financial Stability Board) to create international norms and coordinate regulation across countries.

Solomon stresses that regulation must not crush innovation. Existing approval systems for cars and medicines already work; AI need not be different. But frontier concerns—especially AI in weapons systems—demand vigilance.

The time mismatch: democracy versus technological acceleration

McWilliams raises a critical concern: democracies operate through deliberation and process, which take time. AI develops at near-light speed. How can regulation keep up?

Solomon acknowledges this is not new but is more urgent. He invokes the Aldine printing press in Renaissance Venice, whose colophon read "Fasima Lente"—make haste slowly. This is his governing principle.

Three daily questions guide his work: 1. If we don't build infrastructure domestically, someone else will, and we'll have to buy it. 2. If we don't innovate, we'll have to rent it from others. 3. If we don't make the rules, we'll have to follow someone else's.

The answer is to build, innovate, and legislate at scale, but in partnership with other middle powers (Ireland, Germany, France, Japan, South Korea). This coalition approach allows democracies to move faster than any single nation while preserving deliberation and values.

Sovereignty, ancestral intelligence, and the good ancestor

Solomon recounts hosting the G7 in Montreal, where an Indigenous elder, Kevin Deere, told him: "You're interested in artificial intelligence, and I'm interested in ancestral intelligence. You better make sure that when you build your artificial intelligence, you build it with ancestral intelligence."

This moment crystallizes Solomon's broader point: AI development must be grounded in the wisdom of communities—Indigenous peoples, immigrants, workers, democracies—not just in the logic of algorithms. Respecting ancestral intelligence reduces the likelihood of the "bad scenario" and increases the chance of building something that serves all.

McWilliams closes by referencing The Good Ancestor by Roman Krasnag: our job is to leave the place better than we found it. The central moral question is whether AI will allow us to be good ancestors or bad ones.

The geopolitical realignment

David McWilliams adds crucial context in the outro: Canada's appointment of an AI minister, and Carney's coalition-building, reflect a deeper shift in global politics. The US under Trump is no longer a reliable partner for middle powers. Consequently, talented people like Solomon are leaving the private sector to join a sovereign, national project—reminiscent of the Irish independence movement and the Gaelic League of the 1920s.

Without Trump's antagonism, Canadian politics would be "more of the same." With it, Canada is building an exciting, independent movement for sovereignty and leadership. The key is strong leadership—and, McWilliams notes with a smile, preferably with Irish grandparents.
