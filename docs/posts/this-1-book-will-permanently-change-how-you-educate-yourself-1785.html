Overview

Drawing on Jean Dubuffet's Asphyxiating Culture, this essay explores a central paradox: universities can teach radical ideas about learning but cannot fully embody them without undermining their own institutional authority. The speaker, a peer-reviewed scholar and educator with over 13 years of experience, proposes that the knowledge appearing most useless may actually be most important—and outlines four principles to help you educate yourself in ways most people never will.

Principle 1: distinguish three types of useful knowledge

The speaker first encountered Dubuffet's work through his sister's undergraduate thesis but initially dismissed it as impractical. Over time, he realized the word "useful" had quietly colonized his entire intellectual life. He identifies three distinct categories of knowledge that are usually conflated:

  • Economically useful knowledge: skills that help you perform a job, produce something, or earn money.
  • Socially useful knowledge: credentials and status markers that make you appear educated within a particular group.
  • Knowledge for its own sake: understanding that makes you more perceptive, enriches your lived experience, or reveals beauty and possibility previously invisible to you.

During undergraduate years, the speaker experienced knowledge in an almost aesthetic way—walking into professors' offices for long conversations, discussing philosophical problems with friends, and checking out stacks of books simply because topics interested him. There was no immediate economic calculation. But as graduation approached and student debt loomed, education had to justify its cost and produce a return on investment.

The educational system presented two incompatible messages: become a well-rounded person who loves learning for its own sake, and acquire credentials and skills that make you valuable to employers. Neither goal is illegitimate, but they often conflict. The knowledge that most enlarges your inner life may not be what employers value. The question that fascinates you may produce no credential. The book that transforms you may add nothing measurable to your resume.

Practice: Conduct a learning audit. Write down everything you're currently learning and categorize it as livelihood knowledge, status knowledge, or knowledge for its own sake. Then ask: if I removed economic and social rewards from this activity, would I still care about understanding it? If almost nothing remains in the third category, you may have become highly educated for the market while allowing a significant part of your intellectual life to disappear.

Principle 2: separate knowledge from the prestige surrounding it

Dubuffet's target was not merely impractical subjects but the institutional process that tells us which ideas, artworks, and forms of knowledge deserve admiration. In Asphyxiating Culture, he describes people expressing enormous reverence for figures like the playwright Racine and painter Raphael without having encountered their work directly. They simply know that an educated person is supposed to admire these names, so the name itself functions as a cultural credential.

Intellectuals protect the prestige of canonical works because their own authority partly depends on possessing expertise to interpret them. Others learn to repeat the same judgments because they want to participate in the status of being cultured. This creates a strange situation: you can know a book is important without knowing what it says. You can identify the correct artist, movement, theory, or historical period without developing a direct relationship with the work itself. Your knowledge becomes something you display rather than something that changes how you see the world.

Dubuffet became fascinated by art brut (raw art)—creative work produced outside the recognized art establishment, including work by psychiatric patients, prisoners, children, and graffiti artists. He believed conventional culture could suffocate original expression by teaching people what legitimate art was supposed to look like. As Dubuffet put it: "Culture cannot stand butterflies that fly. It has to catch them, immobilize them, label them, and place them inside a recognized category."

Practice: Ask yourself two questions. First: if nobody could ever know that I read this book, studied this subject, or understood this theory, would I still want to learn it? If the answer is no, you might desire the identity associated with possessing the knowledge rather than the knowledge itself. Second: have I encountered this idea directly, or have I merely learned that respectable people consider it important? Before reading a summary of a classic book, read part of it. Before memorizing what critics say a painting means, look at it carefully and document what you notice. Before asking whether a subject appears on some list of things intelligent people should know, ask whether it produces a real question for you. The point is not to reject expertise but to prevent authority from replacing your own encounter with the object.

Principle 3: protect some knowledge from the demand that it justify itself

Stephen Fry once said, "It is the useless things that make life worth living." When we hear this, our impulse is often to defend useless knowledge by proving it isn't useless after all—citing abstract scientific discoveries that later produced technologies, or arguing that philosophy improves reasoning, literature increases empathy, or art makes employees more creative. Some of these claims may be true, but notice what has happened: we've accepted that knowledge deserves to exist only if it can eventually present evidence of usefulness.

Dubuffet proposes something more radical: some forms of knowing might be valuable even if they never increase your income, improve your productivity, or solve any external problem. Sometimes it is simply worth it in itself to understand why a particular piece of music affects you, how an extinct civilization understood death, why a mathematical proof is beautiful, or what a philosopher meant by a question with no accepted answer.

The experience of understanding possesses aesthetic value in itself. When you learn something, you don't merely add a fact to mental storage. You alter the very framework of your perception. Learning the constellations changes what appears when you look at the night sky. Learning architecture changes what you see walking through a city. Learning music changes what you hear in a symphony. Learning philosophy changes the questions you're capable of asking about your own life. None of this requires producing an economic reward to possess value.

Modern productivity culture has an extraordinary ability to colonize everything: reading becomes content production, exercise becomes optimization, a hobby becomes a potential business, a conversation becomes networking, curiosity becomes a strategy for getting ahead. But if every activity must produce external return, what part of your life is actually allowed to exist as an end in itself?

Principle 4: build two different curricula

Instead of expecting one curriculum to accomplish two incompatible goals, construct two separate ones.

The livelihood curriculum should be organized around useful competence—the knowledge and skills required to perform your work, earn money, support yourself, and contribute something others actually need. It should include concrete objectives, deliberate practice, feedback, and measurable standards. There is nothing shameful about usefulness; it is a genuine form of value.

The curriculum for its own sake can be constructed even over the next week:

  1. Select one question with no obvious professional payoff. Not a broad subject like history or philosophy, but a specific question you genuinely want to answer: Why do certain buildings feel sacred? What makes a mathematical proof beautiful? Why do human beings create fictional worlds? How did ancient cultures experience time? Can a person live meaningfully without believing life possesses objective purpose?

  2. Choose one direct point of entry. A primary book, artwork, body of music, historical document, or scientific source. You can draw on others' commentary when needed, but don't begin by outsourcing your judgment. Answer the thing itself before learning what an educated person is supposed to say about it.

  3. Spend three hours a week for four weeks following the question wherever it leads. Keep a journal answering four questions: What surprised me? What can I now perceive that I could not perceive before? Which assumption of mine did this challenge? What new question arose in my mind?

  4. End the month with a conversation, not an examination. Explain what you discovered to another person and allow them to challenge your interpretation. The final measure is not whether the subject increased your market value, but whether it deepened your experience or gave you a question worth continuing to pursue.

The institutional paradox

Universities can literally teach Dubuffet. Philosophy and art departments can assign Asphyxiating Culture, and individual professors can genuinely embody love of knowledge for its own sake. But the university as an institution operates through tuition, debt, credits, degrees, rankings, accreditation, employment outcomes, and the promise that education will produce economic return. It can teach Dubuffet's words but cannot fully adopt his principle without undermining the structure through which it justifies its own authority.

There is an almost perfect Dubuffet-like irony here: the university can take a radical attack on institutional culture, place it on an approved syllabus, ask students to reproduce its argument on an examination, and reward them with a credential for doing so. The butterfly is permitted to fly only after it has been caught, classified, and labeled.

Dubuffet himself was not entirely outside this contradiction. He became a celebrated figure within the very art world he criticized, and the raw art he championed eventually entered museums and acquired its own institutional prestige. But this doesn't undermine his argument—it simply reveals how difficult it is to escape. Culture can absorb even a rebellion against culture and turn it into yet another respectable object to admire.

Years after his sister first introduced him to Dubuffet, the speaker finally returned to ideas he had dismissed precisely because they did not appear useful.
