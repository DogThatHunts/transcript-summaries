"We Are In The Final Stages Of Western Collapse" | Barry Strauss

Classicist Barry Strauss examines the modern West's political instability and economic decline, drawing vital lessons from the fall of Rome and civilizational cycles to chart a path toward recovery.

In this conversation, classicist and military historian Barry Strauss joins host Peter McCormack to analyze the contemporary challenges facing Western democracies through the lens of ancient history. Strauss argues that while the West—particularly the United States and the United Kingdom—is experiencing a period of transition and visible decline characterized by political instability, inflation, and a hollowing out of the middle class, historical precedents show that collapse is not inevitable. Drawing on the civilizational cycles of Ibn Khaldun and the dramatic recovery of the third-century Roman Empire, Strauss emphasizes the critical need for civic education, cultural assimilation, and realistic leadership to restore social cohesion (asabia) and navigate the geopolitical threats posed by a rising China-led axis.

Key insights

  • Civilizations decay when they lose social cohesion (asabia): Strauss highlights 14th-century thinker Ibn Khaldun's theory that wealthy, soft societies inevitably lose their martial values and common sense, leaving them vulnerable to more cohesive, outer groups.
  • The digital revolution mirrors the printing press: Strauss compares the current social media and digital disruption to the invention of the printing press, which triggered the chaotic but ultimately transformative Protestant Reformation.
  • Rome reversed its decline through radical adaptation: During the third-century crisis, reforming emperors like Aurelian, Diocletian, and Constantine saved the empire by militarizing it, shifting capitals, and adopting Christianity as a unifying, fighting faith.
  • A strong middle class is the backbone of a republic: Just as the Roman Republic collapsed when its middle-class citizen-soldiers were impoverished and replaced by landless "proletarians," the modern West is jeopardized by the hollowing out of its industrial middle class.
  • Civic education and assimilation are vital for survival: Strauss criticizes modern Western education for promoting "luxury beliefs" and self-criticism, arguing instead for a revival of patriotic civics and mandatory cultural assimilation for immigrants.

The cycle of civilizational decline

Strauss references the 14th-century Arab sociologist Ibn Khaldun to explain how empires rise and fall. According to this model, societies start out tough and highly cohesive—a quality called asabia. Over generations of prosperity, they grow soft, adopt "luxury beliefs," lose their common sense, and begin criticizing their own culture. This vulnerability allows more disciplined, cohesive groups from the outside to conquer them. Strauss warns that many in the West falsely believe their societies are invulnerable to this cycle.

Lessons from rome's third-century crisis

When asked if decline can be reversed, Strauss points to the Roman Empire's survival of its third-century crisis. Facing a rapid succession of emperors, plagues, economic collapse, and foreign invasions, Rome was stabilized by military reformers like Aurelian, Diocletian, and Constantine. Strauss notes that saving Rome required transforming it: the emperors militarized the state, raised taxes, restricted personal freedoms, relocated capitals, and eventually adopted Christianity to establish a new cultural and religious unity.

The hollowing of the middle class

Strauss draws a direct parallel between the modern economic struggles of the US and UK working classes and the fall of the Roman Republic. In ancient Rome, prolonged foreign wars impoverished middle-class citizen-soldiers, whose farms were bought up by wealthy elites using slave labor. This forced the military to recruit the landless poor—contemptuously called "breeders" or proletarians—who became loyal to individual generals rather than the state. Strauss argues that the modern hollowing out of the Western industrial base to China similarly threatens the democratic middle class.

Geopolitical threats and the illusion of safety

The rise of a new authoritarian axis comprising China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea poses a severe challenge to Western liberal democracies. Strauss argues that Western leaders blithely assumed opening trade with China would lead to its democratization, whereas China played a highly successful, long-term strategic game. He warns that Western nations have lost the industrial capacity required to quickly rearm themselves, leaving them dangerously exposed.

Education, luxury beliefs, and the path forward

Strauss expresses deep concern over the state of Western education, which he claims teaches young people to feel shame about their nation's history rather than fostering civic pride. He contrasts this with "luxury beliefs"—ideas held by wealthy elites that harm the working class—and advocates for a return to rigorous civic education. Despite these challenges, Strauss finds hope in the grassroots rise of classical academies in the US and the enduring public trust in the military.
