Trump's Quiet War On London | Tom Luongo

Tom Luongo argues the Iran war is the visible front of a bond-market campaign against the City of London. Here is his case in detail, with the data checked.

Recorded around 7 October 2026 for TFTC, this 1-hour-50-minute conversation pairs host Marty Bent with geopolitical analyst Tom Luongo. Luongo's thesis is that the Iran war was never mainly about Iran. In his reading, it is the visible front of a financial campaign to break the City of London and the offshore "Eurodollar" system, and the real battlefield is the sovereign bond market: Treasury buybacks, German Bund spreads, the yen carry trade and the euro. Bent mostly supplies prompts and an optimistic read on US policy. What follows expands the original summary with the mechanics behind each argument, verbatim quotes with timestamped links, four charts built from public data, and a check of 25 factual claims against primary sources. Opinions are reported as opinions; the claim check marks where the facts support them and where they do not.

Key insights

  • "Iran hasn't closed the strait. London has." Luongo argues the war's decisive lever was maritime insurance, not missiles: when London-market war cover was pulled, tankers stopped for legal reasons. The record shows cover was repriced and partly cancelled, and the US built a $20 billion state reinsurance backstop to replace it.
  • Long-dated government bonds are now weapons. In his telling, foreign central banks dump old low-coupon Treasuries to raise dollars, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent buys them back at a discount. Treasury did double its long-end buyback size in August, but the "savings" arithmetic does not survive a present-value check.
  • Europe must choose: save its bonds or save its currency. Luongo says the ECB is pinning the German 10-year yield and has lost the cheap yen funding that made this possible, so the euro takes the strain. EUR/JPY did leave its 2026 range in September, and Italian and French spreads widened sharply in early October.
  • The oil market's tell is the Brent–WTI spread. A wide gap, he argues, shows financial players defending Brent-linked collateral. The gap is real and was widening; his claim that Brent is a "purely financial" contract is not.
  • Bitcoin as a weapon against the old order. Luongo floats a provocation he admits he is only testing: perhaps Bitcoin was built by US defence research, not to steal savings, but to break the Eurodollar system's grip on gold, silver and oil.

1. The real target is London, not Tehran

Luongo starts with the shipping market. He says Iran did not close the Strait of Hormuz. He says London-market insurers closed it. Without war-risk insurance, tankers cannot sail for legal reasons. Then a few Iranian strikes keep the risk visible and keep the price of Brent high. ▶ 07:25

"Iran hasn't closed the straits for moves. Lloyd's of London has closed the straits. The tankers can't move because of legal reasons, because they don't have any insurance."

The record is more mixed. From 1 March 2026, several insurers sent cancellation notices for Gulf war cover. Lloyd's said that cover stayed in place but was reviewed case by case. Premiums rose to about 5% of a ship's value. On 3 March, President Trump ordered the US Development Finance Corporation to sell insurance. It started a $20 billion reinsurance facility on 6 March. 2. Japan, the yen carry trade and the turn of the story

Luongo says the story changed in mid-June. The Bank of Japan raised its rate. Then Bessent became the main US voice, and the US intervened in the yen market. After that, he says, the war was "always a story about … break[ing] the old colonial powers of Europe". ▶ 09:51

His sequence starts in Japan. On 8 February, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi won a snap election. Her coalition with Nippon Ishin no Kai has, he says, "zero British influence". The strikes on Iran started 20 days later. ▶ 12:25

The yen carry trade is the key mechanism in his model. Investors borrow yen at a low rate and buy higher-yield assets. Luongo says European banks and the Bank of England used this cheap yen to support euro and pound assets. When Japan raises rates, this cheap funding stops.

The dates support the sequence, with gaps. The coalition existed before the election. The BoJ raised its rate on 16 June. The joint intervention came about six weeks later. [S5] [S6] [S7] Partly supported

3. The oil tell: the Brent–WTI spread

Luongo watches the price gap between Brent and WTI crude. On the day of recording, he reads WTI at about $88.50 and Brent at about $100.50. That is a $12 gap. ▶ 04:08

He says a wide gap means "someone is short collateral". Banks hold derivatives priced off Brent. If Brent falls, they must post more margin. So, he says, financial players push Brent up near each month end.

The futures prices are close to his numbers. Brent futures were $104.74 on 7 October. [S12] Spot data shows a wider gap. In 2025, the gap was about $4. It was about $29 on 6 October. Supported

One part of his argument is not correct. He calls Brent "purely financial", with no physical oil behind it. ICE Brent futures are deliverable through an exchange-for-physical process, with an option to cash-settle. [S13] Not supported

Luongo also says the attacks hit the middle and end of the supply chain. The pipelines, tankers and refineries are the targets, not the oil wells. A new tanker takes years to build. So each attack causes long stress. ▶ 77:17 The examples he gives are real. Ukraine hit a Russian tanker near Sochi in early October. A power outage stopped Exxon's Joliet refinery on 14 September. A fire stopped Venezuela's Cardón refinery on 6 October. [S14] [S15] No source shows sabotage at Joliet or Cardón.

4. Long bonds as weapons: the Treasury buyback

This is the centre of Luongo's argument. He says foreign central banks hold most of the 10-to-30-year Treasury bonds. He says Europe and the UK added "almost $2 trillion" over five years. ▶ 21:44

These bonds have low coupons of 2% to 3%. Yields are now near 5%, so the bonds trade below face value. Luongo says a central bank under pressure can sell them to get dollars. Bessent can then buy them back for less than face value.

"If the Bank of England dumps that thing on the market and you're the buyer on the other side, and you're willing to buy that off for $750,000 … you neither have to pay the 20 years in interest nor the full coupon value of the bond."

His example uses a $1 million bond with a 2.5% coupon and 20 years left. The US still owes $1.5 million in coupons and principal. A buyback at $750,000 removes that debt. He calls the difference a saving of $750,000. ▶ 22:31

Treasury did increase long-end buybacks. On 19 August it raised the maximum size to at least $4 billion per operation in the 10-to-30-year sectors. [S8] Supported

The "saving" does not survive the arithmetic. Treasury must borrow the cash for the buyback at today's yield. At 5.7%, new 20-year debt that pays for the buyback costs about $1.33 million in total cash. That is close to the $1.5 million it removes. In present-value terms, the two are equal. Treasury states that its buybacks support liquidity and cash management. It finances them like other outlays. [S16] Not supported

The holdings number is also too high. TIC data shows that the UK, Belgium, Luxembourg, Ireland and France held about $2.6 trillion in July 2026. They held about $1.6 trillion in July 2021. The increase is about $1.0 trillion, and it includes all maturities. [S17] Not supported

Luongo then links the long end to the short end. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh raises short-term rates. Higher bill rates bring new demand for Treasury bills, and stablecoins add more demand. The Fed did raise rates on 16 September, its first increase since 2023. ▶ 24:09 [S9] Supported

5. Europe's choice: its bonds or its currency

Luongo says the European Central Bank (ECB) has held the German 10-year yield down since 2022. He calls this "yield curve control". If the ECB holds the German yield, Germany anchors the yields of Italy and France. ▶ 30:54

The ECB's tool is the Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI), announced in July 2022. It is a backstop against "disorderly market dynamics". It does not set a target yield. Also, the ECB raised its rate to 2.5% on 10 September. [S18] [S19] Partly supported

He tracks three spreads: US minus Germany, Italy minus Germany, and France minus Italy. He says France became the weak point in Europe about nine months ago. ▶ 29:06

The data supports him on France. By August 2026, France paid slightly more than Italy to borrow for 10 years. In early October, the Italy–Germany spread went from about 102 to about 131 basis points. The France–Germany spread was about 147 basis points, the highest since 2011–12. The US–Germany spread was about 1.8 percentage points. [S20] [S21] [S22] Supported

"We're at that point where you can either save your bond market or you can save your currency, but you can't save both. And that's why the Euro is breaking down."

He says EUR/JPY stayed in a band of 181 to 186 for more than a year. He says it broke below 180 and is now near 176. ▶ 35:12

The chart shows a band of about 180.5 to 187.6 from December 2025 to August 2026. That is about nine months, not more than a year. The rate went below 180 on 8 September and was 176.9 on 7 October. [S23] Partly supported

Luongo also says that most central banks raised rates in the last cycle. He names two exceptions: the Bank of England and the Bank of Canada. ▶ 36:07 The Reserve Bank of Australia raised its rate on 29 September. The Bank of England held at 3.75% on 16 September. The Bank of Canada held at 2.25%. [S24] [S11] Supported

6. The dollar index and the "relative game"

Bent says sovereign debt is now "a relative game". All yields go up, so the question is who can pay the higher cost. ▶ 16:41 Luongo agrees. He says the US can pay because it issues the reserve currency. Europe cannot pay because it has little fiscal space.

Luongo reads the US dollar index (DXY) as a "Eurodollar index". It measures the dollar against the euro, yen, pound, Canadian dollar, Swedish krona and Swiss franc. It has no Chinese yuan. He says the index broke above 101 and is now near 102. He expects 110 to 115 later. ▶ 64:23 The DXY went above 102 on 1–2 October for the first time since April 2025. [S25] Supported

7. Bitcoin, gold and parallel money

Luongo tests an idea that he does not fully believe. Many critics say that US defence research (DARPA) made Bitcoin to trap savings. He suggests the opposite. Perhaps the US made Bitcoin to break the Eurodollar system and its control of gold, silver and oil prices. ▶ 70:20 No evidence for either version is given. The white paper did appear on 31 October 2008, four days before the US election. [S26]

As support, Luongo says Europe is the most hostile region to Bitcoin. He says the EU's MiCA licence rules removed about 80% of exchanges. Elliptic counted 213 licensed providers when the transition period ended on 1 July 2026. The 80% figure is an industry estimate only. [S27] Bent adds that criminals in France kidnap rich crypto holders. That is correct. A tax official in Bobigny is charged with the sale of investor data to criminal groups. [S28] Supported

For markets, Luongo reads quarterly closes. Bitcoin made a one-bar bullish reversal in Q3. Gold was neutral, and silver was weak. He expects oil to stay near $80 to $85 and gold to reach $20,000 "at some point". ▶ 75:33 These are forecasts, not checkable facts.

Bent ends with history. Constantine introduced the gold solidus. It stayed stable for about 700 years. He suggests that Bitcoin and stablecoins can have the same roles as the solidus and the silver coins above it. ▶ 106:29 [S29] Supported
