Palantir CEO Says Only 2 Types of Jobs Are SAFE From AI

AI is replacing routine jobs, but only two groups—practical tradespeople and neurodivergent thinkers—stand to thrive in the future.

The two groups that will thrive in an ai-driven future

Alex Karp, CEO of Palantir, argues that only two groups of people will have a secure future in the age of AI: those with practical hands-on skills and those who think differently due to neurodivergence.

The first group: hands-on tradespeople

Karp identifies tradespeople—such as electricians, plumbers, carpenters, and welders—as a group that AI cannot replicate.

  • These roles involve physical inspection, real-time judgment, and navigating complex, unstructured environments.
  • For example, a plumber diagnosing a flooded basement with no blueprints must rely on tactile senses and experience to detect a cracked pipe.
  • The decisions made in such situations—like which pipe to tap or how much of an old system to disturb—are based on real-world sensory input and local building codes.
  • This reflects Hans Moravec’s paradox: tasks we consider difficult (like balance and touch) are hard for machines, while tasks we think are easy (like logic) are simple for computers.

The second group: Neurodivergent thinkers

Karp, who is dyslexic, emphasizes that neurodivergent individuals—such as those with dyslexia, ADHD, or autism—will be key to future innovation.

  • These individuals often think differently: they don’t follow step-by-step playbooks, instead seeing problems from a holistic perspective.
  • Karp argues that corporate work has long been built on standardized playbooks (e.g., contract templates), which AI can easily replicate.
  • Neurodivergent thinkers, by contrast, spot patterns, anomalies, and connections that others miss.
  • Traits like ambiguity tolerance and pattern recognition are now valuable as AI takes over routine, playbook-based work.

Why this matters: a shift in education and hiring

Karp criticizes the traditional education system for producing graduates trained to follow rigid processes—exactly the kind of work AI now handles.

  • He specifically targets humanities and generalized degrees, warning they will lose relevance without a specific, tangible skill.
  • A degree that teaches compliance is training someone for a job AI can perform.

Palantir has already reorganized its hiring to reflect this vision:

  • Meritocracy Fellowship: For recent high school graduates (not university attendees), with SAT/ACT scores of at least 1460 or 33, and programming experience.
  • Runs for four to five months in New York.
  • Pays $5,400 per month.
  • Fellows study Western civilization, American history, philosophy, and religion.

  • Then work on live client projects with Palantir engineering teams.

  • Neurodivergent Fellowship: Open to all, with no diagnosis required.

  • Encourages self-taught applicants.
  • Full-time roles in New York or Washington, paying $110,000–$200,000 annually.
  • Final interviews are conducted personally by Alex Karp.

A bold shift in hiring

  • Palantir has moved away from traditional hiring pipelines (top grades, polished internships).
  • It now values real-world experience and diverse thinking over standardized credentials.
  • The company claims this is not a diversity initiative but a strategic decision: neurodivergent people will be the elite builders of the next technological era.

The bigger picture

  • As AI automates routine, rule-based work, employers will increasingly value skills that machines cannot replicate.
  • The future of work may belong not to graduates trained in standard systems, but to those who can solve problems in novel, unstructured ways.
