Seven legal documents every homeowner should have before they die to avoid con artists, court battles, and family disputes.

The seven Legal Documents Every Homeowner Needs

EpicRE shares seven key legal documents that help ensure your home, money, and medical decisions stay in the right hands after you pass.

1. last will and testament

A will is not a way to avoid court—it's a set of instructions handed to a probate judge.

  • Probate is a court process that settles debts and transfers property into someone else’s name.
  • On average, estate settlement takes 16 months and requires about 570 hours of work.
  • If you have no will, your state automatically applies intestate succession, which distributes your assets by law—possibly not what you intended.

A will is a ticket to court, not a way around it.

2. revocable living trust

A living trust holds your assets while you're alive and transfers them upon death without court involvement.

  • You remain the trustee during your lifetime, so daily life stays unchanged.
  • After death, a successor trustee signs over ownership to the named person—no public record, no court.
  • A pour-over will acts as a safety net, directing any assets not in the trust to it.
  • If you have minor children, your will names guardians for them.

Many people buy a trust binder and never update their deed. If your deed still lists your name, the house goes to probate.

3. beneficiary designations

These forms—on life insurance, 401(k), IRA, or payable-on-death accounts—override wills and trusts.

  • The name on these forms is who gets paid, not what’s in your will or trust.
  • Example: William Kennedy named his wife as a beneficiary in 1974. After divorce, she gave up her claim—but he never updated the form. When he died, the plan paid her $400,000. His daughter sued and lost in the Supreme Court.
  • In many states, a transfer-on-death deed allows your house to pass directly to a named person without probate.
  • In Nevada, it’s called a deed upon death—named for its horror-like sound, but it works the same.
  • The deed must be recorded at the county before your death; otherwise, it’s worth no more than a pinky promise.

The form says what it says. If your life insurance names an old girlfriend, she gets the check.

4. HIPAA authorization

This one-page form allows doctors and hospitals to share medical information with named family members.

  • HIPAA law permits sharing with family, but doesn’t require it.
  • Many hospitals and insurers play it safe and say no.
  • A HIPAA authorization removes ambiguity by naming who can access your medical records.
  • Sign it when you complete this list and give a copy to your doctor’s office.

5. health care power of attorney

This document names the person who makes medical decisions for you if you can’t.

  • Without it, your state’s default list applies—usually spouse and adult children.
  • In some states, if kids disagree, a majority vote decides.
  • In four states—Massachusetts, Minnesota, Missouri, and Rhode Island—there is no default list, and a judge must intervene.
  • Only about one in five adults has a medical power of attorney.

Picture three grown kids with different opinions standing in a hospital hallway voting on your care.

6. living will

A living will outlines your wishes about end-of-life care—like whether to use a feeding tube or CPR.

  • The health care power of attorney decides who makes the call.
  • The living will tells them what you would have decided.
  • Example: In 1990, Terry Schiavo collapsed and never recovered. She had no living will. Her husband said she wouldn’t want life support, but her parents disagreed. The case went through 14 appeals and a Supreme Court denial—all because no one knew what she wanted.

A single signed page could have told them.

7. durable financial power of attorney

This document allows someone to manage your finances if you can no longer sign your own name.

  • It’s durable, meaning it remains active after you lose decision-making capacity.
  • About one in nine Americans over 65 has Alzheimer’s—plus many others face strokes or falls.
  • If both names are on the deed, both signatures are needed to sell or refinance.
  • Without a power of attorney, you must go to court to appoint a guardian (a conservator), which may require annual reporting and court permission to sell the house.
  • Adding a child to the deed creates tax and creditor issues.
  • A power of attorney avoids handing over the house.

This document must be signed when you’re still able to understand what you’re signing. The day after diagnosis may already be too late.

Do not make decisions based on this content. Always consult an actual attorney and have them review these documents for your state.

Final notes

  • None of these documents are exotic. A good local estate attorney handles them every week.
  • Protectmyhome.inc can help you connect with an attorney.
  • You don’t need to complete all seven at once.
  • A simple first step: visit your county recorder’s website, search for your name, and verify who is listed on your deed.
  • If you have a trust, confirm the trust name is actually on the deed.
  • If your parents are still alive, run this same check on their house over Thanksgiving.

It’s easier to have this conversation over pumpkin pie than across a mahogany table from a probate attorney who bills by the hour.

All seven documents decide who gets your house and who can make medical calls when you can’t. None decide how much money remains after the house is paid off.
