The quiet shift in Berlin's China strategy

Germany is quietly shifting its economic posture toward China, moving beyond defensive planning to actively map Chinese economic vulnerabilities. According to Jeff Snider of EurodollarU, German officials are studying trade flows, supply chains, and individual companies to identify critical bottlenecks where Beijing remains dependent on European technology. This represents a historic pivot for a country that spent decades building its manufacturing sector around deep commercial integration with China. Snider argues that Berlin now views a direct economic confrontation as plausible enough to begin establishing pressure points for deterrence.

The collapse of the German industrial model

Germany's traditional economic engine, which relied on cheap Russian pipeline gas and high-value industrial exports, has broken down. The loss of inexpensive energy following the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, combined with Germany's decision to phase out its remaining nuclear power plants, has left domestic manufacturers facing unsustainably high electricity and heating costs.

A survey by the German Chamber of Commerce and Industry (DIHK) highlights the scale of this deindustrialization: * Delayed investment: One-third of the 3,000 surveyed companies have postponed expansions due to energy costs. * Relocation: Nearly one-fifth of firms are actively reducing capacity in Germany or moving production abroad.

Snider emphasizes that this structural erosion leaves Germany poorly positioned to absorb further competitive pressure from Chinese imports.

Mutual dependency and technological bottlenecks

While Germany faces severe domestic challenges, Europe still retains significant leverage in high-tech manufacturing bottlenecks. Snider highlights several critical German companies embedded in global semiconductor supply chains: * TRUMPF: A specialist in advanced industrial laser technology. * Carl Zeiss: Producer of precise optical systems essential for ASML's advanced lithography machines. * Siltronic, Aixtron, and SUSS Microtech: Suppliers of silicon wafers and specialized semiconductor manufacturing equipment.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz has framed this relationship under a doctrine of mutual dependency. Rather than seeking complete independence from China, Berlin's strategy appears to rely on deterrence—the threat of cutting off access to irreplaceable precision components if Beijing restricts critical raw materials.

The pressure of a billion-euro daily trade deficit

The European Union's trade deficit with China now exceeds €1 billion per day. EU officials argue that this imbalance is driven by state-supported overcapacity, where Chinese subsidies and weak domestic demand force factories to export surplus goods at prices European firms cannot match.

While Beijing's Ministry of Commerce rejects these allegations—attributing its success to supply chain efficiency and genuine global demand—Snider argues that both regions are trapped by broader macroeconomic forces. Lacking robust domestic growth, China has no choice but to attempt to export its way forward, while Europe must defend its remaining industrial base from being hollowed out.

Domestic political decay driving protectionism

The push toward a trade war is heavily accelerated by political instability within Germany. Chancellor Merz's coalition, elected just last year, has seen its public approval plummet, with an RTL poll indicating an 85% dissatisfaction rate. Meanwhile, the populist Alternative for Germany (AfD) party continues to rise in the polls.

Snider points out that previous top-down economic interventions, such as the "Berlin bazooka" fiscal push, have failed to restart growth. Facing an angry electorate and struggling manufacturers, German politicians are under immense pressure to take highly visible, protectionist actions against foreign competitors, even if those actions carry severe economic risks.

The mechanics of a fracturing global economy

A full-scale trade war between the EU and China would likely escalate in retaliatory phases: * European measures: Tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles, batteries, chemicals, and steel, alongside stricter investment screening and export controls. * Chinese retaliation: Tariffs on European luxury goods, vehicles, and machinery, alongside regulatory delays and restrictions on critical rare earth minerals.

Snider warns that while trade defenses may offer temporary political cover, they cannot resolve Germany's internal structural issues, such as high energy costs, labor shortages, and regulatory friction. Instead, a trade war risks accelerating global deglobalization, forcing expensive supply chain duplications, and raising prices for consumers worldwide.
