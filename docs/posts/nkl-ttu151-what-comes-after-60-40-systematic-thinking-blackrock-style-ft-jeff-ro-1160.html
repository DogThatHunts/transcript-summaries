In this episode of Top Traders Unplugged, hosts Niels Kostrup-Larsen and Alan Dunn sit down with Jeff Rosenberg, Managing Director at BlackRock Systematic, to explore the evolving landscape of portfolio construction in a post-COVID world. Rosenberg discusses the structural transition from an era of too little inflation to one of persistent, above-target inflation, which has disrupted traditional stock-bond correlations and weakened the diversification efficacy of fixed income. To fill this gap, he advocates for a whole-portfolio approach that incorporates liquid alternatives and systematic strategies. Rosenberg details BlackRock's approach to systematic investing, the design choices behind wrapping trend-following and multi-strategy portfolios into ETFs (such as ISMF and the upcoming IALT), and how their proprietary "defensive alpha" strategy leverages credit insights on equities to generate resilient, uncorrelated returns during market downturns.

Five key insights

  1. The structural shift in inflation: Jeff Rosenberg argues that the pre-COVID era was defined by too little inflation, while the post-COVID era is defined by persistent, above-target inflation (lingering around 3%), which eliminates the "divine coincidence" of monetary policy and complicates central bank decision-making.
  2. Bonds as income, not just diversifiers: Due to shifting stock-bond correlations in an inflationary environment, fixed income has transitioned from being a reliable portfolio parachute (total return/diversification) back to its historical role as an income-generating asset class.
  3. The "defensive alpha" equity strategy: Rosenberg explains that by narrowing their equity long-short universe to companies with debt on their balance sheets, BlackRock's defensive alpha strategy exploits accelerated dispersion during market downturns, creating a payoff profile similar to a long put option without the premium decay.
  4. Breadth versus scalability in ETFs: While some trend-following ETFs rely on highly concentrated replication models that can suffer from luck-of-the-draw performance (e.g., holding cocoa futures in 2024), BlackRock's ISMF targets a sweet spot of over 40 highly liquid contracts to balance diversification with institutional scalability.
  5. Mitigating market impact via internal liquidity: Rosenberg highlights that systematic trading at BlackRock benefits from "stepping into the river" of the firm's massive aggregate transaction flows, allowing them to execute trades with significantly less market impact than standalone funds.

The post-covid macro regime and the end of divine coincidence

Jeff Rosenberg frames the current macroeconomic landscape as a structural adjustment to a post-COVID environment. He emphasizes that the pre-COVID world suffered from too little inflation, whereas the post-COVID world is characterized by too much, or persistent, above-target inflation. This shift has eliminated what economists call the "divine coincidence" of monetary policy—a period when central banks could simultaneously pursue growth and inflation targets with a single, accommodative policy direction.

In this new regime, central bankers face constant tension. Rosenberg notes that Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell has acknowledged there is no longer a risk-free path for monetary policy. While inflation has cooled from its peak, it remains sticky around 3%. Rosenberg suggests that while raising the inflation target to 3% might make economic sense, doing so presents a credibility problem for the Fed, leaving investors under-compensated for inflation risk and driving uncertainty around term premiums.

Rethinking fixed income and the rise of liquid alternatives

With the loss of "divine coincidence," the historical negative correlation between stocks and bonds has weakened, reducing the effectiveness of bonds as a portfolio diversifier. Rosenberg explains that for thirty years, investors treated fixed income as a total-return asset class that doubled as an insurance policy. In the current environment, however, fixed income is reverting to its historical origin as a pure income-generating asset class.

To replace the lost diversification of bonds, Rosenberg and host Alan Dunn discuss the necessity of alternative diversifiers. Rosenberg advocates for a "whole portfolio" lens, suggesting a transition from the traditional 60/40 model to a "50-30-20" framework, where 20% of the portfolio is allocated to alternatives. For most individual and retail investors, Rosenberg argues this allocation is best served through liquid alternatives, which offer uncorrelated return streams without lock-up periods.

Defining systematic investing at BlackRock

Rosenberg addresses the linguistic ambiguity of the term "systematic." While some associate it strictly with trend following or CTAs, and others with Fama-French style equity factors, BlackRock Systematic views it through the lens of active portfolio management, specifically referencing the foundational work of Richard Grinold and Ron Kahn. For BlackRock, systematic investing means engineering portfolios for highly explicit, expected outcomes by separating alpha, beta, and strategic risk premiums.

Addressing the rise of artificial intelligence, Rosenberg distinguishes between predictive AI—which BlackRock has utilized for years—and newer generative AI models. He warns of a saturation risk in the industry, referencing the movie The Incredibles to note that "if everyone is a superhero, then no one is." As more market participants adopt systematic tools, maintaining a distinct, data-driven edge becomes increasingly challenging but remains essential for delivering true, information-ratio-driven alpha.

Engineering trend following for the ETF wrapper

BlackRock entered the pure trend-following space with the launch of the Systematic Managed Futures ETF (ISMF). Rosenberg explains that the decision to launch a trend strategy in an ETF wrapper was driven by the desire to bring institutional-grade liquid alternatives to retail clients in a modern, tax-efficient, and accessible format.

However, adapting trend following to a 40-Act ETF or UCITS structure requires navigating strict regulatory constraints, particularly regarding leverage and liquidity. Rosenberg notes that low-volatility contracts require high notional leverage to achieve meaningful risk targets, which can conflict with ETF leverage limits. To manage this, BlackRock designed ISMF to trade over 40 highly liquid contracts across foreign exchange, rates, commodities, and equities, balancing the "law of active management" (maximizing breadth) with the capacity demands of a highly scalable ETF.

Unpacking defensive alpha and capital structure dispersion

Within BlackRock's multi-strategy offerings and the upcoming iShares Systematic Alternatives Active ETF (IALT), Rosenberg highlights a proprietary strategy called "defensive alpha." This strategy roots itself in Robert Merton's capital structure models, using credit-based balance sheet signals to trade a long-short portfolio of equities.

Unlike typical quantitative equity strategies that maximize breadth across thousands of names, defensive alpha narrows its universe to approximately 500 to 600 companies that have debt on their balance sheets. Rosenberg explains that debt acts as an accelerator of dispersion, particularly during market downturns when equity analysts pivot their focus from income statements to balance sheets, liquidity, and credit costs. By systematically overweighting these credit signals, the strategy generates an asymmetric payoff profile that mimics a long put option—performing exceptionally well in down markets and remaining stable in up markets—without the premium decay associated with buying options.

Addressing manager dispersion and market concentration

Niels Kostrup-Larsen raises concerns regarding the proliferation of low-fee trend-following ETFs. He cautions that some of these products achieve scalability and low fees by severely compromising on market breadth, which could lead to performance that fails to replicate traditional CTA behavior during critical market events. Rosenberg agrees, noting that in 2024, the performance of trend managers was highly dispersed based on whether they held niche contracts like cocoa futures. He advises investors to "look under the hood" to manage selection risk within liquid alternatives.

Alan Dunn points out that the equity market's prolonged rally has left investors highly exposed to concentration risk, with the top 10 names in the S&P 500 now commanding roughly 40% of its total market cap. Rosenberg concludes that this unprecedented concentration makes the case for uncorrelated, systematic strategies stronger than ever, as traditional market betas no longer provide the structural diversification they once did.
