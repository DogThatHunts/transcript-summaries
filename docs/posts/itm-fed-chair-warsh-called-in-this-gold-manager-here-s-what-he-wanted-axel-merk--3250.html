Fed Chair Warsh called in this gold manager—here's what he wanted

Summary

Axel Merk, a precious metals manager and longtime monetary policy analyst, sat down with ITM Trading's Daniela Camboni to discuss his early August meeting with Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh—the first public account of that conversation. Merk emphasized that while he cannot disclose specifics, the meeting was part of Warsh's effort to solicit input from a broad range of perspectives as he attempts to reshape Fed policy away from the interventionist approaches of his predecessors. The discussion touched on Warsh's skepticism of quantitative easing, the balance sheet problem, and why gold remains relevant despite elevated real interest rates.

Key insights

  1. Warsh is actively seeking outside input. Merk argues that Warsh's July messaging confusion, followed by clarity at Jackson Hole and in September, suggests the Fed chair was testing communication strategies and gathering diverse viewpoints—a departure from the Fed's traditional insularity.

  2. The meeting was not about gold. Despite speculation linking Warsh, Treasury advisor Judy Shelton, and gold-standard advocates, Merk was explicit: he and Warsh did not discuss gold. Instead, they focused on monetary policy dynamics and institutional reform.

  3. Warsh is a hawk who dislikes QE. Merk characterizes Warsh as fundamentally opposed to quantitative easing, having resigned from the Fed in protest over slow emergency-measure drawdowns. However, Warsh does not want to "use a sledgehammer" on the economy; he seeks a modest tightening bias aligned with growth.

  4. The balance sheet is the hardest problem. Merk argues that fixing the Fed's bloated balance sheet requires returning to pre-2008 market intervention to set rates—a politically and institutionally difficult path that the New York Fed resists.

  5. Gold can perform in a "slight tightening" environment. Real 10-year yields at 2.95% are elevated, but Merk argues this reflects confidence in Warsh's approach. Gold can still appreciate if the Fed maintains a modest tightening bias without crushing growth, and if fiscal pressures eventually force a reckoning.

The meeting with Warsh

Merk explained how the meeting came about: he has been critical of Fed policy for decades but found Warsh's public criticism aligned with his own. After attending multiple monetary policy conferences where Warsh spoke, Merk was invited to discuss policy in early August. The meeting, scheduled for 30 minutes, ran longer due to a scheduled break afterward. Merk described it as a "two-way dialogue" and noted that Warsh was receptive and engaged.

Merk emphasized that he does not operate as a conspiracy theorist. Rather than believing shadowy figures pull strings, he argues that "the world is driven by incentives" and that most policymakers try to do the right thing, even if unintended consequences follow. This framing matters: the convergence of Warsh, Shelton, and Merk is not coordinated but reflects aligned thinking about Fed reform.

Warsh's vision for the Fed

According to Merk's reading, Warsh wants to achieve several things:

  • Stop the micromanagement. Simply halting the Fed's "slippery slope" of ever-greater economic intervention would constitute success. Merk argues this alone would be a win.
  • Restore market feedback. By stepping back from controlling every market signal, the Fed can receive genuine price discovery and adjust policy accordingly—rather than relying on stale data and Fed officials "reading tea leaves."
  • Separate monetary from fiscal policy. Warsh, like Merk, appears to view the Fed's credit allocation (e.g., buying mortgage-backed securities) as an encroachment on fiscal turf that invites political interference.
  • Avoid QE absent true emergency. Warsh's bar for quantitative easing is "extreme," per Merk. Emergency measures are part of the Fed's toolkit, but they must be wound down promptly—a principle Warsh violated in his prior role.

Merk also notes that Warsh has spoken favorably of Paul Volcker and Alan Greenspan but never mentions Bernanke, Yellen, or Powell except in jest. This suggests Warsh admires Volcker's willingness to re-anchor inflation expectations without obsessing over the precise 2% target, and Greenspan's lighter touch.

The gold question and the oil-gold disconnect

When pressed on whether the administration is positioning for a gold-standard revival, Merk was cautious. He argues that Warsh was appointed because he believes in "growth-driven productivity growth" as a way to outgrow fiscal problems—not because of a gold agenda. Judy Shelton's presence at the Treasury may help keep gold-linked instruments in policy conversations, but Merk doubts the Treasury will issue gold-backed debt, fearing it would undermine confidence in Treasuries themselves.

On the oil-gold disconnect that has puzzled observers: Merk explained that gold typically rises when policymakers react to supply shocks with inflation, not from the shock itself. The 1970s oil crisis boosted gold because central banks responded inflationary; the pandemic spike came from Fed and central bank intervention, not the virus. The recent Iran conflict created an oil shock, but absent dramatic political intervention, it is deflationary and thus negative for gold. Merk monitors whether the correlation between oil and bond yields breaks down, signaling that markets are pricing in the disinflationary impact and moving on.

Gold demand and the case for ownership

Merk broke down gold demand into categories: central banks (diversifying from dollars), gold bugs (concerned about purchasing power), diversification buyers, and speculators. Speculators fueled much of 2024's rally but have retreated; retail participation remains modest. However, long-term investors have been steady, and generalist fund managers are newly interested in gold mining, suggesting broadening interest.

Merk also highlighted an underreported dynamic: tariffs reduce currency inflows to the US, meaning fewer foreign bond purchases and higher yields. This is one reason long-term yields have risen, alongside fiscal concerns.

On whether investors should own gold now: Merk's answer is pragmatic. Gold is volatile; only buy if you can afford the risk and sleep at night. He holds gold until "fiscal sanity breaks out in Washington"—a quip that drew knowing laughter. More importantly, he emphasized that spending less than you earn gives you flexibility across all investments. He does not advocate gold because he expects a government-mandated gold standard (why trust the government?), but as a personal hedge and diversifier.

The broader context: Fed independence and fiscal reality

Merk stressed that Fed independence is not about presidential tweets but about keeping the Fed out of fiscal policy. Tariffs, fiscal deficits, and political pressure all complicate Warsh's task. Yet Merk applauded Warsh's effort to bend the arc of history, even if transformative change is unlikely. He cited Volcker's "three verities": stable monetary policy, stable fiscal policy, and sound institutions—all three are suffering, but incremental improvement matters.

Merk also pushed back on the notion that everything is hopeless. He acknowledged middle-class frustration with inflation and cost-of-living pressures but argued that people like Warsh, who did not need the job, deserve credit for trying to put the Fed on a better path. "It's easy to criticize, it's much more difficult to lead."
