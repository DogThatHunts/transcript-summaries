Russell H. Conwell's timeless lecture, "Acres of Diamonds," originally delivered over 50 years prior to this 1962 condensed audio edition, continues to inspire by asserting that opportunities for wealth and success are often found in one's immediate surroundings.

Introduction

Russell H. Conwell, a multifaceted figure who was a soldier, teacher, journalist, lawyer, minister, and founder of Temple University, delivered the influential speech "Acres of Diamonds." The lecture's enduring message, characterized by Conwell's sincerity and enthusiasm, emphasizes that personal success is often within reach, requiring only a shift in perspective.

The parable of Ali Hafed

The core of Conwell's message is illustrated through the story of Ali Hafed, a wealthy and contented Persian farmer living near the River Indus. After a Buddhist priest tells him about the immense value of diamonds, Ali Hafed becomes discontent, obsessed with finding a diamond mine. He sells his farm, leaves his family, and embarks on a long, fruitless search across continents, eventually dying in poverty and despair. Meanwhile, the man who bought Ali Hafed's farm discovers priceless diamonds in the white sands of a brook on the very property Ali Hafed had sold. This discovery led to the famous Kalkanda diamond mine, the source of renowned jewels like the Koh-i-Noor.

The lesson of local opportunity

The Arab guide who told Conwell this story concluded that had Ali Hafed stayed and dug in his own backyard, he would have found his "acres of diamonds." Conwell extends this moral to everyone, asserting that "diamonds of opportunity" are present in our own lives, jobs, and communities. He argues that the opportunity to achieve great wealth honestly is more accessible than ever, particularly in his contemporary America.

The importance of common sense

Conwell states that the first step towards success is not capital, but "common sense." He describes common sense as the map, spade, and knowledge needed to find and wisely use these opportunities. He emphasizes that wealth can be found through improving one's current trade or profession, offering additional service to existing customers, and leveraging opportunities within one's current job, company, or industry.

Illustrations of overlooked wealth

Conwell provides further examples to reinforce his point:

  • Changeable Chester: A young man who constantly changed jobs, homes, and relationships in a "fruitless search for progress, money, and success." Chester's error was changing everything except himself and his attitude towards his current situation, leading to mere substitution rather than genuine improvement.
  • The Pennsylvania farmer: A cautious farmer sold his land to seek work in the oil industry in Canada. Shortly after, the buyer of his farm discovered oil on the property, which became Titusville, Pennsylvania, the birthplace of the US oil industry. The original farmer had been surrounded by "liquid diamonds" but failed to recognize them.
  • The mining engineer: A Yale-educated mining engineer, confident in his knowledge, sold his mother's Vermont farm to search for ore deposits elsewhere. The man who purchased the farm subsequently discovered a priceless load of pure silver on the land, the first such discovery in Vermont's rocky hills. The engineer possessed the knowledge but refused to look for opportunities close to home.

Finding your own acres of diamonds

Conwell concludes that our "acres of diamonds" are within ourselves, our families, neighborhoods, jobs, communities, and wider regions. He urges a change in attitude rather than merely changing locations or vocations. True success, he argues, begins with greatness towards oneself and those closest to us, rooted in humility, fear of God, and love for fellow man. The opportunities are always present, waiting to be discovered through diligent search in one's own backyard.
