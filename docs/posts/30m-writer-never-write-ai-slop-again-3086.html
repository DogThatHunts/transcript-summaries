$30M Writer: Never write AI slop again

Nicholas Cole joins Greg Eisenberg to discuss how writers and brands can build unique personal language models to escape generic AI content and establish true digital moats.

In this conversation, host Greg Eisenberg talks with digital writer and entrepreneur Nicholas Cole about how to write high-leverage content in the age of AI. Cole argues that "AI slop" is simply commodity content repackaged without a unique point of view. To survive and build a true digital moat, creators and companies must construct a "digital brain" or personal language model—a library of personal stories, specific opinions, and approved language. By training AI to repeat and remix our existing ideas rather than generate net-new thoughts, writers can automate low-leverage distribution while freeing up finite time for original, timeless thinking.

Key insights

  1. SaaS and content moats require a distinct point of view. Commodity features and generic advice are easily replicated by AI, but products and writing driven by a specific methodology remain highly defensible.
  2. Language is open source, making ownership a matter of association. Because no one owns individual words or common phrases, establishing "ownership" of an idea requires high volume and distinct personality details so the audience associates the concept with you.
  3. AI should repeat you, not think for you. The highest leverage use of AI is having it remix and distribute your "approved language" (existing stories and frameworks) rather than allowing it to generate net-new ideas from scratch.
  4. The "I" perspective beats the "you" perspective. Writing from personal experience ("I did this") creates unique personality content, whereas prescriptive writing ("you should do this") easily degrades into generic, commodity slop.
  5. Voice is a structured, unmade data set. Voice is not an intangible mystery; it is built from specific word choices, sentence lengths, and formatting that must be actively written and defined before an LLM can replicate it.

The death of commodity SaaS and the power of a point of view

Nicholas Cole argues against the popular belief that SaaS no longer has a moat in the AI era. He explains that while commodity SaaS—platforms that simply provide features to accomplish tasks—lacks a moat, SaaS driven by a distinct "point of view" remains highly defensible.

Using his platform TypeShare as an example, Cole explains that the tool is built around a specific methodology for writing on the internet rather than just scheduling posts. When a product is driven by a unique philosophy, competitors are forced to constantly look back and copy the creator's thinking, leaving them perpetually steps behind. This same principle applies directly to content creation.

Building a personal language model to scale your stories

To avoid writing generic AI slop, Cole advises writers to build a "digital brain" or personal language model. This is a library of your own stories, opinions, lessons, and approved language.

Drawing on his decade of experience ghostwriting for over 300 clients, Cole notes that ghostwriters rarely invent new ideas; instead, they stitch together a client's previously expressed thoughts and approved phrasing. AI can and should perform this exact function. By feeding an LLM your unique personal database, you can instruct the AI to repeat and remix your existing concepts into new formats. This automates low-leverage repetition while freeing up human time for net-new, original thinking.

Establishing ownership through volume and personality

Because language is fundamentally open source, true ownership of an idea online is defined by association. Cole points out that we do not own words or common sentences. Instead, we establish association in two ways:

  • Volume: Writing about a topic so frequently that the audience naturally connects the subject with your name.
  • Personality: Sharing specific life details, formative experiences, and personal mistakes.

Cole shares how his own repeated mention of playing World of Warcraft professionally at age 17 became a signature hook that differentiated his writing. Even mundane details, like moving across the country, allow readers to find common ground. High-personality content consistently outperforms highly credible but dry commodity content.

Timely versus timeless content on the value spectrum

Every piece of content sits on a spectrum between timely and timeless. Cole and Eisenberg break down the trade-offs of both approaches:

  • Timely content: Offers high short-term distribution but has low long-term value. Eisenberg's viral memes capitalizing on recent tech announcements are highly effective for 24 hours but cannot be easily repurposed.
  • Timeless content: Offers low short-term distribution but high long-term value. Deep, structural essays require significant time to write but can be referenced, reused, and remixed for a decade.

Understanding where a piece of writing sits on this spectrum helps creators align their distribution expectations and decide how to feed their digital brain.

Demystifying voice as an unmade data set

Many creators struggle with AI because the output does not "sound like them." Cole demystifies "voice," explaining that it is not an intangible quality but a combination of concrete variables: word choice, sentence length, and sentence structure.

When ghostwriting, Cole found that changing as little as 1% of a draft—such as swapping an adjective or adding authoritative references like "according to Harvard Business Review"—instantly made the piece sound authentic to the client. Because your unique voice is an "unmade data set" that AI cannot guess, creators must do the initial writing themselves to establish these stylistic parameters.

The future of marketing: company brains as a service

Eisenberg and Cole predict a major shift in corporate marketing toward building "company brains." Rather than focusing on superficial metrics like posting times or buying retweets, forward-thinking companies will hire deep-thinking journalists and writers to build high-quality, long-form content libraries.

Once a company possesses a rich, proprietary database of original industry insights, AI agents can easily slice, dice, and automate the distribution of thousands of social posts, videos, and newsletters. The proprietary data set itself becomes the ultimate marketing moat.
