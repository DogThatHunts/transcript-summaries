The Riyadh airport strike and the unraveling of the 1974 bargain

A missile launched from Yemen struck Terminal 3 of King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, resulting in 12 deaths—including a US citizen—and over 300 injuries. While mainstream coverage frames the event primarily as a regional military conflict, Felix argues that the true fallout threatens international finance.

Following the attack, Saudi officials reached out to France, Britain, Pakistan, and Turkey for security arrangements rather than relying solely on Washington. This shift calls into question the foundational agreement established in 1974, in which Saudi Arabia committed to selling its crude exclusively in US dollars and recycling those revenues into American assets in exchange for military protection. If US security guarantees appear uncertain, Felix contends that the rationale for Saudi Arabia to park hundreds of billions of dollars in US Treasuries and maintain its strict currency peg begins to weaken.

The mechanics and vulnerability of the petrodollar peg

The petrodollar framework forces global buyers to acquire US dollars before purchasing oil, effectively creating structural international demand for the currency and enabling large US deficits. Furthermore, the Saudi riyal has maintained a fixed peg to the US dollar since 1986, requiring the Saudi central bank to mirror Federal Reserve interest-rate policy regardless of domestic economic conditions.

Saudi Arabia backs this arrangement with approximately $450 billion in foreign currency reserves and an estimated $140 billion in US Treasuries. However, Felix highlights that Riyadh offloaded roughly $11 billion of US debt within a single recent month. While not definitive proof of an aggressive dump, Felix views this divestment as a symptom of strain between Riyadh and Washington, compounded by lower oil sales and shifting geopolitical priorities.

Physical bottlenecks and alternative financial settlement rails

The security of oil flows faces dual bottlenecks along critical transit corridors:

  • Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea: Approximately a quarter of global oil transits Hormuz. Hostilities along Yemen's coast also threaten the Red Sea backup route, raising shipping freight rates and tanker risk premiums to historic highs.
  • Refinery targeting: Recent drone and missile claims on domestic infrastructure around Yanbu and Riyadh indicate that processing nodes remain directly exposed.
  • Bypassing the dollar: On the monetary front, central banks are testing multilateral platforms such as Project mBridge, which facilitates direct currency settlement for cross-border trade without intermediation through the US dollar. Although Saudi Arabia concluded its participation after the pilot phase, neighboring economies like the UAE remain engaged, and Riyadh has openly explored pricing select oil shipments in euros and Chinese renminbi.

Central bank gold accumulation as an institutional hedge

While retail investors and institutional funds retreated during gold's recent cyclical pullbacks, global central banks continued aggressive gold accumulation. Felix contrasts this divergence between private money and reserve managers, arguing that monetary authorities are deliberately diversifying away from unbacked sovereign debt.

Because the 1974 agreement remains an unbacked paper accord reliant on mutual trust, Felix advises tracking official Saudi gold reserves as the critical metric. A formal, sustained increase in sovereign gold holdings by major Gulf producers would signal a structural shift away from paper reserve currencies toward hard collateral.

Investor implications: stress-testing against an oil shock

Felix warns that a modern energy crisis does not remain localized; higher energy prices filter through transportation and consumer baskets before eroding portfolio valuations. Traditional buy-and-hold approaches built during a 40-year disinflationary trend may struggle under structural resource volatility.

To prepare, Felix suggests three analytical steps:

  1. Consolidate holdings: Aggregate pensions, brokerage accounts, and funds to evaluate gross exposure.
  2. Apply the 2022 test: Review asset behavior during the 2022 inflation and energy surge to identify holdings that protected capital versus those that deteriorated.
  3. Identify vulnerable companies: Separate firms capable of passing through energy input costs from capital-intensive businesses trapped in margin-compression zones.
