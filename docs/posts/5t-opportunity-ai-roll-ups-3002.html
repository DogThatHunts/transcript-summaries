$5T opportunity: AI Roll Ups

A massive wave of retiring small business owners is creating a multi-trillion-dollar opportunity for solo founders to acquire service firms and supercharge their margins using AI agents.

The five trillion dollar retirement wave meets the AI agent revolution

Over the next decade, millions of small business owners will retire, triggering what McKinsey estimates to be a $5 trillion transfer of ownership involving roughly one million businesses by 2035. Many of these businesses—ranging from local accounting practices and insurance agencies to property management firms—have loyal client bases built over 30 years but lack clear succession plans.

At the same time, AI agents have advanced to the point where they can reliably execute repetitive back-office tasks like data entry, document chasing, and draft generation. Traditionally, service firms operate on slim 5% to 10% profit margins. Greg Isenberg argues that by acquiring these businesses and integrating a shared layer of AI agents, solo founders can automate the bulk of the administrative workload, driving profit margins up to 30% or 40% without changing the external customer experience.

The institutional playbook: how Thrive and General Catalyst buy the block

Major venture capital firms are already executing this playbook at scale, proving the viability of the model:

  • Thrive Capital: Josh Kushner’s firm launched Thrive Holdings to acquire local accounting firms, buying nearly 50 firms over the last 24 months and committing another $1 billion to the strategy. For example, they acquired Larson Gross, a 200-person firm founded in 1949. By implementing AI tools built on OpenAI's Codex, accountants saved an average of 31% of their time. One specific task that previously took an accountant 180 hours per year was reduced to just 15 hours. Notably, interns who once prepared tax returns were retrained to review AI-generated drafts.
  • General Catalyst: The firm has earmarked $1.5 billion for this strategy, deploying over $750 million across at least 10 platform companies. One of their portfolio companies, Long Lake, acquires property management businesses and scaled to $100 million in EBITDA in under two years while doubling profit margins. Another portfolio company, Crescendo, uses AI to resolve 90% of frontline customer support tickets.
  • The GC deal structure: When acquiring a business, General Catalyst typically pays 60% to 70% in cash, while the retiring founder rolls the remaining 30% as equity into the new entity. This structure incentivizes the original owner to ensure a smooth transition and preserve long-term client relationships.

The solo founder edge: Building a one-person holding company

While billion-dollar funds must focus on mid-market firms to move the needle, solo founders can target smaller, highly profitable businesses generating around $2 million in annual revenue. Solo founders possess several distinct advantages over institutional buyers:

  1. Access to identical tools: Solo founders have access to the same underlying LLMs (such as OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google Gemini) that power enterprise-grade agents.
  2. Personalized integration: Instead of hiring external managers to learn a business from scratch, a solo founder can personally oversee the integration, read agent drafts, and maintain direct relationships with clients.
  3. Trust with retiring owners: Retiring founders who have spent decades building a business are often highly protective of their legacy and prefer selling to an individual operator rather than a faceless private equity firm.

To scale efficiently, Isenberg recommends structuring the venture as a holding company (holdco). Under the holdco, each acquired business is run day-to-day by a general manager (GM)—ideally a trusted, senior employee promoted from within the firm and incentivized with equity upside. Underneath these businesses lies a single, shared infrastructure layer of AI agents, global rules, and dashboards. Building this layer once makes each subsequent acquisition easier and cheaper to integrate.

The operational blueprint: folders, agents, and workflows

To run an AI-native holding company systematically, Isenberg outlines a standardized organizational and technical structure.

The folder structure

  • The thesis folder: Documents the holdco's investment criteria, target industries, and acquisition rules to prevent emotional buying.
  • The shared folder: Houses the global rules, core agent files, step-by-step runbooks (e.g., day-one transition checklists), and a repository of approved historical work used to test agents after system updates.
  • Business-specific folders: Each acquired business has its own folder containing:
    • Client files: Notes on long-term clients, preferences, and key relationships.
    • People files: Documentation of institutional knowledge held by specific employees.
    • Local rules: Custom operational parameters unique to that business (e.g., "always send reports on the third of the month").
    • Corrections log: A record of every manual edit a human makes to an agent's output.

The agent pipeline

Work flows through a strict four-stage pipeline designed to eliminate errors before they reach the client:

[Intake Agent] ──> [Preparer Agent] ──> [Reviewer Agent] ──> [Human Approval] ──> [Client]
  1. Intake agent: Collects incoming client documents and automatically chases missing files.
  2. Preparer agent: Generates the first draft of the deliverable (e.g., categorizing transactions or drafting month-end closes).
  3. Reviewer agent: Evaluates the preparer's draft against global and local rules, scoring it and either passing it forward or sending it back for corrections.
  4. Human approval: A human employee reviews and approves the final output.

Crucial system rule: Neither the preparer nor the reviewer agent is ever permitted to send files directly to a client. A human must always serve as the final accountability layer.

The holdco owner's weekly rhythm

Operating a portfolio of AI-driven businesses is highly leveraged, allowing the owner to focus on optimization and growth rather than day-to-day operations:

  • Monday (Metrics): Review five core health indicators across all portfolio businesses: profit margins, human hours spent per job, agent draft error rates, client retention, and key employee satisfaction.
  • Tuesday (GMs): Hold a sync call with each business's general manager to identify operational bottlenecks and client issues.
  • Wednesday (The corrections log): Analyze the edits humans made to agent outputs. Use AI to categorize these edits (e.g., factual errors, style preferences, missing context) and write new rules to prevent those errors from recurring. This creates a compounding feedback loop.
  • Thursday & Friday (Deal flow): Meet with prospective sellers, network with business owners, and research new target industries.

Addressing the skeptics: risks, margins, and the human element

Isenberg addresses several common criticisms raised by industry observers:

  • "Roll-ups always fail": Traditional private equity roll-ups often collapse due to over-leverage, overpaying, or rushed integrations. Solo founders can mitigate this by buying slowly, pricing acquisitions based on current cash flows (not projected AI upside), and personally managing the integration.
  • "Margins will be competed away": While widespread AI adoption will eventually compress industry pricing, a multi-year window exists where early adopters can capture massive margin expansion. Furthermore, proprietary client relationships and hyper-specific rule logs are incredibly difficult for competitors to replicate.
  • "Regulated industries require human oversight": This is true, but reviewing an AI-generated draft takes a fraction of the time required to produce work from scratch. The human staff remains, but their productivity is heavily leveraged.
  • "Staff and clients will flee": To prevent disruption, change nothing during the first 30 days. Run agents silently in the background to shadow human workers, and ensure key staff members are financially incentivized to stay.
  • "This is just a fancy word for layoffs": While some underperforming roles may be eliminated, early data suggests the primary shift is qualitative: employees transition from manual data-entry workers to high-level editors and reviewers, allowing them to manage significantly larger client portfolios.

How to get started: the service-first trojan horse

For aspiring operators without millions in capital, Isenberg recommends a "service-first" approach to build trust and acquire their first business:

  1. Identify a target industry (e.g., bookkeeping) and select a single, highly repetitive bottleneck (e.g., chasing missing client documents or cleaning up messy month-end books).
  2. Build an AI agent workflow to solve this specific problem and sell it to existing firms as a specialized service.
  3. By working inside these firms, you learn their operations, refine your agents on real-world data, and build direct relationships with the owners.
  4. When an owner is eventually ready to retire, you will be the natural, trusted buyer positioned to acquire the entire firm.
