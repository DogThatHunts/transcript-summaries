The sacred right to property

Dave Champion asserts that America's Founding Fathers considered the right to own property and be secure in it an indispensable element of personal liberty. He cites figures like John Locke, Thomas Jefferson, and John Adams, who linked property rights, including the right to one's labor and its products, directly to individual freedom. The legal definition of "employee"

Champion claims that the Internal Revenue Code's definition of "employee" for payroll withholding purposes (26 U.S.C. Section 3401(c)) is narrowly defined. He states that it "includes an officer, employee, or elected official of the United States, a state, or any political subdivision thereof, or the District of Columbia, or any agency or instrumentality of any one or more of the foregoing." He further explains that the word "includes" in tax law (per 7701(c) and 27 CFR 72.11) means that the enumerated list establishes a specific class, and only items within that class are covered. Champion argues that this definition exclusively applies to government workers under federal authority (including federal territories like Guam or Puerto Rico), and cannot be legally extended to private sector employees.

The legal definition of "wages"

Similarly, Champion examines the statutory definition of "wages" for payroll withholding (26 U.S.C. Section 3401(a)), which states, "the term wages means all remuneration for services performed by an employee." Given his interpretation of "employee," Champion concludes that "wages" as defined for withholding purposes does not apply to the pay of private sector workers.

The W-4 form and consent

Champion argues that the requirement to sign a W-4 form, "under penalty of perjury," is crucial. He claims that by signing a W-4, an individual legally determines themselves to be a person upon whom Congress has imposed income tax. He further asserts that Chapter 24 of the tax code, which covers payroll withholding, is titled "Collection of Income Tax at Source" and consistently refers to collecting federal income tax owed on U.S. source income belonging to a foreign person. Therefore, Champion contends that signing a W-4 for a private sector job implies that the signer is a foreign person earning U.S. source income while employed by a government agency under federal authority, which he says is a perjurious statement for most Americans.

Employer liability and IRS guidance

Champion addresses employer concerns about liability under 26 U.S.C. 3403, which states that "the employer shall be liable for the payment of tax required to be deducted and withheld under this chapter." He argues that if a worker is not a statutorily defined "employee," then no withholding is "required," and thus the employer has no liability. He dismisses IRS online guidance that suggests withholding at single zero in the absence of a W-4, stating that IRS publications and webpages are not law and cannot be relied upon. Champion advises companies to inform the IRS that a worker declined to provide a signed W-4, claiming that such a response has historically led to the IRS ceasing inquiries.
