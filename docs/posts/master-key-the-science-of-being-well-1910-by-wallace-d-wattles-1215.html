Master Key | The Science of Being Well (1910) by Wallace D. Wattles

A classic New Thought guide outlining how aligning one's mental attitude with a universal life force and practicing simple physical habits can unlock perfect physical health.

Chapter 1: the principle of health

Wallace D. Wattles introduces the fundamental premise that health is the natural performance of bodily functions, which directly results from the action of an inherent "principle of life." He asserts that a single, intelligent living substance permeates the entire universe, and that human beings contain a localized version of this source, termed the "principle of health."

Wattles argues that this internal principle is the true agent behind all physical healing, regardless of the medical system or remedy applied. He points out that opposite medical schools—such as allopathy and homeopathy—both successfully cure diseases, suggesting that patients are healed by their own internal power rather than the specific ingredients in a prescription. He concludes that the principle of health is activated primarily by the way a patient thinks, and that healing requires both faith and a personal application of that faith.

Chapter 2: the foundations of faith

To think in a way that promotes health, Wattles states that individuals must accept several foundational New Thought truths. He explains that the original, formless substance of the universe creates physical forms and motions through thought. Human bodies are physical manifestations of these original thoughts.

Wattles divides bodily functions into two categories: * Involuntary functions: Internal processes (like circulation and cell renewal) controlled entirely by the subconscious mind and the principle of health. * Voluntary functions: Conscious actions (eating, drinking, breathing, and sleeping) under the control of the individual.

He argues that the principle of health naturally guides involuntary functions toward perfection. However, because humans possess limited knowledge, they often hold erroneous thoughts of disease, abnormality, and death. These negative mental impressions pervert the subconscious mind, leading to physical illness. Wattles asserts that nature's sole purpose is the advancement of life toward perfect health, and that individuals can overcome hereditary or environmental illness by aligning their conscious thoughts with this natural perfection.

Chapter 3: life and its organisms

Wattles argues that life is not generated by the body; rather, life is an active force that builds and maintains the body. He asserts that the universal life principle is completely wise and powerful, meaning it cannot naturally think of or produce disease. Consequently, Wattles claims that disease is entirely a product of separate human consciousness and perverted individual thought.

He notes that while many people fail to live healthily because they rely on logic rather than natural instinct, they can correct this by adopting two essential mental actions: 1. Claiming and appropriating health through absolute faith. 2. Severing all mental relations with disease to establish a complete mental unification with health.

Chapter 4: what to think

To establish a mental relationship with health, Wattles advises readers to form a clear mental conception of perfect health and imagine themselves performing daily activities with strength and vigor. He cautions against studying anatomy, physiology, or traditional hygiene, claiming that these sciences are inexact, highly theoretical, and focus too heavily on diseased states.

According to Wattles, studying sickness fills the mind with thoughts of imperfection, which the intelligent "mind-stuff" within the body then translates into physical illness. He instructs readers to instantly cast out any thoughts of disease and focus entirely on the ideal of a perfectly functioning body, trusting that the intelligent substance will rebuild the physical body cell by cell.

Chapter 5: faith

Wattles asserts that thoughts only possess creative, physical power when they are accompanied by faith. Without faith, thoughts are impotent and cannot activate the principle of health. He warns that if an individual does not actively cultivate faith in health, they will naturally default to a faith in disease, which will then dominate their physical condition.

To build faith, Wattles instructs readers to assert that they are currently healthy, rather than claiming they will get well. He advises maintaining the physical posture, walk, and speech of a healthy person. He highlights the practice of deep, reverent gratitude toward the Supreme as the most effective tool for maintaining this mental state, as gratitude closes the mind to negative thoughts and keeps the individual in close, harmonious relation with the source of life.

Chapter 6: use of the will

Wattles explains that the will should never be used to force the physical body to perform beyond its strength, nor should it be directed at external people or forces. Instead, the sole legitimate use of the will is to govern one's own attention and thoughts.

He advises readers to use their willpower to: * Choose thoughts of health and reject suggestions of sickness. * Avoid books, conversations, and environments that depict disease, deformity, or old age. * Focus attention exclusively on things that suggest strength, joy, and vitality.

Wattles maintains that there are no unfriendly forces in the universe; the single cosmic force is entirely supportive of human health, meaning individuals only need to conquer their own habits of negative thinking.

Chapter 7: health from God

Wattles defines "the Supreme" as the intelligent, permeating substance of the universe that seeks the most abundant life for all creation. He argues that when an individual desires more abundant life for themselves and others, they enter into a state of harmony with this intelligence, receiving both wisdom and physical vitality.

Referencing the psychologist William James's observations on the "second wind," Wattles argues that human energy is drawn from an inexhaustible cosmic reservoir. He claims that a sick person who maintains absolute faith and performs their voluntary functions in a natural way will receive a continuous inflow of vital energy from the Supreme, sufficient to heal any disease.

Chapter 8: summary of the mental actions

Wattles summarizes the mental requirements of his system into a single directive: form a conception of oneself in perfect health, and think only thoughts that harmonize with that conception. He emphasizes that this requires goodwill toward all, a creative rather than competitive mindset, and constant gratitude. He states that complex mental exercises, auto-suggestions, or concentrating on specific diseased organs are unnecessary and counterproductive, as the internal principle of health will automatically manage physical restoration once the mind is aligned with the universal life force.

Chapter 9: when to eat

Wattles transitions to the voluntary functions of life, starting with eating. He argues that the subconscious mind signals the body's actual need for food through the sensation of genuine hunger. He asserts that eating when there is no hunger is highly destructive to digestive power.

To ensure natural eating, Wattles advocates for the "no-breakfast plan." He claims that because the body recharges its vital energy during sleep, it is physically impossible to experience genuine hunger immediately upon waking. He argues that the desire for an early morning meal is merely a habit-driven appetite, and advises waiting until the noon hour to consume the first meal of the day, or fasting until an earned hunger arises.

Chapter 10: what to eat

Wattles dismisses contemporary dietary controversies, such as vegetarianism versus meat-eating, arguing that nature has already solved the question of what to eat. He asserts that the "all-intelligence" guides the masses of people in any given geographic zone to utilize the staple foods of that region.

He advises readers to ignore food science, proteins, and carbohydrates, and instead eat the common, plain foods of the working class in their local area (such as wheat, meats, vegetables, and fruits). He claims that brain workers do not require a different diet than manual laborers, though they do require significantly smaller quantities of food. He maintains that indigestion is never caused by plain, hearty foods, but rather by eating to gratify appetite instead of hunger.

Chapter 11: how to eat

Wattles strongly advocates for thorough mastication, referencing Horace Fletcher's theories on nutrition. He instructs readers to chew every mouthful of food until it is completely reduced to a liquid state and swallowed involuntarily.

To practice this successfully, Wattles states that individuals must: * Avoid eating while anxious, hurried, or distracted by business and domestic cares. * Center their entire attention on the taste and enjoyment of the food. * Avoid speculative thoughts about the next dish or the speed of the meal.

He argues that this method of eating ensures perfect digestion and assimilation, making it impossible for the process to result in physical failure.

Chapter 12: hunger and appetites

Wattles provides a simple rule for determining food quantity: eat only when experiencing an earned hunger, and stop eating the absolute instant that hunger begins to abate. He warns that consuming desserts or extra food purely to gratify taste after hunger is satisfied constitutes harmful excess.

Regarding liquids, Wattles advises drinking pure water only when thirsty, and stopping as soon as the thirst is quenched. While he permits occasional indulgences like weak coffee, he advises against drinking sweet beverages or consuming liquids merely to tickle the palate.

Chapter 13: in a nutshell

Wattles provides a concise review of his health philosophy, reiterating that the universe is made of a thinking, living substance that responds to human thought. He emphasizes that perfect health is achieved by combining a steadfast mental conception of health with the natural performance of the four voluntary functions: eating, drinking, breathing, and sleeping. He reminds readers to remain cheerful, praise God, and practice self-control at the table.

Chapter 14: breathing

Wattles states that healthy breathing requires keeping the physical mechanism in good condition. He notes that modern work often causes individuals to stoop, flattening the chest and curving the spine, which makes deep breathing impossible.

To correct this, Wattles rejects formal gymnastics or health exercises, arguing that they keep the mind focused on sickness. Instead, he advises readers to maintain a straight spine and an expanded chest as a matter of personal pride. He recommends a simple, natural exercise: whenever the thought occurs, straighten up, inhale slowly to maximum capacity, stretch the chest and spine, and exhale gently. He also stresses the absolute necessity of living and working in well-ventilated spaces with pure air.

Chapter 15: sleep

Wattles explains that vital power and brain energy are renewed during sleep through contact with the universal life principle. To facilitate healthy sleep, he mandates two conditions: 1. Abundant fresh air: Sleeping rooms must be thoroughly ventilated, with windows open wide to ensure a continuous circulation of outdoor air, regardless of the weather. 2. A grateful mental attitude: Individuals should go to bed with a purposeful, confident faith that their strength is being renewed, using a bedtime prayer of thanksgiving to put their internal principle of health in touch with its cosmic source.

Chapter 16: supplementary instructions

Wattles provides practical advice for common physical challenges: * Pain: He instructs readers not to resist pain, but to recognize it as a benevolent effort by the principle of health to overcome an unnatural condition. He advises mentally cooperating with the healing process and exercising gratitude during times of physical suffering. * Weakness: When feeling weak, individuals should rest and focus on the strength that is coming, rather than driving themselves beyond their limits. * Constipation: Wattles claims that those who eat in his prescribed scientific manner will fully assimilate their food, leaving very little waste. He asserts that daily bowel movements are unnecessary for light eaters, and advises readers to banish all fear of constipation from their minds, though he permits occasional warm-water colon flushes in the beginning to help overcome mental anxiety. * Exercise: He recommends getting daily physical activity through fun, recreation, or useful work (such as gardening or sports) rather than forced health exercises.

Chapter 17: a summary of the science of being well

In the final chapter, Wattles synthesizes the entire New Thought system of health. He reiterates that a single, thinking substance permeates the universe and naturally moves toward perfect health and complete life. Humans can ally themselves with this power by holding a steadfast mental image of themselves in perfect health, cultivating deep gratitude, and performing the voluntary functions of eating, drinking, breathing, and sleeping in a natural, disciplined manner. He concludes that because the principle of health is identical in all human beings, any individual who thinks and acts in this "certain way" can successfully attain perfect health.
