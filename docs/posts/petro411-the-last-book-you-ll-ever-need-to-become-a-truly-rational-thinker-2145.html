In this analysis, Petro411 critiques rational choice theory (RCT)—the dominant decision-making framework in economics and social sciences—by drawing on Barry Schwartz and Richard Schuldenfrey's book, Choose Wisely: Rationality, Ethics, and the Art of Decision-Making. Petro411 argues that relying on mathematical calculations and spreadsheets to make major life choices is a form of "mathematical theater" that fails to address the complexity of human values.

Six mistakes of modern decision-making models

Petro411 outlines six core failures of rational choice theory when applied to real-world scenarios:

  1. The specification problem: RCT assumes decision-makers already have a predefined set of options and preferences. It offers no guidance on how to generate these options or determine what we actually value in the first place.
  2. The quantification problem: The theory requires assigning precise numerical values to subjective preferences. This forces individuals to assign arbitrary numbers to incommensurable values (such as comparing academic quality to social life) that do not share a common metric.
  3. The interaction problem: RCT treats variables as isolated factors. In reality, choices interact dynamically; for instance, one family member's reluctance to visit a museum dampens the experience for everyone else in ways that cannot be pre-calculated.
  4. Radical uncertainty: Standard decision theory assumes we can assign probabilities to outcomes. Drawing on Frank Knight and Daniel Ellsberg, Petro411 highlights that real-life decisions involve radical uncertainty where calculating odds is impossible.
  5. The index problem: When we attempt to quantify success, we end up optimizing for the metric rather than the goal itself. This is evident in college rankings or historical military metrics, where gaming the index replaces genuine quality.
  6. The framing leakage problem: How a decision is framed directly alters how the outcome is experienced. For example, labeling beef as "75% lean" versus "25% fat" changes how people experience its taste, proving that the narrative frame is inseparable from the decision's quality.

The alternative: cultivating genuine judgment

Instead of attempting to maximize utility, Schwartz and Schuldenfrey advocate for a return to Aristotle's concept of the golden mean. The goal of decision-making should be to exercise genuine judgment to find options that are "good enough" (satisficing) rather than objectively "best."

To implement this approach, Petro411 highlights five essential questions to guide major life choices:

  • What kind of person am I trying to become? This shifts the focus from external rules or rankings to internal virtue and personal growth.
  • How does this choice fit into the story of my life? Humans understand their lives through narrative coherence, not quantitative spreadsheets. A good decision is one that makes sense within your broader life story.
  • How am I framing this decision, and does that frame reflect what I actually value? Framing is a tool to close down open decisions; it should align honestly with your core values.
  • What is genuinely good enough here, rather than objectively best? Seeking the absolute best choice often leads to misery, whereas seeking what is good enough brings clarity.
  • Am I letting calculation substitute for judgment? Over-relying on data and metrics causes our capacity for personal judgment to atrophy. Ultimately, we must trust our own judgment, as no spreadsheet is infallible.
