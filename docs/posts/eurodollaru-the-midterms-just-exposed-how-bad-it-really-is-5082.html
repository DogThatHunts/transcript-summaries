The election didn't change the economy

The 2024 U.S. election saw Democrats gain control of both the House and Senate, flipping seven Senate seats and winning at least 258 House seats. This was not a minor shift—it was an electoral earthquake. Yet, the financial markets reacted with almost pure indifference.

Wall Street’s indifference reveals a deep disconnect

The stock market did not crash, nor did it react meaningfully to the change in congressional control. This is because, for Wall Street, very little actually changed. The same banks opened, the same companies made the same investment decisions, and the same households faced the same rent, grocery, and job prospects.

The market’s lack of reaction is rational—but revealing. It shows how financial prices are disconnected from the lived economic reality of ordinary people.

The Real crisis: affordability, not Inflation

According to CNN’s exit poll, 88% of voters cited the cost of living as a crucial or major factor in their decision to vote. Only 1% said it played no role. This is not a narrow partisan issue—it is a widespread signal of economic distress.

People don’t believe they’re being heard because they aren’t. When officials dismiss household struggles as 'vibe sessions' or 'partisan media,' they ignore the reality that groceries are expensive because people buy them, rent is unaffordable because they pay it, and job markets are weak because they apply for dozens of jobs and hear nothing back.

The eviction cycle: a global pattern

This cycle repeats across countries: - In Britain, voters ousted the Conservatives after 14 years in power, despite claims that inflation was falling. In reality, prices had already risen to a permanently higher level. - In France and Germany, voters have shifted toward more radical parties as economic stagnation deepens. - In New Zealand, unemployment reached an 11-year high, and wage growth slowed to just 2%, despite the ruling party’s campaign on economic competence.

The pattern is consistent: governments lose support when employment weakens and incomes stagnate. Voters give a new party a chance, only to throw it out when it fails to deliver.

Why Politicians fail to fix the economy

Governments can borrow money, cut taxes, send checks, or subsidize industries. These actions can temporarily shift demand or redistribute income.

But they cannot create sustainable economic growth. Real growth requires a functioning system of credit, investment, production, and rising productivity.

A bank makes a loan based on future income expectations. A business hires workers based on projected demand. An entrepreneur takes a risk based on perceived returns.

Politicians cannot order these expectations into existence. When a manufacturer faces weak orders, rising costs, and deteriorating demand, a tax credit may help marginally—but it doesn’t transform a bad investment into a good one.

The misdiagnosis of the problem

Mainstream economists and central bankers continue to misdiagnose the crisis as inflation. Inflation is not the core issue.

The supply shock of 2020–2022 pushed prices higher, impoverishing households. But falling inflation rates don’t reverse that damage. Grocery bills don’t return to 2019 levels. Rent doesn’t reset. Insurance premiums don’t fall.

To repair the damage, the economy needs: - Strong, sustained job growth - Wages rising faster than prices - New investment and rising productivity

None of this has happened. GDP growth has been weak, employment has stagnated, and wage growth has been insufficient.

The failure of the traditional recession model

The traditional definition of a recession—a sharp drop in activity, followed by a recovery—no longer fits.

In the current economy, activity may grow slightly, unemployment may rise slightly, and GDP may remain positive—but the real problem is not a collapse.

It is a failure to recover. The economy has forgotten how to grow.

The defining feature of today’s crisis is not a dramatic downturn, but failed recovery.

The market’s conclusion and Main Street’s reality

Wall Street didn’t crash because professional investors understand the distinction: government policy does not control the economy’s fundamental machinery.

The election changed who will write spending programs, taxes, and regulations—but it did not change the economy’s capacity to generate credit, employment, productivity, or income.

The market’s indifference was perfectly rational—but it also revealed a profound disconnect.

A radical change in Congress barely moved stock prices. An affordability crisis moved millions of votes.

This contrast is the entire story.

The next political shift will be even larger

If governments continue to claim weak growth is prosperity and unaffordable prices are a perception problem, the anger will only intensify.

The next political swing will be even larger. The alternatives will become more extreme. And the danger will no longer be limited to the economy.

The stock market didn’t crash after the election because beneath the political spectacle, nothing fundamental has changed.

That’s Wall Street’s conclusion—and it’s also Main Street’s.
