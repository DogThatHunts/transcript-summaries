Former Army Ranger and trust expert Dave Champion breaks down the legal principles, key roles, and practical boundaries of non-statutory common law trusts.

Former Army Ranger and legal researcher Dave Champion joined host John Dowling to discuss the history, structure, and legal mechanics of common law trusts. Unlike statutory trusts created by state legislatures, common law trusts rely on the constitutional right of contract and exist independently of government regulation, provided they do not engage in criminal activity or heavily regulated commercial operations. Throughout the interview, Dave Champion details the specific roles within a trust—including grantors, trustees, protectors, managing agents, and unit certificate holders—while highlighting the necessity of strict ethical standards, proper separation of duties, and maintaining arm's-length relationships to ensure the entity remains legally sound.

Key insights

  • Constitutional foundation: Dave Champion emphasizes that common law trusts are rooted in the unalienable right of contract recognized by the U.S. Supreme Court, operating independently of statutory law.
  • Role separation: To avoid invalidation under legal doctrine, unit certificate holders, trustees, and protectors must maintain strict arm's-length boundaries and cannot share overlapping, conflicted duties.
  • Protection via protectors: An independent protector serves as an essential check against rogue trustees, holding the authority to demand compliance or initiate court actions to remove an unethical trustee.
  • Regulatory limits: Common law trusts are not suitable for heavily regulated industries like alcohol distilling or firearms manufacturing, where statutory oversight directly applies to the underlying activity.
  • Drafting integrity: Dave Champion warns against overpriced, boilerplate trust documents filled with irrelevant personal philosophies, which risk being struck down by equity courts.

Distinction between statutory and common law trusts

Dave Champion explains that statutory trusts are creatures of the state, meaning their existence depends entirely on legislation passed by elected officials. If a legislature repeals statutory trust laws, statutory entities cease to exist. In contrast, common law trusts derive their validity from centuries of common law tradition and the constitutional right of contract. Because common law trusts do not ask the government for permission to exist, they owe zero allegiance to state or federal regulatory schemes unless the underlying activity itself—such as distilling alcohol—falls under specific regulatory statutes.

Core roles and structural mechanics

John Dowling and Dave Champion walk through the essential positions required to structure a valid common law trust:

  • Grantor: The individual who transfers property into the trust corpus.
  • Trustee: The fiduciary holding legal title to property solely for the trust's purpose. Dave Champion notes that a trustee holds no personal rights to the assets and must adhere to strict ethical duties.
  • Unit certificate holders: Beneficiaries who hold economic interests in the trust corpus but cannot participate in management or hold conflicting roles.
  • Protector: An optional, neutral third party empowered to oversee trustee conduct and pursue legal remedies if the trustee breaches fiduciary responsibilities.
  • Managing agents: Individuals contracted to oversee day-to-day operations or specific business tasks without usurping trustee authority.

Legal jurisdiction and court challenges

When disputes arise, Dave Champion notes that standard equity courts retain jurisdiction to enforce fiduciary obligations. While common law is unyielding and rigid, modern equity jurisdiction offers flexibility in resolving disputes. If a trustee acts in bad faith, an independent protector can intervene or petition the court to issue a cease-and-desist order or remove the trustee. Dave Champion stresses that courts treat trustee fiduciary standards nearly identically whether dealing with statutory or common law entities.

Selecting trust experts and avoiding fraud

Addressing common pitfalls, Dave Champion warns listeners about scam artists and unqualified advisors who charge exorbitant fees—ranging up to $10,000—for boilerplate documents filled with irrelevant personal or religious rhetoric. He estimates reasonable creation costs range between $4,000 and $6,000, with annual trustee fees generally staying below $1,000. Dave Champion underscores that anyone seeking to establish a common law trust must perform thorough due diligence and select independent, highly principled officers to ensure long-term legal sustainability.
