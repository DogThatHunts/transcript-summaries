Master Key: the Law of Success, Book 8 of 8 (1928)

Lesson 14: Failure

Core reframing: Hill distinguishes between failure and temporary defeat. He argues that what the world calls "failure" is often a blessing in disguise—a redirection of energy toward more advantageous paths. Strength grows out of resistance; character is built through reverses and setbacks.

Hill's seven turning points: Over 30 years, Hill experienced seven major defeats:

  1. First turning point (mining/stenography): Lost his position when his employer lost his fortune. Though beyond his control, Hill later recognized this as a necessary nudge.

  2. Second turning point (lumber business): Achieved rapid success and partnership, but the 1907 panic destroyed his business overnight. He lost everything but was redirected toward law.

  3. Third turning point (law school and automobiles): Entered law school while selling automobiles. Built a profitable educational department training mechanics, but a banker took over the business when Hill became indebted. This forced him out of a business that offered no self-knowledge.

  4. Fourth turning point (legal position): Secured a comfortable position as assistant counsel for a coal company through family connections. Recognizing the danger of inertia and complacency, Hill voluntarily resigned—the first turning point he chose himself.

  5. Fifth turning point (candy business): Became president of the Betsy Ross Candy Company, which expanded to eighteen cities. Business associates conspired to steal his interest through false arrest. Though vindicated and awarded damages, Hill chose forgiveness over revenge, discovering that revenge was not part of his nature.

  6. Sixth turning point (teaching): Turned to teaching advertising and salesmanship. The school prospered until the military draft depleted his student body, costing him over $75,000 in tuition fees.

  7. Seventh turning point (publishing): On Armistice Day 1918, Hill sat at his typewriter and wrote a document about the golden rule and fair dealing. This essay became the foundation for Hill's Golden Rule magazine, which brought him into contact with progressive thinkers nationwide and ultimately led to the creation of this course.

Key insight: Each defeat taught Hill a needed lesson. The collective effect of these seven turning points—not any single one—shaped his philosophy and prepared him for his life's work. He argues that failure is nature's crucible, burning away dross and purifying character.

The golden rule in action: Hill includes a story of a lawyer who, overhearing an elderly man's prayer for mercy before losing his home, chose to drop a lawsuit rather than enforce it. This incident laid the foundation for Lesson 16 on the golden rule.

Lesson 15: Tolerance

The problem: Intolerance is a form of ignorance that blocks success. It is the chief cause of wars, disintegrates organized society, and fragments organized religion into warring sects.

Hill's personal awakening: Hill describes meeting a young man wearing a Knights of Columbus pin. Instinctively, he released the man's hand—a spontaneous act of intolerance that shocked Hill himself. This incident prompted him to undertake systematic research into the roots of intolerance, leading him to study religion, biology, Darwin's evolution, and psychology.

The discovery of social heredity: Hill's research revealed that most of our beliefs—on religion, politics, economics—are not reasoned conclusions but products of early childhood training and social heredity. We inherit our worldview from our environment and those who raised us, often without examining it critically. A prominent clergyman once said: "Give me control of the child until it is twelve years old, and you can teach it any religion you please after that time."

Historical examples of social heredity in action:

  • Germany: Under Adalbert Falk and Kaiser William II, the ideal of Kultur was systematically implanted in German youth through schools, creating a generation willing to sacrifice for the nation.
  • Japan: Similarly uses social heredity to cultivate national ideals of subordination of individual rights.
  • Russia: The Soviet government orders 20 million books for children to shape the next generation's ideology.
  • United States: Americans have cultivated an ideal of wealth accumulation, making financial success the measure of worth.

The plan to abolish war: Hill proposes that the churches, schools, and press of the world could establish universal peace as an international ideal within one generation if they coordinated their efforts. The remedy lies in using social heredity—the same tool used to prepare people for war—to prepare them for peace. He envisions a world conference at the U.S. Capitol, with religious and educational leaders broadcasting a commitment to peace globally.

Application to business: Hill demonstrates how the principle of social heredity can be applied to business. A banker could cultivate children from birth, ensuring their loyalty as future customers. A business school owner could cultivate students from fifth grade onward. A preacher could equip a church basement as a children's playroom, raising future parishioners.

The root cause: Intolerance stems from ignorance about our own origins—we don't realize how much of what we believe was implanted in us before age fifteen. Once we understand this, we can examine our prejudices and biases and choose to adopt more enlightened views.

Tolerance and cooperation: The World War demonstrated that when men of different races, creeds, and nations work together under unified command (General Foch), they can achieve unprecedented power. The same principle applies to peace: cooperation and tolerance are the foundation of enduring success.

Lesson 16: the Golden Rule

The apex of the course: This lesson is the "governor" that prevents misuse of the power developed in the preceding fifteen lessons. It is based on an eternal law: Whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap.

The law beneath the rule: The golden rule is not merely ethical advice; it operates through a universal law of compensation. When you act toward others, you set in motion a power that returns to help or hinder you. More importantly, every thought and act you release modifies your own character through your subconscious mind. You cannot injure another without injuring yourself; you cannot help another without helping yourself.

The mechanism: Through autosuggestion, your acts and thoughts toward others become impressed on your subconscious mind, building your character in exact duplicate of those thoughts and acts. Your character then becomes a magnet that attracts people and conditions harmonizing with it. Thus, kindness begets kindness; unkindness begets unkindness—not always directly from the recipient, but through the law of attraction operating on your character.

Two stories illustrating the golden rule:

  1. The lawyer and the prayer: A lawyer is about to foreclose on an elderly couple's home when he overhears the old man's prayer for mercy. Moved by the prayer and remembering his own mother, the lawyer drops the case. The story shows that the golden rule operates through emotion and conscience, not mere calculation.

  2. The judge and the song: In a San Francisco police court, a former opera singer sings a hymn from his cell. The song so moves the judge and the prisoners that the judge dismisses all cases without fines or sentences. The power of the golden rule, expressed through beauty and emotion, accomplishes what punishment could not.

Honesty and the golden rule: The course has not emphasized honesty as a separate principle because the golden rule, when rightly understood and applied, makes dishonesty impossible. It also makes impossible selfishness, greed, envy, bigotry, hatred, and malice.

Application to labor and capital: Hill applies the golden rule to the conflict between employers and employees. Both parties see only their own interests and believe the other's gain is their loss. But if each party would put itself in the other's position—if the capitalist would imagine being a laborer, and the laborer would imagine being a capitalist—they would understand that their interests are mutual and interdependent.

The mutual dependence of labor and capital: Capital cannot function without labor; labor cannot achieve its full potential without capital's organization and resources. Yet both have been governed by selfishness rather than the golden rule. The solution is not legislation or profit-sharing schemes alone, but a fundamental shift in consciousness: both parties must recognize their common humanity and common interest.

A vision of cooperation: Hill quotes extensively from a great philosopher (likely Henry George or a similar thinker) describing how capital and labor are essential to each other. The laborer benefits from railways, steamships, and factories built by capital; the capitalist depends entirely on labor for any value. When both recognize this interdependence and apply the golden rule, conflict ceases and cooperation flourishes.

The power of thought: Man alone has the power to transform thought into physical reality. Through the evolutionary power of thought, the hopes of one generation become the reality of the next. This power has given man dominion over all other creatures and must be used to attain success—whatever form success takes for the individual.

A code of ethics: Hill provides a twelve-point code for those who wish to follow the golden rule literally:

  1. Never do to another what you would not wish done to you if positions were reversed.
  2. Be honest in all transactions, to impress honesty on your own subconscious mind.
  3. Forgive those unjust toward you, understanding that forgiveness strengthens your character.
  4. Be just, generous, and fair always, knowing your character is the sum of your acts.
  5. Devote time to correcting your own faults rather than exposing others'.
  6. Slander no one, lest you plant destructive suggestions in your own mind.
  7. Recognize thought as an inlet from the universal ocean of life; set no destructive thoughts afloat.
  8. Conquer hatred, envy, selfishness, jealousy, malice, pessimism, doubt, and fear.
  9. Fill your mind with courage, self-confidence, goodwill, faith, kindness, loyalty, love, truth, and justice.
  10. Understand that passive belief in the golden rule is worthless; apply it actively in all transactions.
  11. Guard carefully all that goes into your character development.
  12. Help others find happiness, knowing that kindness is always rewarded, even if indirectly.

The law of compensation: Hill emphasizes that the law of compensation is not a theory but an immutable law of nature. Those who render service to others without direct reciprocation are not wasting their effort; they are building character that attracts reciprocation from unexpected sources. The law operates through the principle of magnetic attraction: your character attracts those and conditions that harmonize with it.

The final word: Success in its highest form brings one to view all human relationships with deep emotion and a sense of the sacred. No man can attain true success without earnest prayer—not as a means of solving daily problems, but as a recognition of forces beyond the individual will. The golden rule, when understood and applied, makes one's life a blessing to all who encounter it.

Closing: decision and Procrastination

Hill concludes with a meditation on decision and procrastination. Indecision is the devil's favorite tool. The great leaders of history—Grant, Napoleon, Carnegie, Ford, Edison—were men and women of quick, firm decision. Procrastination robs opportunity; delay means defeat because no man can recover lost time.

Life is a checkerboard; time is the opponent. If you hesitate, you will be wiped off the board. If you keep moving, you may win. The only real capital is time, but only when used.

Hill closes with inspirational quotes emphasizing kindness, character, purpose, and the power of the individual to shape their destiny through thought and action.
