Master Key | The Law of Success, Book 8 of 8 (1928) by Napoleon Hill - "Failure, Tolerance, & The Golden Rule"

Hill's final three lessons: defeat as a teacher drawn from his seven turning points, intolerance as childhood conditioning, and the Golden Rule as a law that works through auto-suggestion.

Book 8 of 8 of Napoleon Hill's The Law of Success (1928), read by Dan John Miller for the Master Key Society. The ideas are Hill's, reported as he states them, not endorsed or verified here. Every timestamp opens the video a few seconds early.

The final book holds the course's last three lessons: Lesson 14, Failure; Lesson 15, Tolerance; and Lesson 16, The Golden Rule. Hill calls Failure "my favorite lesson of this course", says Tolerance may be "the most important lesson of the entire sixteen", and presents the Golden Rule as "the apex of the pyramid", the lesson that governs how the power built up in the earlier lessons is used. The book closes with an after-the-lesson visit on indecision and the inspirational quotations from the printed volume.

The lessons share one argument. Defeat is a teacher, not failure, and Hill's seven "turning points" are offered as proof that the whole philosophy grew out of reverses. Intolerance is ignorance planted in childhood by "social heredity", the same mechanism that trains nations for war and that could instead teach peace or build a business. The Golden Rule is the expression of a law: every act and thought toward others is registered in one's own subconscious mind through auto-suggestion and comes back through the law of compensation.

Lesson 14: Failure [00:43]

Hill begins by separating failure from "temporary defeat", which is usually "a blessing in disguise" because it "brings us up with a jerk and redirects our energies along different and more desirable lines" [01:10]. Recalling Lesson 9, he holds that strength grows out of resistance and that sound character is "usually the handiwork of reverses and setbacks" [01:39]. Defeat "talks to us in a dumb language", and the lesson's object is to teach it [02:21]. Over about thirty years he came seven times to a point "which the uninformed call failure", each time "a kindly unseen hand" redirecting him [02:53].

The first four turning points [03:44]

A stenographer and bookkeeper at a mining concern, Hill rose by "performing more work and better work than that for which I was paid" to general manager of the mines, until his employer lost his fortune [03:46]. As sales manager and then partner in a Southern lumber firm, he still could not conceive of success measured "in terms other than money and authority" [06:22], and the 1907 panic "rendered me an enduring service by destroying my business" [07:52]. He studied law at night, sold cars by day, and ran a school training auto mechanics until his banker lent him into debt and "took over my business as calmly as if it had belonged to him, which it did" [08:53]. He now calls that "probably the greatest single blessing that ever came my way" [10:57].

The fourth turning point was his own choice. "Because I was my wife's husband, and her people had influence", he became assistant to the chief counsel of a great coal company, then resigned because he saw himself "drifting into the habit of inertia": "strength and growth come only through continuous effort and struggle" [12:28]. Acting on "a hunch", he went to Chicago to test his "sterner qualities" [15:35], and he urges the student never to accept a defeat as failure "until you shall have had time to analyze the final result" [16:46]. There, as advertising manager of a correspondence school, he earned $5,200 the first year and learned that "self-approval is a dangerous state of mind" [18:02].

The fifth turning point: the Betsy Ross Candy Company [18:46]

With the school's president he founded the Betsy Ross Candy Company, with stores in eighteen cities. His associate and a third man had him "arrested on a false charge" to force him to surrender his interest [19:49]. Hill was vindicated and won a tort judgment that under Illinois law would have let him jail both men [20:53]. He let them go free, and "there was laid in my heart the foundation upon which the sixteenth lesson of this course is built" [21:51]. Time punished them anyway, one in the penitentiary and the other reduced to pauperism: "We can circumvent the laws which men place upon statute-books, but the law of compensation never" [22:34]. He learned "that revenge was not a part of my makeup", and each defeat supplied material for the course [23:35].

The sixth and seventh turning points [24:53]

Teaching advertising and salesmanship at a Middle Western college, with a correspondence school reaching most English-speaking countries, he lost his students to the second military draft and "charged off more than $75,000 in tuition fees" [25:59]. With Edward Bok he calls poverty "the richest experience that can come to a man" [26:25]. The six turning points meant nothing singly; together they prepared the seventh, and his defeats came because he had not found the work into which he could "throw my heart and soul" [26:49].

On Armistice Day, November 11, 1918, he wrote at his typewriter, without planning, the editorial that financed a national magazine [29:06], reproduced as "A Personal Visit with Your Editor" [31:14]. It foresees an idealism based on the Golden Rule [32:13] and recalls Hill's first job, at a dollar a day in the Virginia coal fields. A union organizer persuaded the miners not to strike but to ask the owner to share any added profits and then to work for thirty days as partners; every man got a twenty percent bonus in a red envelope marked for "the work which you did that you were not paid to do" [34:51]. Hill credits that principle with his rise from laborer to assistant chief counsel [37:47]. George B. Williams of Chicago then made Hill's Golden Rule magazine possible [41:24].

The magazine led to a 1920 speaking tour [43:37]. In a Dallas restaurant, watching rain run down a window like "a great ladder of water", Hill outlined the fifteen points of "The Magic Ladder to Success", and the course is "but the sum total of that which I gathered through these failures" [44:26]. Its income, sold at a popular price on "the Ford philosophy", is all he needs, and his long poverty was "very largely a matter of choice" [46:15].

What defeat teaches [48:05]

The dumb language of defeat is "the plainest and most effective language in the world" [48:11]. "Defeat is a destructive force only when it is accepted as failure." Enemies are so useful that "if I had none I would feel it my duty to create a few" [48:42]. After poems by Joaquin Miller [49:27] and Angela Morgan ("When Nature Wants a Man") [50:42], Hill declares that "failure is nature's plan", "nature's great crucible" that burns the dross from the heart, and that great men's success ran in "almost exact ratio" to their obstacles [54:53]. He rejects Shakespeare's "tide in the affairs of men": fear and the admission of failure bind us "in shallows and in miseries", and we can throw them off [58:11]. Socrates, Columbus in chains, Thomas Paine awaiting the guillotine and "the man of Galilee" were "failures all", and "on such failures rest the hopes of the world" [1:00:08]. "No one has the right to brand you as a failure, except yourself" [1:02:15]. He closes with Croesus on the wheel of fortune, Lincoln's "this too will soon pass" and Walter Malone's "Opportunity" [1:02:40].

Lesson 15: Tolerance [1:05:39]

Intolerance is "a form of ignorance which must be mastered before any form of enduring success may be attained", "the chief cause of all wars", and "the chief disintegrating force in the organized religions of the world" [1:05:47].

The Knights of Columbus pin [1:07:33]

Introduced to a fine young man, Hill saw a Knights of Columbus pin on his vest and "instantly I released his hand as if it were a piece of ice", then looked at his own Masonic pin and wondered how "a couple of trinkets" could divide strangers [1:08:56]. The shock sent him into Catholicism and Protestantism, which "differ more in form than they do in effect" [1:10:36], then biology, Darwin and psychology, until he had to "unlearn much" [1:11:03]. The key was the difference between physical and social heredity [1:12:21]. Senator Robert L. Taylor once asked why Hill was a Democrat; "because my father was one", Hill said, and Taylor asked how he would fare "if your father had been a horse thief" [1:12:59]. We are largely "the result of the training you received before you reached the age of fifteen years" [1:14:22].

How to abolish war [1:14:35]

Hill reproduces the plan he sent to Edward Bok's American Peace Award, as an application of organized effort [1:14:35]. Physical heredity fixes the body; social heredity, from environment and early training, forms our religion, politics, economics and views of war [1:15:38]. "The Catholic is a Catholic, and the Protestant is a Protestant, because he cannot help it", adult religion being set between four and fourteen, and beliefs absorbed "under highly emotionalized conditions" root deepest [1:16:36]. Schools, churches and the press can, within one generation, force an ideal "upon the minds of the young so effectively that they cannot resist it" [1:18:36]. Germany's soldiers were made by "Kultur" taught in the schools under Adalbert Falk and William II [1:19:47]; Japan used the same method and sought to control China's schools [1:21:55]; Soviet Russia was ordering 20 million children's books [1:23:51]; war statues and Stone Mountain glorify war [1:25:56]. The American ideal is "the desire for wealth" [1:27:35].

The plan: war ends "only by education" subordinating national interests to those of the human race [1:30:00], by having churches and schools so teach the young "that the very word war will strike terror in their hearts" [1:32:09]. Hill stages it as a play at the Capitol, with the President and Congress, clergy, educators, the press and radio, titled "Thou shalt not kill" and aimed at a world court [1:33:34], set in motion by a Golden Rule Society [1:34:46]. The appeal must be "to the emotional side of humanity", not reason [1:36:01]; if churches, schools and press will not join, new ones must be created [1:37:27]. The church, resting "solely upon the power of emotion", would gain power by it [1:41:23], and the task falls on its women, for in Schopenhauer's words "the race is always to her more than the individual" [1:42:15]. "Those who do not want peace are the ones who profit by war" [1:44:04].

Social heredity in business [1:44:47]

A banker should greet every newborn, send birthday storybooks on saving and run a children's playroom; business schools, stores, preachers, barbers and restaurants can do the same [1:45:01]. "Attract the children and you attract the parents"; if nations can build soldiers to order, "businessmen can build customers to order through the same principle" [1:49:16].

Organized effort and tolerance [1:49:28]

The Allied armies turned the tide under General Foch, and they were "the most cosmopolitan group of soldiers ever assembled" [1:50:06]. If they could set aside intolerance to fight, Hill asks, why not cities, churches and employers for better business ethics [1:51:18]? "Cooperation and tolerance are the very foundation of enduring success" [1:52:44]. The manuscript had already moved his proofreader, a business college owner and an Atlanta Golden Rule Club to act [1:55:03], and the aim is "to educate more than it is to inform" [1:57:18]. His closing essay hopes to find beyond the grave "only human souls, brothers and sisters all, unmarked by race, creed, or color" [1:59:11].

Lesson 16: The Golden Rule [1:59:52]

The earlier lessons hold "more power... than most men could trust themselves with", and this lesson is "a governor" against its misuse [2:00:13]. Its law "both equals and transcends in importance every other law" of the course [2:01:18]. The only proof is the student's own test; for authority Hill names Christ, Plato, Socrates, Epictetus, Confucius, Emerson, James and Münsterberg [2:02:54].

The law behind the rule [2:03:31]

Men have preached the Golden Rule for four thousand years without grasping its law. It means treating others as you would wish to be treated "if your positions were reversed", because of "an eternal law through the operation of which we reap that which we sow" [2:04:14]. Lesson 11 on accurate thought explains it [2:05:14]. The law returns "the results of every thought that you release", so one must "think of others as you wish them to think of you" [2:05:46]; understand it "and you understand all that the Bible has to unfold to you" [2:06:34].

The lawyer, the prayer and the singing prisoner [2:07:48]

A lawyer drops a foreclosure after overhearing an old man pray beside his sick wife, and the creditor gives up the claim [2:07:55]. Hill holds that "no man can attain success in its highest form without the aid of earnest prayer", "the key" to the "secret doorway" of Lesson 11, while only suggesting it be tried "after everything else fails" [2:13:03]. In a San Francisco police court a jailed opera singer sings "The Holy City", the prisoners weep, and the judge dismisses them all [2:13:59].

An active rule, and honesty [2:16:37]

A passive belief in the rule brings nothing, and using it "as a cloak" fails because "human character does evermore publish itself" [2:16:49]. Hill quotes Emerson [2:17:51], Thomas Troward on the creative power of thought, through which "cooperation will take the place of competition" [2:20:14], and George D. Herron: "we are hourly choosing between brotherhood in suffering and brotherhood in good" [2:23:47]. The luncheon clubs and the League of Nations show the rule spreading [2:25:43], and rejecting it in business is now "the equivalent of economic suicide" [2:28:10]. Honesty was never listed as a prerequisite because the Golden Rule "makes dishonesty impossible" [2:28:28]; honesty earns honor when it costs a material loss [2:29:24].

The rule works through auto-suggestion [2:31:02]

"The law through which the Golden Rule philosophy operates is none other than the law through which the principle of autosuggestion operates" [2:31:02]. Every act and thought toward others is registered in one's own subconscious mind, building character "in exact duplicate"; "whatever we do unto others we do unto ourselves" [2:32:07]. Character is "a sort of center of magnetic attraction", so one cannot afford to hate, envy or strike back [2:32:39]. When an enemy did Hill "the most severe injustice" just as he began the lesson, he declined civil and criminal libel actions and forgave, because "no man can strike back at another without cost" [2:34:18].

Character and retaliation [2:41:15]

Unreturned service still charges one's character, "which may be likened to the charging of an electric battery", and attracts repayment from other sources [2:42:00]. "Your character is but the sum total of your thoughts and deeds" [2:43:57]; Lesson 3's formula and Lesson 8's self-control serve this [2:44:48]. "An eye for an eye" is the same law in negative form, and Confucius stated the rule negatively [2:45:46]. Kindness yields "compound interest on compound interest" [2:49:04], and "your reputation is made by others, but your character is made by you" [2:49:28].

Capital and labor [2:51:15]

Capital and labor succeed "in exact ratio" to their use of the Golden Rule [2:51:57]. Hill quotes a long essay by "one of the great men of this generation", unnamed in the reading, [2:52:55]: the two are mutually dependent [2:54:55]; the laborer is served by a world-wide army bringing his dinner and his newspaper [3:01:19]; conflict comes because "each party seeks to get the largest returns for the least service" [3:07:55]; and the cure is "put yourself in his place" [3:16:54]. "If labor is the arm, capital is the blood" [3:22:48].

Thought, inventory and indecision [3:23:17]

"Thought is the only power that can systematically organize" to a plan, and "whatever man believes he can do, he eventually does" [3:23:56]. The student should take inventory against the fifteen factors and may send in a free Personal Analysis Questionnaire [3:26:12]. The closing visit warns that "procrastination robs you of opportunity" in a game of checkers against time [3:28:44], citing Grant, Napoleon, Carnegie, James J.
