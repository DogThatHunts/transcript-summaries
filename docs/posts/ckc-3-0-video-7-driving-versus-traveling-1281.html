Summary of driving versus traveling

In this presentation, Brandon Joe Williams discusses his theories regarding the legal distinctions between "driving" and "traveling," the nature of state and federal jurisdiction, and strategies for challenging the authority of the Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) and law enforcement.

The three pillars of state citizenship and jurisdiction

Williams argues that understanding the distinction between driving and traveling requires grasping three fundamental concepts of jurisdiction:

  • State citizenship and the Ninth Amendment: Williams claims that individuals who qualify as "white citizens of a state" are fully protected by the Ninth and Tenth Amendments, which he argues grant an unenumerated right to travel non-commercially without government interference.
  • The Commerce Clause limitation: For state citizens, Williams asserts that the federal government can only assert jurisdiction over their road travel through the Commerce Clause, which he claims is strictly limited to interstate commerce (transporting goods or people across state lines for hire).
  • Fourteenth Amendment citizenship: In contrast, Williams argues that "U.S. citizens" (whom he associates with the Fourteenth Amendment) are subject to the plenary power of the federal government. He references 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(39) to argue that U.S. citizens are legally treated as residents of the District of Columbia for jurisdictional purposes, meaning they cannot escape federal oversight regardless of where they reside.

Williams defines "traveling" as non-commercial movement and "driving" as commercial transport. He contends that a state citizen has the right to drive commercially within their own state's borders without a state-issued driver's license, as long as the activity does not cross state lines to trigger the federal Commerce Clause.

The driver's license as a rebuttable presumption of residency

According to Williams, obtaining a driver's license is a contractual agreement that establishes a "rebuttable presumption" of residency in that state. He points to California Vehicle Code Section 4000(h) as an example of how possessing a license legally presumes state residency.

Williams strongly opposes holding a driver's license, characterizing it as a betrayal of American sovereignty (which he personifies through the historical figure of "Columbia"). He claims that because state citizens are not legal residents of the federalized state structures, they do not qualify for driver's licenses and would have to commit perjury to obtain them. Instead, he advocates for formally surrendering driver's licenses and obtaining certified proof of voluntary cancellation from the state.

Private motor carrier DOT numbers as a legal strategy

To navigate the roads without a standard driver's license, Williams suggests registering for a free "private motor carrier" Department of Transportation (DOT) number through the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA).

He distinguishes between two types of carriers:

  • Common carriers: Entities that hold themselves out to transport any passengers or goods for hire (which Williams compares to a public taxi service).
  • Private carriers: Selective entities that transport goods or people in specific instances.

While admitting that both terms are technically commercial, Williams argues that a private DOT registration removes the automatic presumption of commercial activity. If stopped by law enforcement, he claims the burden of proof shifts to the officer and prosecutor to prove that the individual was operating in a commercial capacity at that exact moment. He advises anyone applying for a private DOT number to use burner phone numbers and P.O. boxes to avoid having their personal information sold to marketing agencies.

The power of discovery in traffic litigation

Williams criticizes the sovereign citizen community's focus on obtaining quick case dismissals. He argues that a dismissal does not resolve the underlying jurisdictional dispute and leaves the individual vulnerable to future stops.

Instead, Williams advocates for aggressively pushing traffic cases into the discovery phase of litigation. He suggests that litigants use discovery to force the state and the DMV to answer complex questions regarding:

  • The true legal and beneficial ownership of registered vehicles.
  • Whether the vehicle registration process establishes a constructive trust.
  • The commercial status of the state itself, which Williams notes is listed as a business on Dun & Bradstreet and possesses an Employer Identification Number (EIN).

Williams claims that government agencies and prosecutors will do everything they can to avoid answering these questions in discovery, giving the litigant the upper hand.

The ultimate goal: dismantling the Fourteenth Amendment

To inspire his followers, Williams quotes historical statesman Daniel Webster on the duty to defend the Constitution regardless of personal consequences. Williams states that his primary research focus is not merely winning traffic disputes, but finding the legal mechanisms to challenge and ultimately dismantle the Fourteenth Amendment. He argues that the repeal of the Fourteenth Amendment would collapse the modern federal administrative state, including the DMV, the FBI, the federal reserve note system, and public school systems, returning the country to a decentralized, pre-1868 legal framework.
