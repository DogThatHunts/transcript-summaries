The First Amended Complaint: structure and arguments

The defendant is the City of Glendale, a 14th Amendment citizen corporation.

Background: Negotiable instruments and monetary systems

The complaint provides extensive background on:

  • Lawful money vs. legal tender: Lawful money is defined as gold and silver coins (per Article I, Section 10 of the U.S. Constitution). Legal tender is defined as Federal Reserve notes and other negotiable instruments authorized by Congress.
  • The shift to fiat currency: Executive Order 6102 (1933) eliminated gold coin holdings; the Coinage Act of 1965 eliminated silver coins; President Nixon closed the gold window in 1971, ending convertibility to gold.
  • Negotiable instruments under the CCC: Federal Reserve notes and municipal utility bills are negotiable instruments governed by California Commercial Code Division 3, which establishes presumptions of payment and rules for acceptance, dishonor, and discharge.
  • Citizenship and currency: 14th Amendment citizens are tied to the District of Columbia and must use legal tender (Federal Reserve notes). State citizens are tied to Article I, Section 10 and may use gold and silver coins (though this is now largely theoretical).

Causes of action

The complaint brings multiple causes of action, each tailored to one or both plaintiffs:

  1. Breach of CCC 3-series obligations (sole proprietorship): For failure to properly accept, dishonor, or discharge three separate bills (dated 8-18-2025, 10-15-2025, and 12-16-2025) tendered as payment. Each cycle is treated as independent, with its own presumptions of payment.

  2. Violation of California Constitution of 1879, Article I, Sections 1, 6, 7, 13, 2, 4, 11 (sole proprietorship): Alleging deprivation of life, liberty, property, freedom from slavery, due process, and security in persons and property.

  3. Public Utility Code § 2106 violation (sole proprietorship): Alleging willful and unlawful disconnection of service despite timely payment, with request for exemplary damages.

  4. Proposition 218 violation (sole proprietorship): Alleging double collection of fees (once via draft, once via forced credit card payment) without proper notice or voter approval.

  5. Violations of California Constitution of 1849, Article I, Sections 1, 2, 11, 18, 19, 21 (white Californian): Alleging deprivation of inalienable rights, freedom from slavery, security in property, and violation of unenumerated rights (Ninth Amendment equivalent).

Addressing the demurrer

Williams explicitly addresses each ground of the demurrer:

  • Res judicata: By splitting the plaintiff and treating each billing cycle as independent, he argues that res judicata does not apply across cycles. He cites CCC § 3-603(b) and cases holding that a breach in one payment cycle does not preclude a separate breach in another.
  • Uncertainty: He provides extensive background on negotiable instruments, citizenship, and constitutional law, and invites the court to identify specific sections that are unclear so he can provide additional information.
  • Failure to establish duty: He explicitly invokes CCC § 1-305, which he argues creates a cause of action for any breach of the CCC, and cites Five-Star Bank Cases for the proposition that statutory damages or minimum recoveries can be awarded even without traditional injury.
