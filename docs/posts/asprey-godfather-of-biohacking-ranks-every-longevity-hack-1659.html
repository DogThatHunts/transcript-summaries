Dave Asprey ranks 60 longevity hacks from creatine to stem cells, revealing which biohacks deliver real results and which are expensive theater.

Asprey ranks every longevity hack from worst to best

Overview

Dave Asprey systematically ranks approximately 60 biohacking interventions—from supplements and pharmaceuticals to devices and procedures—using a tiered system (S, A, B, C, D, F) based on evidence quality, safety, cost, and real-world effect size. His core criterion: replicated human trials, measurable effect size, safety, and affordability define S-tier; mouse studies with no human data define F-tier. Asprey emphasizes that price often signals the absence of evidence: proven interventions get copied and commoditized (cheap), while unproven ones command premium prices to justify their cost.

Key insights

  1. The price paradox: Expensive biohacks (NAD+ IVs, exosomes, young plasma) typically lack human longevity trials. If something works, it gets copied until it's cheap. High price often indicates the opposite of efficacy.

  2. Only three interventions reached S-tier: blackout curtains (sleep), blood flow restriction training (movement), and creatine (the only supplement to make S-tier). Asprey notes he has spent $2 million and 25 years searching for something better than these "boring" fundamentals.

  3. Relationships outrank all measured interventions: A meta-analysis of 148 studies (300,000 people) found strong social connection matched smoking's mortality risk reduction and beat obesity and inactivity. The Harvard longitudinal study (since 1938) showed relationship satisfaction at 50 predicted physical health at 80 better than cholesterol levels—yet it cannot be sold, so it never appears in biohacking rankings.

  4. Replication failure is common: NMN (NAD precursor), rapamycin, and berberine all show impressive animal or preliminary human data but fail to replicate or miss primary endpoints in larger trials. Asprey argues this is "the whole story of this field in one compound."

  5. Honest conflicts of interest matter: Asprey discloses ownership stakes in creatine, C8-MCT oil, methylene blue, nicotine, blue light glasses, and 40 Years of Zen neurofeedback, then ranks them on evidence anyway—placing his own neurofeedback program at D-tier for consumer versions while defending the clinical 5-day intensive.

Supplements and pills

S-tier

  • Creatine (10 cents/scoop): 22 randomized controlled trials in older adults show real lean mass gains. Asprey emphasizes it as an energy compound for the brain (which burns 20% of body energy despite being 2% of body weight), not just muscles. Cognitive effects strongest under sleep deprivation, stress, or in vegetarians with depleted stores.

A-tier

  • Magnesium glycinate (~30 cents/day): Reduces sleep latency by ~17 minutes in human studies. Critical distinction: most drugstore magnesium oxide is poorly absorbed and acts as a laxative. Glycinate form is safe, cheap, and addresses sleep—"the thing that fixes everything else."
  • Omega-3 (1g/day): Only supplement with positive randomized trial on biological aging clock. 777 people over 70 for three years showed epigenetic aging slowed by up to four months. Caveat: most capsules contain only ~300mg of active EPA/DHA despite 1000mg label claim.
  • C8-MCT oil (20-year user): Six-month trial raised brain ketone metabolism 230% in mild cognitive impairment. Aging brains lose glucose-burning ability but retain ketone metabolism. Limits: ketones peak in ~3 hours; ~33% of trial participants experienced stomach issues.

B-tier

  • Modafinil (prescription): Real effects on planning and complex decision-making, but essentially nothing for memory or attention (the reasons most people take it). Asprey revised his claimed 50% brain boost down to 10% with better tracking. Critical undisclosed risk: induces liver enzyme that clears hormonal birth control, potentially rendering the pill ineffective.
  • Apigenin (400mg nightly): Binds the same receptor as Valium ~400 times more weakly, providing calm without knockout. 12 randomized trials on sleep quality hold up, but none used sleep lab (polysomnography) data. Asprey notes most products sell 50mg (capsule sizing) rather than the studied dose.
  • Ashwagandha (standardized extract, proper dose): Cortisol drops ~28% in replicated trials. Real data on stress and sleep, but most shelf products are underdosed root powder. Requires 5 randomized trials (400 people) to qualify for B-tier; lacks polysomnography.
  • Urolithin A (from pomegranates): Triggers mitophagy (cells clearing broken mitochondria). Muscle strength improved ~12%, but missed ATP and VO2max endpoints the company set. 30–40% of people lack gut bacteria to produce it from food, supporting supplementation argument.
  • Nicotine (pouch/patch/lozenge, not cigarettes): Small, real, short-lived effects on attention and working memory. Genuinely addictive and raises blood pressure—a real cost for an anti-aging list. Asprey discloses investor stake.
  • Hyperbaric oxygen (B-tier for potential): Headline study showed 37% telomere lengthening and senescent cell reduction, but no control group, only 25 people, and result spread wider than effect itself. Mechanism plausible; real data in traumatic brain injury and wound healing. Asprey promoted it on his show without reading the paper carefully enough.

C-tier

  • L-theanine (8 cents): Best human study only 30 people; no aging data. Asprey takes it daily for 8 years because it makes mornings better (removes coffee jitters), but ranks it honestly as C.
  • Methylene blue (1800s textile dye, now nootropic): No human trial for cognition or longevity. Mechanism "one of the most interesting on this list." Asprey takes it weekly and discloses he sells it as affiliate—refuses to pretend the trial exists.
  • Berberine ("nature's Ozempic"): Real human data; genuinely lowers blood glucose, comparable to metformin in some trials. Problems: absorption terrible (most never enters blood); inconsistent results between products. It's a glucose drug, not a longevity drug—no lifespan data. Reasonable tool if blood sugar is a mess; no evidence it extends life.
  • Spermidine ($30/month): Gets sold standing next to 2016 Nobel Prize for autophagy, but spermidine appears nowhere in that Nobel. Real trial (100 people, 12 months) found no effect on memory or biomarkers. Actual evidence is in food: 829 Italians followed 20 years showed highest spermidine intake (whole grains, apples, salad, potatoes) had 39% lower mortality risk—equivalent to being 5.5 years younger. Capsule is C-tier; eating it is better.
  • Collagen (capsule): 19 studies on skin, nearly all funded by collagen sellers. Small, short-term effects. Mechanical problem: gut breaks collagen into amino acids; body decides where they go (not your face). If low on protein, take protein—cheaper.
  • Activated charcoal: Works exactly as advertised—and that's the problem. Binds indiscriminately, can't distinguish toxin from $400 of supplements taken an hour prior. Useful in ER, not as daily longevity habit. Take far away from everything else.
  • Glutathione (pill): Master antioxidant that declines with age, but swallowing it means digesting it (it's a protein). Topical version rests on one small pilot. Better to give body raw materials (glycine, NAC) to make it.
  • Prebiotic fiber (powder): 185 studies tie fiber intake to 15–30% lower death rates—"one of the biggest findings in this entire video." But that's fiber from food (beans, vegetables, whole grains). Powder is distant second; nobody has shown the powder produces those mortality numbers. A-tier for fiber, C-tier for the tub.
  • Acarbose (diabetes drug): Blocks starch-digesting enzyme; carbs pass partly undigested, flattening blood sugar spike. In the same animal longevity program where metformin failed, acarbose extended lifespan (especially in males). No human longevity trials. Side effect: undigested starch feeds gut bacteria (not a dinner party drug). Better animal data than metformin; same missing human data.
  • CoQ10 with PQQ: Body already makes CoQ10 unless on statin (which depletes it). Most people top off a full tank. B-tier if on statin; C-tier for everyone else.
  • Colostrum: Almost no human data outside gut permeability in athletes. Interesting mechanism, expensive, unstudied. C-tier for lack of evidence rather than bad evidence.
  • Ozone therapy (injected/insufflated): Asprey cleared mold damage with ozone and still uses it. No longevity trial. 20 years clinical use in Europe; mechanism holds up. C-tier; would move higher with a study. "I am not waiting for them to."

D-tier

  • NMN and NR (NAD precursors): "Biggest story in longevity supplements right now." One unreplicated study found muscle glucose uptake up ~25%; everything else (weight, body fat, liver fat, blood glucose) unchanged. Asprey has taken these for years, wants them to work, but "wanting something to work isn't evidence." D-tier now; "I'll happily move it the day somebody replicates the good result."
  • Racetams (piracetam, aniracetam): No consistent evidence in healthy adults; not approved as supplements in US. Bought from websites "designed in 2004 for research purposes."
  • 5-HTP (serotonin precursor): Most converts to serotonin in gut, not brain, bypassing body's control step (which exists for a reason).
  • Resveratrol (red wine compound): Launched entire longevity supplement industry on mouse data. Never delivered on promise. 20 years of human trials; inconsistent results at best.
  • Taurine (newest longevity story): 2023 Science paper showed taurine levels fall with age; giving it back to mice extended lifespan and improved every health marker. Also saw effects in monkeys. But human data is observational only (people with higher taurine are healthier ≠ taurine makes them healthier). No randomized longevity trial. Cheap, safe, Asprey takes it anyway. "It's the one I most expect to move up. But right now, it's a spectacular mouse study."
  • Rapamycin (immunosuppressant): "The frustrating one." Best animal longevity data in existence—extends lifespan in mice more reliably than anything else tested. Human trial missed primary endpoint completely. "Spectacular in mice, and we don't yet know in people."
  • Metformin (diabetes drug): Failed to extend lifespan in best animal longevity program. Real evidence it blunts exercise response—adaptations from training smaller on metformin. May trade "the single best evidenced longevity intervention we know of" (exercise) for a pill that didn't work in mice.

F-tier

  • C60 (carbon molecule in olive oil, ~$80): Entire longevity claim traces to one rat study (6 animals per group) from 2012, never replicated. Zero human trials. People drinking it because rats lived long.
  • Deuterium-depleted water (~$40/bottle): Only human data from company that sells it. That's the whole review.
  • Senolytics and fisetin (senescent cell clearers): Animal data "spectacular." Every human study to date under 20 people or feasibility pilot. Asprey wants this to work "more than almost anything else on this page." Still F-tier "until somebody runs a real trial."
  • Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) IV ($600–$1,000/bag): No trial for any healthspan outcome. Most leaves in urine. "Extremely expensive way to make your bathroom trips more colorful."

Devices, chambers, and procedures

S-tier

  • Blackout curtains ($30): 85,000 people wearing light sensors; brightest bedrooms had 53–67% higher type 2 diabetes risk independent of genetics. Gap between bright and dark nights ≈ gap between low and moderate genetic risk. "Your curtains are worth roughly as much as your DNA." 13 million hours of sensor data. "Nothing else in this category comes close."

A-tier

  • Blood flow restriction training (band + light weight): Muscle growth comparable to heavy lifting at 20–40% of load. Asprey calls it "the most underrated thing in this entire video." Muscle mass is one of strongest predictors of longevity after 65. "This belongs everywhere."
  • Cold water immersion (A-tier for cold, C-tier for equipment): Cold exposure spikes norepinephrine sharply, stays elevated for hours. Reduces muscle soreness after hard training. Caveat: if done straight after lifting, blunts muscle adaptation (cold shuts down inflammation signaling adaptation). Expensive chamber matched by cold bath in head-to-head (bath actually did better on muscle function). $5,000 chamber is "nice to have."
  • Red light panels (A-tier at right dose, D-tier at typical dose): Collagen density up 5.75 in treated vs. slight loss in controls. Collagen is ~33% of all body protein; you lose ~1% yearly after 20s. Real protocol: 15–25 minutes at close range. Most people flub dose (6 feet away for 4 minutes ≠ study protocol).
  • Cooling mattress (~7.5 more minutes deep sleep/night): Blinded, three centers, 72 people. Body cannot enter deep sleep until core temperature drops; air is terrible conductor. Cooling bed beats cooling room. Asprey loves his and won't give it up.
  • Infrared sauna (A-tier, traditional Finnish sauna better): 2,300 men followed 20 years; 4–7 sessions/week vs. 1 showed sudden cardiac death hazard ratio 0.37 (63% lower). All-cause mortality down 40%. Observational, one country, men only, self-selected. Still "damn impressive." Data from traditional Finnish saunas, not infrared (cheaper, lower temp, less studied).
  • 9-Minute AI Bike (high-intensity intervals): Training effect completely real. VO2 max is "about as strong a predictor of mortality as anything we can measure." Technically achievable on $50 spin bike or stairs, but "nobody does it" at right intensity, consistently, for years. Machine buys adherence. Asprey sells one.

B-tier

  • Neurofeedback (consumer headband): Against placebo, looks fantastic. Against sham version (identical-looking, identical-sounding, does nothing), effect not statistically significant. "Sham-controlled problem" is honest state of entire field. Theater follows benefit. D-tier for headband online. Asprey owns 40 Years of Zen (5-day intensive, clinical EEG, person watching brain whole time, adjusting). "Nobody has run a sham trial on that because you can't fake five days of it." Would rank his "a lot higher"; also owns it, so "weigh that however you want."
  • PEMF (pulsed electromagnetic fields mat): Real signal for knee pain; osteoarthritis data "not nothing." Nothing for energy, longevity, or cellular anything (what it gets sold for). C-tier, only if knees hurt.
  • Whole body cryotherapy (3 min at −200°F): Head-to-head against placebo, does no better. Feels incredible (not nothing), but cold water beats it, costs nothing, better data. C-tier.
  • Grounding (mat connecting to Earth's electrical potential): ~20 papers, most funded by seller. D-tier.
  • Sleep tracking (ring, wearable): Deep sleep number not lab-grade (estimates from movement/heart rate, not brain waves). Don't chase absolute number; chase trend. Change one thing, hold two weeks, watch line. Genuinely valuable for measurement. B-tier.
  • Continuous glucose monitors (arm sensor, reads blood sugar every few minutes): No trial showing healthy person wearing one lives longer. Very good feedback loop; glucose control upstream of aging. Same rule as sleep ring: chase trend, not number. B-tier.
  • Hyperbaric oxygen (B-tier for potential, see above under supplements).
  • Plasma exchange (filter blood plasma, replace with albumin): Biological age down 2.6 years with plasma exchange + antibody infusion; 1.3 years with exchange alone. "Most interesting result in this entire category." Single-blind, surrogate endpoint (not outcome), authors sell treatment. Reasons to wait; still promising. B-tier.
  • Compression boots (inflatable leg sleeves): Reduce soreness; decent data. Don't do anything for aging, circulation, or lymphatics (what marketing says). Nice 20 minutes if into that. C-tier.
  • Vagus nerve wearables: Mechanism genuinely good; vagal tone matters. Outcomes in healthy people thin. Strong data in epilepsy/depression with implanted devices, not ear wearables. C-tier.
  • Red bulbs after sunset (4 cents/day average): Clock in eye peaks in blue spectrum, nearly blind to red. Lets you see kitchen at 10pm without disrupting circadian rhythm. Not to be confused with red light panels. B-tier for sleep.
  • Blue light glasses (Asprey makes these): 17 randomized trials on eye strain found "not much." For evening screens and sleep timing, they hold up (circadian effect real). Honest answer: F-tier for eye strain, A-tier for sleep. C-tier on average; A-tier if using for sleep. "Actually supposed to make you look cool as hell."

C-tier

  • Ozone therapy (see above under supplements).
  • Acarbose (see above under supplements).
  • CoQ10 with PQQ (see above under supplements).
  • Colostrum (see above under supplements).
  • EWOT (exercise with oxygen therapy, mask during workout): If lungs work, blood already ~98% saturated. Dissolving more oxygen into plasma under load is real thing. No randomized trial yet. C-tier; would move with study. Asprey still uses it.
  • Peptides (BPC-157, etc.): Not one completed human trial published. Closest: 46-person study never past conference abstract. July FDA panel voted 8–6 to let compounding pharmacies make it over written objection of agency's own scientists. Animal healing data "genuinely interesting"; people report injury healing. C-tier; goes up day somebody finishes trial. "Untested is not the same as does not work and it is not the same as unsafe. It means nobody has run it yet." Critical: know your source (real risk is contamination, not molecule).

D-tier

  • Neurofeedback (consumer version, see above).
  • Stem cells (for longevity in healthy people): Asprey had half-liter bone marrow extracted, processed, injected into every joint (shoulders, knees, spine, hands). "Not cheap and not comfortable." Joints better; had arthritis at 28, doesn't now. For specific orthopedic injuries, real and growing data (knees especially). For longevity in healthy people, "not one trial." Nobody ran study asking whether stem cells make healthy person live longer. Ranking longevity claim, not his knees. D-tier. "Happy to change it with new studies, though I'm not happy to do it again."

F-tier

  • Young plasma ($80,000/liter): No trial showing clinical benefit in human being. "$80,000 for a maybe."
  • Exosomes (vesicles from stem cells, injected): No FDA-approved exosome products. FDA safety notification after Nebraska patients hospitalized. Agency: clinics "taking advantage of patients and flouting federal regulations." Stay away.
  • EMF and quantum products (stickers, pendants, router discs): No measurable mechanism. "This category is where the biohacking word I invented gets used to sell jewelry. Bummer."
  • Structured water (rearranged molecular structure): No.

The meta-pattern

Asprey identifies a crucial pattern: price often signals absence of evidence. Expensive interventions (NAD+ IVs, exosomes, young plasma, stem cells for longevity) typically lack human trials. If something works, it gets copied until commoditized and cheap (creatine, 10 cents). If it doesn't work, the only way to profit is to charge a lot once with a compelling story. "If something actually works, it gets copied until it's cheap. If it doesn't work, charging a lot once is the only way to make money on it."

However, the reverse isn't automatic: cheap junk (grounding mats, structured water, EMF stickers) is still junk. And some expensive things are excellent (sauna data best observational dataset in video; cooling mattress A-tier).

The real rule: "Somebody either ran the trial or they didn't."

The overrated

  • NAD+ IV drips ($600–$1,000/bag): No healthspan trial; most leaves in urine.
  • NMN (NAD precursor): Entire longevity supplement industry built on it; D-tier because good result never replicated.
  • Exosomes, young plasma, stem cells (for longevity, not orthopedics): All expensive, all lacking human longevity trials.

The underrated

  • Blood flow restriction training: Muscle growth at 20–40% of heavy-lifting load with band and light dumbbell. "Should be in every clinic in the country."
  • Red bulbs after sunset: 4 cents/day; lets you see without disrupting circadian rhythm.
  • Creatine (for brain, not biceps): Only supplement to make S-tier.

Asprey's mistakes

  • Hyperbaric oxygen: Promoted on his show without reading the paper carefully. 25 people, no control group.
  • Ozone as longevity play: Talked about it as longevity intervention when he can only tell you how it makes him feel. "That is a real distinction, and I should have made it sooner."
  • Consumer neurofeedback: D-tier, including his own program. Still sells it, still believes in it. "I'd rather tell you that straight than pretend the trial exists." Believes in 40 Years of Zen clinical version; has "incredible stories."

The boring answer that works

After $2 million and 25 years, Asprey found only three S-tier interventions:

  1. Sleep (blackout curtains)
  2. Movement (blood flow restriction)
  3. Nutrition (creatine, or steak)

"Everything above the boring answer is optimization. Optimization is real. I love optimization. It's my entire career, but it's the last 5%, and most people are buying the last 5% while the first 95 is wide open."

The unmeasured intervention: relationships

Asprey notes one intervention never appears on his list because "nobody at my conference is selling it. There's no chamber for it. There's no subscription."

Relationships: Meta-analysis of 148 studies (300,000 people) found strong social connection came with 50% higher likelihood of survival—matched smoking's effect and beat obesity and inactivity. Harvard study (since 1938) showed relationship satisfaction at 50 predicted physical health at 80 better than cholesterol.

"I can sell you glasses and a mattress and a bag of creatine. I cannot sell you that one, and it may be the strongest intervention in this entire video."

The foundation

"Sleep, movement, food, people—everything I rank today sits on top of those four. Nothing replaces them. Get those right, and the rest of this list becomes fun. Get those wrong, and look, just don't get those wrong."
