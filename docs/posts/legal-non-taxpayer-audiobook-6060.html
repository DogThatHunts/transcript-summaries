Article I, Section 9, Clause 4 requires direct taxes to be apportioned by census. Hughes explains tariffs (taxes on imports), excise taxes (voluntary, indirect taxes on specific goods like gasoline), and direct taxes (involuntary, imposed on people). He argues the modern federal income tax is a direct tax that violates the apportionment requirement. The 16th Amendment is introduced as the purported fix, but Hughes says it creates a direct conflict with Article I, Section 9, Clause 4 that has never been resolved by repeal.

Chapter 13. the two united states

Hughes distinguishes the United States of America (50 sovereign states) from the corporate United States, Inc. (D.C. and territories). He cites Hooven & Allison Co. v. He then examines 26 U.S.C. § 7701: the definition of "United States" for income tax purposes "includes only the states and the District of Columbia," and the definition of "state" "shall be construed to include the District of Columbia" — mentioning no other states. By the rule of statutory construction (Steinberg v. Carhartt, 2000; Meese v. Keene, 1987), what is not included is excluded. Chapter 14. but wait, there's more

Hughes traces the statutory definitions from 1913 through 1960. In 1913, "state or United States" included territories (Alaska, Hawaii, D.C., Puerto Rico, Philippines) but not the 48 states. When Hawaii became a state (August 21, 1959), it was struck from the definition on that exact date. The pattern: as territories become states, they are removed from the tax code’s definition of "United States," confirming that the 50 states are constitutionally shielded from direct taxation. He challenges skeptics: if the tax lawfully applied to the 50 states, Congress could simply amend § 7701 to include them — but it hasn’t in over a century because Article I, Section 9, Clause 4 prevents it.
