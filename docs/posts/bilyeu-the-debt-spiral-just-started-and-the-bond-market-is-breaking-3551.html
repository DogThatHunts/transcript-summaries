The debt spiral just started — and the bond market is breaking

Summary

Bilyeu and Andre Jick examine the escalating global debt crisis, arguing that the U.S. and other developed economies are entering a dangerous debt spiral driven by massive deficit spending, monetary recklessness, and the bond market's loss of confidence in government solvency. With global debt at $365 trillion and the U.S. adding roughly $6 billion daily to its debt burden, interest payments alone now exceed $1 trillion annually. The core problem: governments plan to inflate away their obligations, but bond markets are demanding higher yields in response, creating a vicious cycle. Jick contends that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's attempt to suppress bond yields through government purchases proved futile against a $32 trillion market, and that the U.S. faces an imminent "treasury market break" comparable to conditions preceding the 2008 financial crisis.

Five key insights

  1. Deficit spending and inflation are the real culprits. Jick argues that blaming inflation solely on the Iran war masks the deeper fiscal recklessness: $2 trillion in annual deficit spending and capital flows into AI forcing the government to print money to cover debt. Anyone discussing inflation without mentioning these factors is "lying."

  2. The bond market understands the Ponzi scheme. Lenders recognize that governments plan to inflate away debt, so they demand higher real yields. This is why the 10-year U.S. Treasury hit its highest level since 2002, Britain's 30-year bond exceeded 6% for the first time since 1998, and Japan recorded all-time highs—a global phenomenon.

  3. Scott Bessent's arrogance backfired spectacularly. When the Treasury Secretary declared "I am the house now" and challenged the bond market to bet against him, he fundamentally misunderstood his own power. His attempt to buy $6 billion in bonds proved insignificant against a $32 trillion market, forcing him to publicly admit he cannot set equilibrium prices—a power that resides solely with the Federal Reserve.

  4. China's lower bond yields reflect economic distress, not strength. While the U.S. pays over 5% for 10-year borrowing, China pays only 1.7%. Jick argues this is not a sign of Chinese superiority but rather severe domestic deflation, a housing market collapse worse than publicly admitted, and a government prioritizing balance-sheet cleanup over stimulus—a deflationary Japanese-style response.

  5. The debt cannot be canceled, paid off, or sustainably managed. Three-quarters of U.S. government debt is owed domestically to 401(k)s, pension funds, Social Security, and banks. The top 10% of Americans own 67% of all wealth and hold most bonds, meaning debt cancellation would harm the very people who own the assets. The only viable exit is inflation or financial repression, both of which steal from savers.

The inflation trap and government strategy

Jick argues that the government's stated strategy is to use inflation to reduce the real value of debt. Trump told Time magazine that "certain levels of inflation will also pay off that debt very rapidly"—a statement Jick calls "one of the most unhinged things" ever said, as it amounts to admitting plans to steal purchasing power from ordinary citizens. This is financial repression: paying bondholders less in real terms than promised, a tactic the U.S. used after World War II (capping rates at 2.5% while inflation reached 19%) and again in 2020 (0% rates during 9% inflation).

The fulcrum and the treasury market break

A trader with 30 years of experience in U.S. interest rates argues that the Treasury market is the "fulcrum" on which the entire financial system balances. Every loan, mortgage, and stock price depends on it. By artificially suppressing Treasury and mortgage yields through forced bond purchases (e.g., Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac buying $200 billion in mortgage bonds), policymakers distorted risk pricing across the system. When external shocks—such as the Iran war driving oil prices up—force rates back up, the entire structure tilts dangerously. The trader predicts 30-year rates could reach 6–7% within six months, potentially triggering a treasury market "break" comparable to September 2007, the year before the 2008 crash.

China's housing collapse and deflationary response

Jick notes that China's government enforced strict borrowing limits on property developers in 2020, allowing the largest developer, Evergrande, to default and be liquidated. Home prices fell 22%. Unlike the U.S., which bails out investors during housing crises (the Fed bought over $1 trillion in mortgage bonds in 2008 and 2020), China allowed the correction. However, this deflationary approach—refusing to borrow despite lower rates—suggests the housing collapse was far worse than publicly admitted and that China's economy is in serious distress, not strength.

The divergence between U.S. and Chinese economic models

The U.S. is "hyper-financialized," meaning it has outsourced manufacturing, hollowed out its industrial base, and become dependent on asset price inflation (stocks and real estate). When crises hit, the government prints money to prop up these assets, which benefits the wealthy disproportionately. China, by contrast, spent 20 years borrowing to build productive infrastructure (electricity output has quadrupled since 2005, while the U.S. output is flat). When China's housing bubble burst, it chose not to bail out investors, instead focusing on balance-sheet cleanup. Jick argues the U.S. must either return to manufacturing and real production or accept that it will continue to bail out the financial sector—the only economy it has left.

The wealth concentration trap

The interest payments on government debt flow directly to bondholders, who are disproportionately wealthy. The top 10% own 67% of all U.S. wealth and hold most bonds. As interest rates rise, the government collects taxes from everyone but pays interest primarily to the wealthy. Jick notes that interest costs have doubled in four years and now consume the highest share of the economy in 60 years, while large corporations pay the lowest share in interest since 1962—a stark inversion of who bears the burden.

Speculation on systemic collapse and crisis scenarios

Jick explores whether policymakers might deliberately time a financial collapse with a global catastrophe (energy crisis, quantum computing threat, AI-driven job losses) to justify emergency measures. He notes that crises enable governments to implement unpopular changes—printing money becomes a "rescue" rather than theft, and people accept reduced benefits, later retirement, and delayed care. He cites Klaus Schwab's June 2020 statement about the pandemic as "a rare but narrow window of opportunity to reflect, reimagine, reset our world" and suggests that Agenda 2030 goals (digital money, digital identity) may be positioned to roll out during the next crisis. This section ventures into speculative territory and is presented as hypothesis, not established fact.

What happens next

Jick emphasizes that there are only two paths forward: keep rates high (increasing government interest burden and slowing the economy) or lower rates and print money (inflating asset prices, benefiting asset owners disproportionately). If the system breaks, those with cash and access to cheap credit will buy assets forced onto the market by those without resources. He stresses that understanding the bond market is essential because it reflects whether lenders believe the government can pay interest—the only question that matters. When that confidence breaks, "all hell breaks loose."
