Five ancient occult symbols—the Uroboros, hexagram, Ankh, Eye of Horus, and Black Sun—reveal hidden layers of meaning about transformation, balance, and the human soul.

The deeper meaning behind occult symbols explained

SecretWithin explores five ancient symbols that have persisted across millennia, embedded in temples, mythology, and modern culture. Rather than mere decorations, these symbols functioned as encoded messages about cosmic order, transformation, and the nature of consciousness itself.

The Uroboros: the serpent consuming itself

The Uroboros—a serpent swallowing its own tail—first appeared in ancient Egypt coiled around the sun god Ra during his journey through the underworld. It later migrated into Greek philosophy, medieval alchemy, and Norse mythology as Jörmungandr, the world serpent encircling the Earth.

On the surface, it represents eternity and the endless cycle of birth, death, and rebirth. Deeper still, SecretWithin argues, it whispers of cosmic unity—the eternal dance between creation and destruction, a universe endlessly devouring and renewing itself.

For alchemists, the Uroboros was a sacred map of transformation: the self consuming itself, breaking down, only to rise again as something greater. Carl Jung called it the ultimate symbol of individuation, the moment the ego awakens and realizes it is both the question and the answer. The symbol ultimately poses a personal question: are you trapped in a cycle or evolving through it?

The hexagram: union of opposites

Two triangles meeting to form a star—the hexagram has been known by many names: the Seal of Solomon, the Star of the Macrocosm, the Union of Heaven and Earth. Today it is primarily recognized as a symbol of Judaism, but it was originally a mystical key passed through secret traditions from alchemists to Kabbalists.

In ancient alchemy, the upward-pointing triangle symbolized fire—masculine, active, and spiritual—while the downward-pointing triangle represented water—feminine, receptive, and material. When these forces meet, they create balance and harmony reflecting the architecture of the universe itself. This is duality in union, not duality in conflict.

In Kabbalistic traditions, the hexagram became a map of the divine, representing Tiferet, the central sphere on the Tree of Life where beauty, truth, and balance reside—the point where God's energy meets human consciousness. Legend holds that King Solomon used this seal to command spirits and demons, not to destroy them but to understand and integrate their power. The hexagram does not banish shadow; it embraces it.

SecretWithin connects this to Jungian psychology as the sacred marriage—the alchemical union of inner masculine and feminine, conscious and unconscious. Enlightenment, the symbol teaches, comes not from escaping the world but from integrating it, reconciling opposites within oneself to become whole: not pure or perfect, but complete.

The Ankh: the breath of life

The Ankh—a cross with a loop on top—was far more than a decorative symbol to ancient Egyptians. It represented the breath of life, the key of the Nile, and the sacred loop of eternity. It appears throughout Egyptian art held in the hands of gods, offered to the noses of pharaohs, and floating beside the sun disk of Ra.

In Egyptian mythology, life was not merely about survival but about alignment with cosmic forces, divine order, and the afterlife. The Ankh reminded people that true life began after death. In the Osirian myth, when Osiris is torn apart by Set and resurrected by Isis, the Ankh becomes a symbol of restoration and divine resurrection—a process of transformation rather than a promise of heaven.

Gods used the Ankh to breathe energy into the soul, to awaken the Ka (the life force), and guide it beyond the veil. Temple initiates carried ankhs during sacred rites not as ornaments but as tools of passage, posing the question: am I truly alive or am I just surviving?

In esoteric traditions, the Ankh became a symbol of life transcending time—a convergence of male and female, spirit and matter, birth and rebirth. The loop represents the womb of creation, the crossbar the horizon, and the vertical line the path of ascent from earth to the divine. It is a map of how the human becomes eternal, because to the ancients, death was never the end but initiation.

The Eye of Horus: seeing within

One eye surrounded by light, floating above a pyramid or etched into stone—this symbol appears on currency, in architecture, in secret society documents, and in conspiracy theories. But long before it became known as the Eye of Providence, it was the Eye of Horus.

In Egyptian mythology, Horus, the falcon-headed god, loses his eye in a divine battle against Set, the god of chaos. The eye is restored not just physically but spiritually, becoming a symbol of healing, clarity, and cosmic order. The left eye of Horus represented the moon, intuition, mystery, and the unconscious—not about seeing the world but seeing within.

Centuries later, esoteric orders reinterpreted this sacred eye. To Freemasons, it became the Eye of Providence—divine intelligence observing all things not as a tyrant but as a witness. In myth and mysticism alike, the eye is not merely a watcher but a mirror reflecting intentions, fears, and truths while demanding self-awareness.

To see with this eye is to awaken, to open the third eye—the space between illusion and insight. In psychological terms, it represents individuation, the moment one steps out of the story one has been told and begins seeing the patterns behind it. The symbol appears across mythologies: Odin sacrificed one eye for wisdom, Shiva opens his third eye to destroy ignorance, and in Christianity the eye of God watches not to punish but to reveal. The symbol endures because it asks an ancient question: what if someone sees everything, and what if that someone is you?

The Black Sun: the threshold of transformation

A sun that does not shine but pools—the Black Sun is a symbol whispered in secret circles, carved into occult diagrams, and feared for what it might represent. Some trace it to esoteric alchemy, where it represented the negredo, the first stage of transformation: decomposition, ego death, and dissolution of the false self.

Others link it to ancient solar cults where the sun disappears each night only to be reborn—a symbol not of light but of what gives birth to light. In Norse mythology, at Ragnarök (the end of all things), the sun is swallowed and the world falls into darkness before the rebirth of a new age. The Black Sun is that threshold between death and transfiguration.

However, in the 20th century the symbol was twisted and co-opted by dark ideologies to project dominance, power, and control. SecretWithin emphasizes this is not its true origin. The real Black Sun does not dominate others; it challenges you. It is the void within yourself—the part you refuse to look at, the wound never acknowledged, the truth buried to survive.

In Jungian psychology, it mirrors the shadow, the part of the psyche where all repressed thoughts, desires, and traumas live. Yet here lies a paradox: the Black Sun is not your enemy but the source of real transformation. Nothing evolves in comfort, and nothing is reborn in light without first passing through darkness. In myth, heroes always descend before they rise. In life, we break before we awaken. The most powerful light sometimes comes from the place where nothing seemed able to grow. The Black Sun is a symbol of endings, beginnings, and the power that only reveals itself when you dare to face the dark.
