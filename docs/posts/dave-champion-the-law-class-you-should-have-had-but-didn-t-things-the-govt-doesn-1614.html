The statutory limits of the federal income tax

Dave Champion argues that the federal income tax, since its inception in 1913, has only ever been legislated to apply to three distinct categories of persons: non-resident aliens with U.S. source income, foreign corporations with U.S. source income, and U.S. citizens residing temporarily abroad with foreign earned income. He asserts that in the 111 years of the tax's existence, no Treasury regulation or decision has ever legally stepped outside these boundaries.

According to Champion, the government is fully aware of these limitations. He claims that the Treasury Department deliberately keeps its regulations within these strict guardrails to prevent a clean constitutional challenge in the Supreme Court, which would expose the fact that the tax does not apply to the vast majority of the 334 million Americans. He traces the widespread belief that everyone owes income tax to post-World War II propaganda, which successfully framed the tax as an unavoidable levy on earning a living.

The 16th Amendment and the nature of excise taxes

Champion explains that the U.S. Supreme Court has established two great classes of taxation: direct and indirect. Historically, direct taxes applied only to land and slaves and required apportionment among the states. Champion highlights Brushaber v. Union Pacific Railroad, where Chief Justice Edward Douglass White clarified that the 16th Amendment did not authorize a direct tax on labor without apportionment, but rather corrected the court's previous error in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co.

Consequently, Champion argues that the income tax remains an indirect excise tax. By definition, an excise tax is a tax on the exercise of a government-granted privilege and must lack "absolute unavoidable demand." Because working to feed and shelter oneself is an inherent right rather than a government privilege, Champion asserts that ordinary domestic labor cannot constitutionally be subjected to an excise tax.

Coercive compliance and the W-4 form

A central mechanism of what Champion terms the "largest financial crime in history" is the systematic misuse of tax forms. He explains that private property—such as a worker's paycheck—cannot be taken by the government without an excise-taxable event. To bypass this, Champion claims the government relies on citizens voluntarily, if unknowingly, declaring themselves subject to withholding.

According to Champion, the W-4 form is legally designed only for individuals already subject to withholding, such as non-resident aliens or federal employees working within exclusive congressional jurisdictions (like territories). However, private employers, acting as compliance agents out of fear or ignorance, force domestic workers to sign W-4 forms under penalty of perjury. Champion argues that signing these forms constitutes a false declaration of status, effectively trapping citizens within the tax system.

Executive influence over the judiciary

Champion and Gary Brown discuss why these arguments rarely succeed in federal courts. Champion points to "judicial conferences"—continuing education events for federal judges held at luxury destinations. He claims these conferences are organized and led by the Department of Justice's Tax Division, meaning executive branch prosecutors and civil litigators are directly instructing the judicial branch on how to interpret tax law and dismiss challenges.

This executive influence, Champion argues, has created a deep split among federal circuits. Half of the circuits align with Supreme Court precedent by viewing the income tax as an excise, while the other half erroneously rule that it is a direct tax without apportionment. Champion notes that judges in non-compliant circuits frequently sanction attorneys who cite Brushaber, making it incredibly difficult for litigants to secure a fair hearing without appealing directly to the Supreme Court.
