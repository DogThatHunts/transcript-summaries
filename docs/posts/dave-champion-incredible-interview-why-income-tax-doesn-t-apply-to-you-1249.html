The legal distinction between direct and indirect taxation

Champion bases his core argument on the constitutional division of taxation into direct and indirect taxes. He highlights the 2024 Supreme Court decision in Moore v. United States, which he claims reaffirms the court's 1916 ruling in Brushaber v. Union Pacific Railroad Co. that taxes on income are indirect taxes.

Because Congress has never successfully levied an unapportioned direct tax on property, Champion argues that the income tax must be classified as an indirect excise tax. In U.S. jurisprudence, an excise tax is synonymous with a privilege tax, meaning it is levied on the exercise of a government-granted privilege (such as importing goods or distilling alcohol). Champion contends that working a normal job, earning a living, and owning personal property are inherent rights rather than government-granted privileges, meaning ordinary domestic income cannot constitutionally be subjected to an excise tax.

Decoding Title 26 and the targeted classes

According to Champion, Title 26 of the United States Code (the Internal Revenue Code) explicitly defines who is subject to the income tax, though the public narrative obscures these details. He argues that Subtitle A is divided into distinct functional chapters: * Chapters 1 and 2 serve merely as "rules of computation" to calculate the amount of tax owed if a person is already determined to be liable. * Chapter 3 acts as the actual "collection mechanism," which is explicitly titled as withholding on non-resident aliens and foreign corporations.

Champion asserts that the tax code only imposes the income tax on three narrow categories: non-resident aliens with U.S. source income, foreign corporations with U.S. source income, and U.S. citizens residing abroad with foreign-earned income. For domestic citizens living and working within the states, he argues no statute exists requiring them to pay or have taxes withheld.

Practical compliance, w-9s, and the "status questionnaire"

In response to caller questions about modern financial systems, LLCs, and cryptocurrency exchanges, Champion warns that financial institutions are designed to treat everyone as a "foreign person" until they certify otherwise. When an individual signs a Form W-9, they are swearing under penalty of perjury that they are a domestic agent receiving U.S. source income on behalf of a foreign person.

To bypass this, Champion provides a free "status questionnaire" that individuals can submit to vendors or institutions. This form acts as a legal filter, certifying that the signer is not a foreign entity and therefore does not require a W-9 or subsequent 1099 reporting. For those who successfully exit the tax system, Champion argues that asset appreciation (such as real estate or cryptocurrency) is legally treated as an increase in personal property rather than taxable capital gains.

Responding to audits and IRS lock-in letters

When dealing with IRS audits (which the agency calls "examinations") or "lock-in letters" sent to employers to force maximum withholding, Champion strongly advises against staying silent or telling the agency to "pound sand."

He explains that IRS letters (such as CP54) are legally designed to offer a taxpayer "due process" to prove the agency's presumptions are incorrect. Champion advises individuals to actively respond to these inquiries by submitting affidavits, statements of law, and factual evidence to establish an "evidentiary record." He notes that "exempt" status on a W-4 is a technical designation reserved for non-resident aliens who have overpaid their taxes, and ordinary citizens who incorrectly claim this status inevitably trigger IRS lock-in letters.
