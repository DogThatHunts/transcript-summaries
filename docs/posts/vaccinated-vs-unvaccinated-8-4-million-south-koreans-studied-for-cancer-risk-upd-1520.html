Vaccinated vs. unvaccinated: 8.4 million South Koreans studied for cancer risk (update #196)

An analysis of a massive South Korean population study reveals a statistical correlation between COVID-19 vaccination status and increased risk of certain cancers, though researchers urge cautious interpretation.

Dr. Mikhail Oreshchik of Merit Genomics reviews a large-scale population study from South Korea that analyzes cancer incidence rates based on COVID-19 vaccination status. Utilizing data from the Korean National Health Insurance registry, the study tracked approximately 8.4 million residents of Seoul over a three-year period (2021–2023). Dr. Oreshchik breaks down the study's methodology, key statistical findings, and critical limitations.

Study design and cohort matching

The study was made possible by South Korea's single-payer healthcare system, which covers approximately 98% of Seoul's population. Researchers compared a vaccinated cohort against an unvaccinated cohort of over 600,000 individuals (representing about 8% of the city's population).

To ensure an accurate comparison, the researchers matched participants based on age, gender, insurance level, and comorbidities. However, Dr. Oreshchik points out several exclusions and potential areas of confusion in the methodology: * Exclusions: Anyone with a history of cancer prior to the study, individuals who died during the study period, and those with unspecified vaccine types (~70,000 people) were excluded. Crucially, partially vaccinated individuals (those who received only one of two required doses, totaling roughly 300,000 people) were completely excluded rather than grouped with the unvaccinated. * Timeline discrepancies: The observation window for the unvaccinated group began on January 1, 2022, tracking them for one year. For the vaccinated group, tracking began the day after they completed their primary two-dose series, which for many occurred in 2021. Dr. Oreshchik notes this timeline difference means the unvaccinated group technically had more time to develop cancer during the overall study period. * Vaccine types: Within the vaccinated group, 81% received mRNA vaccines (predominantly Pfizer), 15% received cDNA/adenovirus-vector vaccines (such as AstraZeneca), and 5% received a mixed regimen.

Cancer risk and hazard ratios

The study analyzed hazard ratios (HR) to determine the relative risk of developing cancer between the two groups. A hazard ratio of 1.0 indicates no difference in risk, while anything above 1.0 indicates an increased risk. Dr. Oreshchik focuses strictly on results that achieved both statistical and clinical significance (where the 95% confidence interval remained entirely above 1.0).

  • Overall risk: Vaccinated individuals showed a 27% higher overall risk of a cancer diagnosis compared to unvaccinated individuals (HR 1.27).
  • Risk by cancer type: Several specific cancers showed clinically significant increases in the vaccinated cohort:
  • Prostate cancer: HR ~1.7 (70% increased risk)
  • Lung cancer: HR ~1.5 (50% increased risk)
  • Thyroid cancer: HR ~1.4 (40% increased risk)
  • Gastric and colorectal cancers: HR ~1.3 (30% increased risk)
  • Breast cancer: HR ~1.2 (20% increased risk)
  • Risk by vaccine platform: Adenovirus-vector (cDNA) vaccines were associated with the highest overall cancer risk (HR approaching 1.5). Mixed vaccine regimens showed an HR of approximately 1.3, while mRNA vaccines showed the lowest relative increase (HR ~1.2).
  • Demographics: The increased risk was more pronounced in women than in men, and was highest among individuals over the age of 75. Dr. Oreshchik flags a minor anomaly in the data where unvaccinated individuals aged 65–75 appeared to have higher cancer rates than those over 75, which he describes as counterintuitive.

Specific cancer associations and booster effects

By cross-referencing specific cancers with vaccine types, the study revealed distinct patterns: * Thyroid cancer risk was elevated across all vaccine types. * Gastrointestinal cancers were uniquely elevated in the cDNA-only vaccine group. * Colorectal and lung cancers saw elevated risks in both the cDNA and mRNA groups. * Breast cancer risk was elevated in the mRNA and mixed vaccine groups. * Prostate cancer risk was primarily elevated in the cDNA group.

The researchers also compared individuals who received only the primary two-dose series (~300,000 people) against those who received a third booster dose (~700,000 people, almost exclusively Pfizer mRNA). The booster dose was associated with a significant increase in the risk of gastric and pancreatic cancers. Dr. Oreshchik correlates this with a recent Japanese study that observed worse pancreatic cancer outcomes in highly vaccinated populations, a phenomenon that Japanese researchers hypothesized might be linked to IgG4 antibody class-switching.

Study limitations and causal interpretation

Dr. Oreshchik emphasizes that this study is observational and cannot establish direct causality. He highlights several limitations noted by the authors and himself: * Short follow-up period: The study only tracked participants for one year. Because solid tumors typically take years to develop, a one-year window is highly likely to miss long-term oncogenic trends. * Selection bias: While the authors argue that universal healthcare access in Seoul minimizes selection bias (meaning unvaccinated individuals are just as likely to visit doctors and receive diagnoses as vaccinated individuals), it remains a potential confounding factor. * Absolute vs. relative risk: Dr. Oreshchik notes that while the relative risk increases (hazard ratios) appear high, the absolute incidence of cancer in the population remains low. * The role of infection: The authors of the paper note that SARS-CoV-2 infection itself is known to contribute to cancer development through various mechanisms, potentially mediated by the spike protein—a biological agent common to both natural infection and vaccination.

Ultimately, Dr. Oreshchik advises interpreting these findings with caution but supports the authors' call for broader, more detailed scientific investigation into the long-term health outcomes of COVID-19 vaccination status.
