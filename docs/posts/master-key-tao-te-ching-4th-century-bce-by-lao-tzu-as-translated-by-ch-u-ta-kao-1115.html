Master Key | Tao Te Ching (4th Century BCE) by Lao Tzu - as translated by Ch’u Ta-Kao

A timeless translation of Lao Tzu's foundational philosophical text, exploring the power of non-action, humility, and the inexhaustible, nameless source of the universe.

This text presents the 1937 translation of Lao Tzu's Tao Te Ching by Ch'u Ta-Kao, read by Sams Huck for the Master Key Society. The work outlines the foundational principles of Taoism, emphasizing the nameless and formless source of the universe (the Tao), the power of non-action (wu wei), the strength of humility, and the path to peace through simplicity and non-contention.

The nature of Tao and non-action (chapters 1–20)

Lao Tzu introduces the Tao as an inexpressible, eternal reality. It is the antecedent of heaven and earth, existing as a profound, nameless source from which all apparent distinctions manifest.

  • The power of emptiness: The utility of everyday objects—such as carriage wheels, clay vessels, and houses—arises not from their solid parts, but from the empty spaces within them (Chapter 11).
  • Governing by non-action: The sage rules by emptying the people's hearts, filling their stomachs, and weakening their ambitions. By practicing non-action, nothing remains ungoverned (Chapter 3).
  • The metaphor of water: The highest goodness resembles water, which benefits all things without contending and willingly occupies the low places that others despise (Chapter 8).
  • Sensory and intellectual moderation: Excessive colors, sounds, and tastes dull human senses and conduct. The sage provides for the stomach (inner sustenance) rather than the eye (external desires) (Chapter 12).
  • The loss of Tao: When the great Tao is lost, artificial virtues like benevolence, righteousness, and filial duty are emphasized. True harmony is replaced by hypocrisy and formal rules (Chapter 18).

Humility, simplicity, and the strength of the weak (chapters 21–40)

The middle chapters focus on the paradoxes of strength and weakness, demonstrating how yielding and humility preserve life and order.

  • The paradox of yielding: Lao Tzu states, "Be bent, and you will remain straight. Be vacant, and you will remain full" (Chapter 22). By not displaying or praising himself, the sage shines and excels.
  • The universe as a divine vessel: The world cannot be forced or shaped by human will. Those who attempt to shape it damage it, and those who insist upon it lose it (Chapter 29).
  • Weapons as ill omens: Arms are instruments of destruction and are rejected by the man of Tao. Conquests should never be celebrated, as delighting in conquest is delighting in the slaughter of men (Chapter 31).
  • Self-mastery over external power: "He who knows others is wise; he who knows himself is enlightened. He who conquers others is strong; he who conquers himself is mighty" (Chapter 33).
  • The regression of virtue: When Tao is lost, virtue follows; when virtue is lost, benevolence follows; when benevolence is lost, righteousness follows; and when righteousness is lost, ritual follows—which is the beginning of confusion (Chapter 38).

Governing through quietude and moderation (chapters 41–60)

Lao Tzu applies the principles of the Tao to statecraft, advocating for a quiet, non-interfering government that allows the people to thrive naturally.

  • The pursuit of decrease: While formal learning increases daily, the pursuit of Tao decreases daily until one arrives at non-action, through which everything is accomplished (Chapter 48).
  • The danger of excess: Royal palaces that are well-kept while fields are left weedy and granaries empty represent a deviation from the Tao, serving as an encouragement to robbery (Chapter 53).
  • The infant as a model of virtue: An infant possesses tender bones and soft sinews but holds a strong grasp, representing the peak of vitality and perfect harmony, untouched by venomous insects or beasts of prey (Chapter 55).
  • Non-interference in governance: The more restrictions, laws, and sharp implements exist in an empire, the poorer and more confused the people become. The sage declares, "Inasmuch as I betake myself to non-action, the people of themselves become developed" (Chapter 57).
  • Gentle leadership: Governing a great state should be approached as gently as cooking a small fish, avoiding harsh movements that ruin the whole (Chapter 60).

The virtue of non-contention and ultimate peace (chapters 61–81)

The final chapters reinforce the ultimate triumph of the weak over the strong, the necessity of love, and the vision of a simple, peaceful society.

  • The three treasures: Lao Tzu identifies his three primary treasures as love (which enables bravery), moderation (which enables amplitude), and not venturing to go ahead of the world (which enables leadership) (Chapter 67).
  • The sickness of false knowledge: "Not knowing that one knows is best. Thinking that one knows when one does not know is sickness" (Chapter 71).
  • The supreme executioner: Human authorities should not rush to execute citizens. To kill in place of the "supreme executioner" (nature or destiny) is compared to trying to cut wood in place of a master carpenter—one will inevitably cut their own hand (Chapter 74).
  • The softness of life: Humans and plants are soft and tender when alive, but hard and dry when dead. Therefore, hardness is an attribute of death, while softness and flexibility are attributes of life (Chapter 76).
  • The triumph of water: Water is the weakest and most yielding substance, yet nothing is better at attacking the hard and strong. This demonstrates that the weak can overcome the strong (Chapter 78).
  • The ideal simple state: Lao Tzu envisions a small, self-contained state where people are satisfied with their food, delighted in their dress, and secure in their dwellings. Though neighboring states are close enough to hear their dogs bark, the people have no desire to travel or wage war (Chapter 80).
