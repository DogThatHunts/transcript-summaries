Overview

Nathan Apffel, a filmmaker and religious accountability advocate, joins DannyJones for an in-depth conversation about the corruption of institutional Christianity in America. Apffel argues that capitalism has fundamentally transformed churches from spiritual communities into profit-driven enterprises, enabling financial abuse, sexual abuse, and the erosion of Christian values. He discusses his documentary series The Religion Business, which investigates the legal architecture that allows churches to operate without financial transparency, and his mission to reform religious institutions before the 2028 presidential election.

The corruption of American Christianity

From spiritual practice to enterprise

Apffel traces Christianity's trajectory through history using a quote from former U.S. Senate Chaplain Richard Halverson: "In ancient Greece, it was a spiritual practice. In Rome, it became a philosophy. In Europe, it became a culture. In America, it became an enterprise."

The early church in Rome was dangerous to institutional power because it transformed society through direct action—picking up orphans and widows from the streets, creating hospitals and orphanages. But once Constantine adopted Christianity and gave it state resources, the institution began to corrupt. Today, American churches have become capitalistic ventures where pastors function as CEOs, buildings serve as real estate portfolios, and congregants are treated as revenue streams.

The legal architecture of abuse

Churches exploit a loophole in U.S. tax law: while nonprofits must file Form 990 (a public financial disclosure), churches are exempt. This means a $50 million church has zero legal obligation to disclose: - Executive salaries - Where money is spent - Asset ownership - Financial conflicts of interest

Apffel documents examples like Ed Young Jr.'s church in Dallas, where the pastor allegedly has a $3.6 million annual housing allowance (based on property valuations), drives golden Range Rovers, and built an underground parking garage and pickleball court on church property—all funded by congregant donations and completely legal.

The housing allowance scam

Pastors can claim a "housing allowance" that is tax-free, allowing them to offset massive portions of their income. Kenneth Copeland, for example, lives in a 19,000-square-foot parsonage (owned by the church, not him) on a 1,200-acre property with a private airstrip, all funded by donor money. The church claims it's a "house of worship," allowing the plane to be registered as a tax-exempt asset.

Real estate as generational debt

Churches leverage real estate to extract wealth across generations. A pastor can acquire a paid-off church building worth $25 million, then convince congregants to fund a $25 million purchase of that building, pocketing the difference. The new congregation then inherits the debt, and the cycle repeats.

The Catholic Archdiocese of New York agreed to pay $800 million to settle lawsuits from 1,300 sex abuse survivors. However, the settlement included a one-year lookback window, meaning hundreds of thousands of potential victims are barred by statute of limitations from coming forward.

The Child sexual abuse crisis

Systemic enablement

Religious institutions are not mandatory reporters in most states, meaning clergy can conceal abuse without legal consequence. Churches use non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) to silence victims, and financial secrecy allows abuse to continue unchecked. Apffel's upcoming documentary features interviews with survivors of ritualistic abuse within the LDS and other denominations, including allegations of blood oaths and child murder in temple ceremonies.

Underfunded Law enforcement

Internet Crimes Against Children (ICAC) task forces receive less than $40 million annually in federal funding—a fraction of the $40 billion spent on border security. Individual ICAC officers report being unable to afford basic equipment like hard drives to investigate child exploitation cases.

Legislative solutions

Elizabeth Phillips is pushing "Trey's Law," which would prohibit NDAs in cases of child sexual abuse and make clergy mandatory reporters. The law passed unanimously in the Senate but faces opposition from churches and insurance companies—the only two groups lobbying against it.

The pendulum theory of Christ

Apffel presents a framework for understanding Christ's teachings across the spectrum of Christian belief:

On one end: Conservative Christians believe Christ is the literal Son of God, born of a virgin, died and resurrected, and is coming back.

On the other end: Historians unanimously agree a historical figure named Jesus was crucified by Romans.

The key insight: Regardless of where one falls on this spectrum, Christ's core teachings about loving your neighbor, caring for the poor, and rejecting ego remain constant. The "ethos of Christ" transcends theological literalism.

The role of psychedelics and ancient Religion

DannyJones and Apffel discuss the Eleusinian Mysteries—ancient Greek religious rites where participants consumed ergot-laced wine (a natural psychedelic) as part of mystery rituals. Some scholars argue this influenced early Christian mysticism and that figures like John (author of Revelation) may have had visionary experiences induced by psychedelics or spiritual states.

The Bible and interpretation

The apocrypha and lost Books

Books like Enoch, the Infancy Gospel, and others were removed from the Bible ~200 years ago but were standard reading for Christians for 1,800 years. The book of Jude quotes the book of Enoch, yet most Christians never read it. Apffel argues this removal was deliberate to prevent questioning of institutional authority.

Translation and manipulation

The Greek word for "lestai" (used when Jesus is arrested) can mean "pirate," "bandit," or "human trafficker." Different translations and interpretations of ancient Greek have been weaponized to support various political and theological agendas.

The mark of the beast

The number 666 in Revelation translates to "Nero Caesar" in Hebrew numerology—referring to political power and monarchy, not a future antichrist. The Geneva Bible's margin notes explained this, but King James removed these annotations to maintain institutional control.
