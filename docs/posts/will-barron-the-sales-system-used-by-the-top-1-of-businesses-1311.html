Will Barron | The Sales System Used by the Top 1% of Businesses

A practical guide to mapping your buyer's journey, scripting key interactions, and building a predictable sales process that runs without you.

To sell like the top 1% of businesses, you must get your sales process out of your head and onto paper. Will Barron argues that top performers are not inherently better or more confident; they simply run a structured process that can eventually be automated and delegated. Many business owners get trapped in inconsistent "panic selling" cycles because they try to automate or hire salespeople before actually defining their system.

The five steps of the sales ladder

Barron visualizes sales maturity as a five-step ladder that businesses must climb sequentially: 1. Effort: Selling only when panicked by low numbers. 2. Habits: Performing regular sales activities, though inconsistently mixed with ineffective tasks. 3. Processes: Documenting current steps and eliminating unnecessary tasks. 4. Systems: Laying out the entire customer acquisition process to make revenue predictable. 5. Automation and delegation: Handing off the system to software or staff while monitoring performance via a CRM.

Attempting to jump directly to delegation without a documented system is a primary cause of hiring failures and wasted software spend.

Step 1: map the buyer's journey

The first step is to physically draw the prospect's journey from left to right on a landscape sheet of paper. This map should span from the moment they realize they have a problem to when the invoice is paid. Drawing this visually offers more flexibility than bulleted lists for inserting notes and identifying bottlenecks.

To illustrate, Barron shares the story of Colin, an interior design studio owner who generated plenty of leads but struggled to close proposals. By mapping the journey, Colin realized his prospects got stuck trying to visualize the space and calculate ROI. By changing his process from emailing a static PDF to hosting an interactive meeting with all stakeholders, Colin's close rate jumped from 17% to over 35%.

Step 2: document the transition requirements

Instead of reinventing the wheel for every prospect, document exactly what is required to move a buyer from one stage of the journey to the next. Barron compares a sales system to a recipe: a description of a meal (e.g., "creamy and hot") is useless for replication, whereas a recipe provides precise ingredients, quantities, and timing. Documenting these steps prevents the exhaustion of making ad-hoc sales decisions late at night.

Step 3: script the critical sales moments

Write down the exact words and questions for key interactions, such as discovery calls, follow-up messages, and proposal presentations.

This process is often difficult because experienced business owners rely on "unconscious competence"—the ability to navigate sales calls instinctively without thinking. When scaling his own team at Salesman.com, Barron spent hours reviewing his own calls to extract and document his natural questioning sequence. Systematizing this implicit knowledge is essential for training new hires successfully.

Step 4: build a complete, imperfect system

The final step is to assemble these elements under the philosophy that "done is better than perfect." Many business owners delay building their sales system because they are waiting for a large block of free time that never arrives.

In zero-sum service markets where buyers choose only one provider, you do not need a flawless system to win. Having a basic, documented process—even a "three out of ten"—puts you ahead of the competition and makes your revenue generation predictable.
