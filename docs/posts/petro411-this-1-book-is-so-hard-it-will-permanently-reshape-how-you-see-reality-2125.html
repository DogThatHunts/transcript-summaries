The core bet: why group averages lie

Imagine a coin flip game: heads wins you 50% of your wealth, tails loses you 40%. For a large group, the "ensemble average" is highly positive, yielding a 5% average gain per flip. However, if a single individual plays this game repeatedly over time, they will eventually go bankrupt.

This divergence highlights the difference between two types of averages: * Ensemble average: A snapshot of many subjects at a single moment in time. * Time average: The path of a single subject moving through time.

When these two averages are equal, a system is ergodic. In real life, systems are rarely ergodic. As decision theorist Nassim Taleb warns, a single individual faces "uncle points"—moments of absolute ruin or bankruptcy from which they cannot recover. In non-ergodic systems, the group can get rich while the individual gets wiped out.

Trap 1: the ergodic fallacy

The ergodic fallacy occurs when we assume that the average outcome of a group applies to an individual over time.

  • The investment trap: A highly volatile stock might show incredible average returns across 1,000 investors. However, an individual investor moving through time cannot survive a single massive drawdown that takes them to zero. Petro411 notes that Ole Peters argues this exact flaw in expected-value thinking helped drive the 2008 financial crash.
  • Taleb's warning: "Never cross a river that is, on average, four feet deep."

Trap 2: the aggregation fallacy

First popularized in psychology by researcher Peter Molenaar in 2004, the aggregation fallacy is the mistake of averaging across many people and assuming the result describes any single one of them.

  • The learning curve illusion: Standard models show learning as a smooth, elegant curve. In reality, individuals do not learn smoothly; they plateau for long periods and then jump abruptly. The smooth curve is a statistical artifact.
  • Individual variation: Researcher Aaron Fisher (2018) found that variation within a single person over time is two to four times larger than the variation between different people.
  • Medical and dietary averages: A drug or diet may be approved because it works "on average," but this average might mask a reality where it helps a small minority dramatically, does nothing for most, and actively harms others.

Trap 3: the ecological fallacy

The ecological fallacy is the mistake of inferring individual traits from group-level statistics.

  • The Robinson study (1950): Sociologist William Robinson analyzed 1930 US census data. At the state level, states with more immigrants had higher literacy rates. However, at the individual level, immigrants were actually less likely to be literate. The group-level correlation flipped because immigrants naturally settled in states where the native-born population was already highly literate.
  • The danger of stereotyping: Applying a population average (like test scores, income, or IQ) to an individual standing in front of you ignores conditional probability and the unique factors acting on that specific person.

Trap 4: Simpson's paradox

Simpson's paradox occurs when an aggregate trend completely reverses when the data is split into subgroups.

  • The UC Berkeley bias case (1973): Aggregate data suggested graduate school admissions were heavily biased against women. However, when broken down by department, the bias vanished or even favored women. The paradox occurred because women applied in much larger numbers to highly competitive departments with low acceptance rates.
  • The kidney stone study (1986): A newer, less invasive treatment had an 83% overall success rate compared to 78% for traditional surgery. Yet, when split by stone size (small vs. large), traditional surgery was superior in both categories. The aggregate data misled because the newer treatment was disproportionately given to patients with easier, small stones.

The critical checklist for decision-making

To avoid these traps, Petro411 recommends asking four questions when presented with statistical claims:

  1. The ergodic question: Is there a difference between the group average and the individual average over time?
  2. The aggregation question: Is the model of this phenomenon the same for the group as it is for the individual?
  3. The ecological question: Is it valid to make an inference about this specific individual based on their group's average?
  4. The Simpson's paradox question: When we slice and dice this data into subgroups, do the averages stay the same or do they reverse?
