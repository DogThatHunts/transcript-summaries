Rome's monetary collapse and diocletian's reforms

  • The familiar image of Rome (legions, Colosseum, stable emperors) ended with Severus Alexander (~215–235 CE), after which the Crisis of the Third Century saw 26 emperors in 50 years — "like getting a terminal cancer diagnosis."
  • Barracks generals bought loyalty with donatives; when the treasury lacked silver, they debased the denarius by adding copper, triggering Gresham's Law: good money was hoarded, prices rose, and the cycle repeated.
  • By the end of the crisis, silver content fell from ~80% to ~3%.
  • Diocletian (c. 284 CE) faced a currency with no silver left to debase. He could not pay soldiers in worthless coin, so he restructured the empire:
  • Divided administration among regional military governors called duces (origin of the title "duke").
  • Shifted taxation from coin to in kind: farmers owed grain, brewers owed beer, drovers owed transport labor.
  • Made occupations hereditary: a farmer's son must be a farmer, binding families to the land and the state's revenue needs.
  • Low asks Johnson what to call someone forced to work without pay, unable to leave or change profession, punished by armed men — Johnson answers "a slave." Low agrees: "Diocletian enslaved the entire Roman Empire, basically with the stroke of a pen."
  • This system limped on for ~175 years; when Germanic kingdoms replaced Roman authority, they kept the serfdom structure, yielding a millennium of feudalism.

Why the modern outcome may differ

  • Unproductive labor: In Rome, most people were subsistence farmers, so in-kind taxation was feasible. Today, many work in roles (HR, compliance, etc.) that cannot render payment in kind to a state.
  • Firearms level the playing field: Low cites the asymmetric cost of SWAT raids (eight armored officers for one armed individual) and notes that without "consent of the governed," enforcement becomes an Afghanistan-style insurgency where every house is a threat. He references the YouTube channel Dequitem for realistic medieval combat demonstrations showing how physical disparity dominates melee, whereas firearms drastically reduce that disparity.
  • Insufficient gold to sustain the state: The U.S. has obligated ~32.5 million tons of gold against ~8,300 tons in Fort Knox. Even total confiscation of all private gold and silver would fall orders of magnitude short of funding 11 carrier battle groups, global bases, and the existing bureaucracy. Collapse is mathematically certain and likely rapid.
  • Low expresses concern for U.S. military personnel stranded abroad (analogous to Xenophon's March of the 10,000) but hopes the Republic can persist as a loose federation of states to avoid interstate conflict.

Sound money as the test of freedom

  • Low proposes a two-question test: (1) Are you paid or can you trade in gold, silver, or honest derivatives? (2) If not, can you leave for a jurisdiction where you can? If both answers are no, "you have been enslaved."
  • He frames slavery as a spectrum: in 1970, U.S. citizens could not legally own gold bullion; today they can convert Federal Reserve notes to gold relatively easily (e.g., via Miles Franklin), so some liberty remains.
  • Historical example: J.D. Rockefeller's company towns paid workers in scrip redeemable only at the company store, trapping them — a private analog to fiat currency. Government intervention forced payment in dollars.
  • Low warns that "the hardest working slaves are the ones who think themselves free"; debt-based lifestyles keep people on a "hamster treadmill" of partial enslavement.
  • He recommends the film The Jones Plantation as an illustration.
  • Censorship and techno-dystopian controls are symptoms of the inflationary system and "will die with the dollar's death," though he cannot guarantee unbounded liberty follows.
  • Suboptimal but stable systems can persist (antebellum South; a historical Yankee planter who paid slaves in silver was murdered by neighbors defending the slave system).
