Distinction between statutes at large and the U.S. Code

Champion opens by distinguishing between Statutes at Large—the actual legislation passed by Congress—and the United States Code, which reorganizes statutes into titles and chapters. He claims that reading codified titles masks the original scope and intent of legislation like the 1935 Social Security Act, arguing that Congress never possessed constitutional authority to enact the program across the 50 states.

Statutory definition of "state" and federal jurisdiction

Examining the statutory language in the 1935 Act and subsequent Internal Revenue Code provisions (such as 26 U.S.C. § 3121(e)(1)), Champion asserts that the term "state" is defined using "includes" to encompass only federal territories, possessions, and the District of Columbia. He highlights amendments in the Alaska and Hawaii Omnibus Acts that struck those regions from special definitions upon statehood as proof that the program was intended solely for areas under exclusive federal legislative jurisdiction.

Citizenship categories and constitutional scope

Champion argues that American law recognizes distinct categories of citizenship, citing Supreme Court decisions such as Downs v. Bidwell and Dred Scott v. Sandford. He claims that native state citizens possess unalienable rights, whereas "citizens of the United States" under the Fourteenth Amendment or territorial statutes possess statutory privileges subject to congressional regulation. According to Champion, applying for a Social Security number leads the federal government to legally presume an individual is subject to federal jurisdiction.

The nature of FICA taxes and employer obligations

Analyzing Chapter 21 of Subtitle C in Title 26 (the Federal Insurance Contributions Act), Champion argues that FICA imposes an excise tax on employers. Because contracting for labor in the private sector is a constitutional right rather than a government privilege, he contends that private sector businesses in the 50 states are not legally subject to employer FICA taxes. He asserts that filing obligations under Subchapter C apply strictly to federal agencies and territorial instrumentalities.

Supreme Court rulings and the trust fund myth

Referencing Supreme Court cases Steward Machine Co. v. Davis (1937) and Flemming v. Nestor (1960), Champion notes that Social Security taxes enter general Treasury revenues rather than earmarked accounts, and that paying into the system creates no enforceable contract or property right. He further argues that the Old Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund is merely an accounting line item rather than a true legal trust (omitted from 31 U.S.C. § 1321), concluding that Social Security is funded through annual congressional appropriations similar to welfare.
