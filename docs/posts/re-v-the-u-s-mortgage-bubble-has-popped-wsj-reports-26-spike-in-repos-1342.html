Rising foreclosures and market normalization

Recent data from The Wall Street Journal indicates that U.S. foreclosure filings surged 26% year-over-year to 119,000 in the first quarter, reaching a six-year high. While current foreclosure volume remains low relative to historical post-2008 peaks and is roughly back to pre-pandemic levels, the surge arrives at a time when home prices are already dropping across more than half of the country. RE-V argues that foreclosures are a necessary and healthy mechanism for resetting inflated housing values, providing patient buyers an opportunity to purchase inventory at lower prices.

Policy shifts ending pandemic-era borrower protections

The recent spike in foreclosures follows the expiration of federal moratoriums and pandemic-era forbearance programs. RE-V notes that policy changes under the Trump administration eliminated remaining Biden-era mortgage relief clauses that allowed distressed borrowers to delay foreclosure. With federal safety nets removed, defaulted loans that were previously stalled are now entering the market, forcing price reductions on surrounding properties.

The shift from low mortgage rates to six-percent realities

Despite common narrative claims that sub-3% mortgage rates lock inventory in place permanently, RE-V emphasizes that natural market churn—driven by divorces, deaths, relocations, and refinances—is shifting the market. Mortgages with interest rates above 6% now outnumber sub-3% mortgages in Sunbelt states like Tennessee, Texas, and Florida. As more homeowners take on higher monthly debt obligations, delinquency rates and forced sales are expected to increase further.
