The return of money to the Federal Reserve

During recent Senate testimony, Federal Reserve Chair nominee Kevin Warsh noted an "Easter egg" embedded in a key Federal Reserve report: nearly an entire page dedicated to discussing the money supply, particularly aggregates like M2. While Warsh clarified that he does not present himself as a strict monetarist, he argued that monetary aggregates belong within the broader "mosaic" of data evaluated by central bankers.

Eurodollar University argues this small inclusion marks a pivotal moment. For over four decades, the Federal Reserve has conducted what it calls monetary policy without incorporating actual money into its primary econometric models, communications, or flagship policy analyses. Instead, officials substituted macro indicators—such as consumer prices, GDP, unemployment, inflation expectations, and interest rate targets—for direct observations of money and credit creation.

The rise of the offshore Eurodollar system

The fundamental reason the Fed dropped monetary aggregates from its framework was not an academic discovery that money ceased to matter, but rather an evolution in the banking system that rendered traditional measures obsolete. Beginning in the 1950s and 1960s, global commerce outgrew the domestic American regulatory perimeter, spurring the development of the offshore Eurodollar system.

In this global wholesale ledger framework, commercial banks create dollar-denominated assets and liabilities independently of physical Federal Reserve notes or domestic central bank reserves. Constrained primarily by balance sheet capacity, perceived counterparty risk, and collateral availability, international institutions created an expansive network of:

  • Interbank loans and negotiable certificates of deposit (CDs)
  • Repurchase agreements (repo) and commercial paper
  • Foreign exchange (FX) swaps and derivatives
  • Complex collateral rehypothecation arrangements

Because this wholesale network operates across global balance sheets, it largely escaped direct oversight and statistical capture by the Federal Reserve.

Why traditional aggregates failed and the 1982 pivot

Throughout the 1970s, monetary authorities attempted to redefine monetary aggregates (M1, M2, and later M3) to reflect changing financial structures. However, commercial banks continually adapted around these static metrics. An instrument classified as part of the money supply could suddenly lose monetary significance, while uncounted wholesale liabilities began functioning as effective liquidity.

By 1982, the Federal Reserve effectively abandoned quantitative money targeting and shifted to pegging the federal funds rate—the price of domestic overnight bank reserves. As former Fed Chair Alan Greenspan later acknowledged in public speeches throughout the 1990s, the historical relationship between standard monetary aggregates and economic output had broken down. Rather than solving the measurement challenge, mainstream economics pivoted toward manipulating short-term interest rates while assuming those rates would automatically guide broader financial conditions.

Eurodollar University contends that by fixing the federal funds rate, the central bank also destroyed the informational value of a market-determined interest rate, blinding itself to real-time wholesale liquidity conditions.

The consequences of central bank blindness

The absence of monetary analysis in central bank models creates systemic misinterpretations of economic events:

  • The 2008 financial crisis: Mainstream policymakers viewed the panic as an unexpected subprime issue because traditional aggregates showed nothing alarming. In reality, the breakdown was a massive contraction in global interbank balance sheet capacity, wholesale funding lines, and collateral chains.
  • The September 2019 repo spike: Sharp interest rate increases inside repo markets failed to attract dealer balance sheets, demonstrating that nominal yields cannot overcome internal balance sheet constraints.
  • Confusing interest rates with liquidity: Central banks frequently misinterpret low policy rates as "easy money," whereas market-driven low interest rates typically signal economic weakness, risk aversion, and elevated demand for safe collateral.
  • Central bank reserves vs. usable money: Reserve creation via quantitative easing (QE) is limited to bank accounts at the Fed and does not automatically translate into circulating private money or expanded dealer balance sheet capacity.

The limits of M2 and the path forward

While Warsh's reintroduction of M2 into Fed publications is an implicit admission that macroeconomic aggregates alone are insufficient, Eurodollar University emphasizes that M2 remains a domestic, retail-focused metric. It fails to monitor repo transactions, FX swaps, offshore dollar creation, or the velocity and distribution of wholesale balance sheets.

Rebuilding genuine monetary competency requires central banks to look beyond static domestic definitions and analyze the broader market signals that reflect the offshore Eurodollar system—including yield curves, money market spreads, swap spreads, and dealer behavior.
