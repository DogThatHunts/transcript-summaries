The aftermath of the Spartacist uprising in Berlin

In January 1919, Berliners voted in their first national elections amidst the ruins of the failed Spartacist uprising. The provisional government, led by Friedrich Ebert of the Social Democrats (SPD), had crushed the communist rebellion using the Freikorps—nationalist volunteer militias of war veterans and right-wing youth. The uprising's prominent leaders, Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg, were captured and executed under the orders of Captain Waldemar Pabst. While many Germans viewed the insurgents as traitors, the extrajudicial and brutal nature of Luxemburg's murder deeply unsettled both the left and moderate conservatives.

Leo Jogiches and the continuation of the communist struggle

Following Luxemburg's death, her long-time collaborator and wealthy Jewish revolutionary Leo Jogiches took control of the German Communist Party (KPD). Jogiches, who had spent decades organizing underground movements across the Russian and German empires, sought to avenge Luxemburg and Liebknecht. Despite the KPD's electoral defeat—where anti-communist parties outnumbered them overwhelmingly—Jogiches and his followers believed that revolutionary will and commitment would triumph, drawing inspiration from the Bolshevik success in Russia. By March 1919, general strikes and Soviet declarations erupted across industrial regions like the Ruhr, Bremen, and Bavaria.

The bloody March battles of Berlin

On March 3, 1919, the KPD declared open war on the German Republic, sparking a second, far more violent uprising in Berlin. Over 15,000 armed revolutionaries, including mutinous sailors from the Volksmarine Division, clashed with government forces. In response, Defense Minister Gustav Noske deployed 30,000 Freikorps and regular army troops under Captain Pabst. Noske issued a ruthless "shoot-on-sight" order for anyone caught with weapons. The ensuing urban warfare saw the use of heavy artillery, mortars, and tanks, reducing entire working-class neighborhoods to rubble. Jogiches was executed in his cell, and surrendering rebels were summarily executed. The fighting ended with up to 3,000 dead, cementing a precedent of extreme political violence.

The silent devastation of the hunger blockade

While street battles raged, Germany remained under the Allied wartime hunger blockade, which persisted months after the November 1918 armistice to force compliance with peace terms. Malnutrition and related diseases claimed between 500,000 and 700,000 German lives, with over 100,000 dying after the active shooting had stopped. This prolonged starvation, coupled with the Spanish flu epidemic, created deep psychological and physical trauma across the population. It fostered a pervasive sense of vulnerability, demonstrating to Germans that they could not feed themselves and were at the mercy of vindictive foreign powers.

The Baltic frontier and the Iron Division

On Germany's eastern border, the collapse of the Russian, Austro-Hungarian, and German empires left a geopolitical vacuum filled by ethnic conflict and the advancing Bolshevik Red Army. In the Baltic states, the remnant of the German army transformed into volunteer Freikorps units to protect the historic Baltic German minority from Bolshevik extermination. Attracted by promises of land and citizenship from the fledgling Latvian government, tens of thousands of German volunteers formed the "Iron Division." Many of these volunteers were young men who had missed the Great War and sought to wash away the "stain" of non-combat through blood, alongside hardened veterans who knew only warfare.

Kurt Eisner's bohemian revolution in Bavaria

In Catholic, conservative Bavaria, the post-war revolution took a highly unusual turn. Led by Kurt Eisner—a Prussian Jewish drama critic and bohemian intellectual—Bavaria declared itself an independent People's State in November 1918. Unlike the proletarian-led movements in Berlin, the Munich revolution was driven by Schwabing's artistic and literary counterculture. Eisner's government, filled with coffeehouse visionaries like anarchist Gustav Landauer, proved entirely inept at managing basic state functions, failing to secure food, fuel, or public order. In the January 1919 elections, Eisner's party was overwhelmingly rejected, winning only 2.5% of the vote.

The assassination of Eisner and the descent into chaos

On February 21, 1919, as Eisner walked to parliament to submit his resignation, he was shot and killed by Count Anton Graf von Arco-Valley, a 22-year-old nationalist aristocrat of half-Jewish descent seeking to prove his Germanic credentials. Eisner's assassination triggered immediate chaos; a leftist gunman shot and wounded SPD leader Erhard Auer in the parliament building, causing the government to scatter. Left-wing radicals used Eisner's death to declare the democratic elections null and void, paving the way for the proclamation of the Bavarian Soviet Republic on April 7, 1919, under the nominal leadership of 25-year-old playwright Ernst Toller.

The Jewish dimension and the rise of anti-semitism

The prominent involvement of Jewish intellectuals—such as Eisner, Toller, Landauer, and Erich Mühsam—in the Bavarian revolution fueled a rapid and virulent rise in anti-Semitism among the conservative Bavarian middle class. This tension exposed a deep generational and cultural rift within the Jewish community itself. Orthodox figures like Sigmund Frankl publicly denounced the revolutionaries, arguing that their destructive, atheist doctrines were generating a dangerous backlash against the law-abiding Jewish middle class. Meanwhile, Zionist philosophers like Martin Buber warned that the highly visible role of Jewish intellectuals in a collapsing Germany was an "unspeakable tragedy" that would inevitably invite severe retribution.

The Bolshevik takeover and the hostage massacre

The amateur "play-acting" of Toller's anarchist government was quickly brushed aside by hardened Russian Bolshevik agents, including Eugen Leviné, Max Levien, and Towia Axelrod, who established a strict dictatorship of the proletariat. Under their rule, Munich suffered severe food shortages, middle-class homes were plundered, and Russian POWs were armed to form a Red Guard. As the national army and Freikorps forces under General Ernst von Oven surrounded the city, the Bolshevik military commander, Rudolf Egelhofer, ordered the execution of ten prominent hostages, including a young countess. This brutal act shattered any remaining local tolerance for the regime.

The fall of Munich and the Baltic bloodbath

The Freikorps, led by Captain Hermann Ehrhardt's brigade, stormed Munich and crushed the Red Guard in a matter of days. Egelhofer was beaten to death by a mob, Landauer was summarily executed, and Leviné was tried and shot. The subsequent white terror saw the execution of hundreds of suspected communists and innocent bystanders. Simultaneously, in the Baltic states, the German Freikorps captured Riga from the Red Army in an incredibly savage campaign. The Baltic war was characterized by total disregard for conventional military laws, with both sides engaging in horrific mutilations, hostage killings, and the slaughter of civilians. The brutalizing experiences of these post-war conflicts, where the line between combatant and civilian disappeared, laid the psychological groundwork for the extreme violence of the decades to follow.
