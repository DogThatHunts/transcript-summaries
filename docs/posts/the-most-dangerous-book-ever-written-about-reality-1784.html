Overview

Nearly a century after Florence Scovel Shinn published The Game of Life and How to Play It, her teachings on consciousness and spiritual law continue to influence millions. Throughout, Shinn's principles are validated by modern psychology, neuroscience, and behavioral science, revealing a framework for understanding how inner consciousness crystallizes into outer reality.

  1. Life is a game, not a battle. Shinn argues that if life is a battle, strength wins; if it's a game, understanding the rules wins. This reframes suffering not as punishment but as a misunderstanding of spiritual law.

  2. Your words are a creative force. Speech is not neutral; it reveals and reinforces beliefs, impressing itself on both the subconscious mind and reality itself. Changing your words signals a shift in consciousness that precedes external change.

  3. Resistance perpetuates problems. Emotional resistance binds consciousness to unwanted conditions.   4. Imagination precedes manifestation. The unseen always comes before the seen. Holding a vision of the fulfilled state before physical evidence appears is not wishful thinking but a law of consciousness that modern neuroscience supports through studies of athletes and musicians.

  5. Faith is acceptance, not religious doctrine. Faith means believing in the certainty of what you seek and preparing for it as though it already exists—what Shinn calls "digging ditches" before the rain comes, embodying the desired state through action and expectation.

A woman struggling financially discovers that resentment and fear have dominated her consciousness; once she releases them, her situation transforms. Your word is your wand

Shinn devoted two entire books to the power of speech because she believed words are never neutral. They reveal beliefs, reinforce themselves into reality, and possess both creative and destructive capacity. Most people, she observes, speak against themselves without realizing it: "I never have enough," "Nothing works out for me," "I'm unlucky," "I always attract the wrong people." Then they are surprised when life reflects these assumptions.

For Shinn, speech does not merely describe life; it participates in creating it. Words impress themselves on the subconscious mind and the field of reality itself. The miracle someone seeks often begins when they change how they speak—not because different words are magical, but because they reveal a different consciousness. When someone stops speaking from fear and starts speaking from faith, their decisions, identity, expectations, and circumstances all shift.

The speaker connects this to modern understanding: words are crystallized vibrations of thought. If thoughts are vibrational, words are amplified frequency. They are the first representation of thought in the physical world—thought made flesh through speech. The stories we repeatedly tell about ourselves become the lens through which we perceive reality.

The speaker invites listeners to notice, over 24 hours, how often they speak negatively or misaligned with their desires: "I don't have enough time," "My body hurts," "It's going to be hard." These words represent current consciousness, and consciousness creates reality. By stopping negative speech, we "choke off the sunlight to the mind" and negative beliefs eventually dry up. Words are the doorway between inner and outer worlds, the gate determining what enters physical reality.

Non-resistance is the key to creation

One of Shinn's deepest teachings is that resistance strengthens the very thing resisted. She repeatedly directs readers to Christ's instruction: "Resist not evil." At first glance this sounds passive, but Shinn's interpretation is active: emotional resistance binds consciousness to unwanted conditions. Fear keeps fear alive. Resentment keeps the past alive. Conflict perpetuates conflict.

Instead of forcing life into submission, Shinn teaches non-resistance. Her stories show people finally receiving what they want only after letting go of fear, control, bitterness, or desperation. The external breakthrough always follows internal surrender. Letting go is not giving up; it is creating space for a higher order to enter the system and resolve the problem itself.

The speaker aligns this with his own teaching: the "golden equation" is desire plus non-resistance equals desired result. Yet the moment we want something, the mind notices its absence and explains why it's not here: "There's not enough time," "There's not enough money," "I'm not worthy," "The doctors say nobody recovers from this." If thoughts create reality, we must maintain a non-resistant state to what we desire, ignoring current reality and holding the vision steady. This is fundamental to overcoming challenges and achieving goals.

Imagination precedes manifestation

Shinn consistently taught that the unseen precedes the seen, the inner image precedes the outer condition. This is not a question of which came first; the vision must be held first, and crystallization into reality follows. She encourages people to "mentally occupy" the fulfilled state long before physical evidence appears—to hold the vision despite current circumstances.

Shinn calls this "living in anticipation" or "living in preparation": assuming what you desire already exists and living as if you were already the person who received it. She tells stories of a woman who sets her table every night in anticipation of her estranged husband's return; after two months of consistent routine, he reappears. Another woman places orders for furnishings of a new home before finding it; miraculously, she discovers her dream home.

Shinn frequently ties imagination and faith together: "Man's supply is inexhaustible and unfailing when fully trusted but faith or trust must precede the demonstration." She references scripture: "According to your faith be it unto you" and "Faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen." Faith holds the vision steady while adverse pictures dissolve.

Modern neuroscience supports Shinn's teaching. Professional athletes can imagine playing their sport and their muscle fibers respond as if training. Pianists imagining playing activate their brains as if actually playing. The mind is powerful both practically and vibrationally; what is consistently held in imagination will, given time, outpicture itself into reality. But imagination comes first; manifestation follows.

Faith is acceptance

Shinn's understanding of faith is profoundly practical: faith is not belief in an external deity but acceptance—unconditional acceptance. Faith is what you believe. If you believe in scarcity, that is your faith. If you believe in abundance, that is where your faith is placed. Faith is belief, period.

The speaker asks: Do you believe in fear more, or do you have faith in the certainty of goodness in the future? Shinn demonstrates repeatedly that people receive after they have inwardly accepted the reality they seek. You have a desire, begin preparing, start expecting, act, embody—all long before circumstances provide confirmation.

Shinn illustrates this with a biblical story of three kings whose armies march into the desert to war but run out of water. Desperate, they seek the prophet Elisha, who gives them a seemingly bizarre instruction: "Make this wadi full of ditches. For thus says the Lord, you shall not see wind, nor shall you see rain, yet that valley shall be filled with water." Already dehydrated and weak, the soldiers dig deep trenches in dry dust with no sign of clouds, wind, or rain. Yet they obey. The next morning, a supernatural flash flood fills every ditch, saves their lives, and tricks their enemies into a tactical blunder, giving the kings victory.

Shinn uses this to prove you cannot simply wish for something; you must do all the work as if you expect it. That is true faith. By digging ditches, the armies gave water a place to go. The average person asks for a miracle but checks the horizon for wind and rain before believing. Shinn argues: "The average person is always looking for results. He wants to see the wind and the rain before he digs his ditches, but the law is dig your ditches first and then expect the water."

Shinn gives practical examples: a woman seeking a job buys a work bag, plans her commuting wardrobe, prepares a desk she doesn't yet have. Someone wanting to travel abroad buys a suitcase or applies for a passport with zero dollars in the bank. A home buyer purchases a decorative piece or keychain, signaling to the subconscious that the move is already done. These physical actions train the mind to stop looking at current lack and focus entirely on expected abundance. The external world eventually catches up with internal decisions and the faith and preparation accompanying them.
