Corporate earnings, collapsing credit card balances, and stalling home sales reveal a fragile American consumer suffering from demand destruction rather than an inflationary boom.

The Pepsi warning: volume declines over nominal revenue

Pepsi's latest quarterly earnings report contained a stark warning from CEO Ramon Laguarta, who noted that the consumer is "worse than what we had anticipated," primarily driven by rising gas prices. While mainstream economic narratives point to nominal spending to argue that the consumer remains resilient, Pepsi's underlying metrics tell a different story.

In North America, Pepsi's beverage volume dropped by 4% in the second quarter, while its food business remained flat. This decline in actual units sold—rather than nominal revenue, which can be artificially inflated by higher prices—indicates genuine demand destruction. When an energy shock like rising gas prices forces consumers to cut back on everyday items like snacks and sodas, it reveals a highly fragile household budget rather than an inflationary boom.

Credit card balances hit a wall

Further evidence of consumer exhaustion appears in the Federal Reserve's own data on consumer credit. In May, revolving credit balances (primarily credit cards) fell by more than $5 billion, marking the sharpest decline in nearly two years.

While paying down debt is positive in a healthy economy, in this context, it suggests that households have hit their borrowing limits. After using credit cards and tax refunds as a temporary bridge to absorb rising costs in March and April, consumers pulled back sharply in May. This pattern represents the classic payback phase of demand destruction: consumers initially dip into savings and credit to maintain spending, but eventually must cut back discretionary purchases to regain control of their finances.

The housing market's structural weakness

The real estate sector provides another clear signal of a weakening consumer. According to the National Association of Realtors (NAR), sales of previously owned homes fell unexpectedly by 2.4% in June to an annualized rate of 4.09 million, missing economists' expectations of 4.2 million.

Although NAR Chief Economist Lawrence Yun attributed this slowdown to mortgage rate fluctuations, lower interest rates are actually a symptom of weak economic fundamentals rather than a source of stimulus. True housing demand is driven by job stability, rising incomes, and consumer confidence. With home prices flattening or slipping across major indexes (such as S&P CoreLogic Case-Shiller and the FHFA) despite tight inventory, the market is fundamentally lacking buyers who are financially secure enough to take on massive mortgages.

Retail miniaturization and the push for smaller transactions

Faced with a hesitant consumer, retailers are shifting their strategies toward "miniaturization." Companies like Lowe's and various grocers are increasingly focusing on smaller products, downsized packaging, and lower-ticket items to lower the psychological barrier to purchasing.

This shift goes beyond the traditional "lipstick effect" (where consumers buy small luxuries during downturns) to encompass practical, everyday goods. If corporations possessed genuine pricing power, they would raise prices without sacrificing volume. Instead, the prevalence of flatter volumes, price cuts, and smaller transaction sizes demonstrates that retailers must actively coax cash-strapped consumers into spending.

The Federal Reserve's inflation misunderstanding

The Federal Reserve and mainstream economists continue to view energy shocks through the lens of inflation psychology, worrying that rising oil prices will trigger a wage-price spiral. However, sustained inflation requires robust, resilient demand—something the current economy lacks.

When businesses attempt to pass higher costs onto consumers who are already broke, they face immediate resistance, resulting in lower sales volumes. Rather than signaling inflationary strength, higher energy costs act as a direct tax on households, forcing trade-offs, substitution, and credit stress. Esoteric market signals—from Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) to the flattening yield curve—consistently align with this disinflationary reality, pointing to an economy that is buckling under pressure.
