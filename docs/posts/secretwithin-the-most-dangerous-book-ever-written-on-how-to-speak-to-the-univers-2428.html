SecretWithin presents a comprehensive breakdown of Florence Scovel Shinn's The Game of Life and How to Play It, a 1925 spiritual classic that argues our thoughts, words, and beliefs directly shape our lived experience. The ten principles of the game

1. Know what you want

Shinn taught that clarity precedes change. Rather than focusing on what you don't want ("I don't want to be broke"), define what you actually do want ("I want financial security"). However, she warned against becoming so attached to one specific outcome that you inadvertently wish harm on others—for instance, hoping someone loses their job so you can have it.

The key is asking for "what is right" rather than demanding a particular solution. Shinn's affirmation captures this balance:

Infinite Spirit, open the way for my right home, my right friend, my right position. I give thanks that it now manifests under grace and in a perfect way.

This approach combines specificity with openness: you know what category of good you want, but you remain receptive to how it arrives.

2. the power of words

Shinn believed we "make laws for ourselves through our spoken word." She illustrated this with a story of a man who always said, "I always miss a car," while his daughter said, "I always catch a car"—and each experienced exactly what they repeatedly declared.

The mechanism, as SecretWithin explains it, is psychological: repeated statements create expectations, which shape attention and behavior. If you say "nothing ever works for me," you may stop trying or dismiss opportunities. If you say "I recognize and act on good opportunities," you direct your focus toward possibility.

Shinn encouraged people to follow negative statements with affirmations. For prosperity:

God is my unfailing supply, and large sums of money come to me quickly, under grace and in perfect ways.

For work:

I have a perfect work in a perfect way. I give a perfect service for perfect pay.

3. not giving your power to fear

Shinn described fear as "misdirected energy that needs to be transformed into faith." She illustrated this with a woman terrified of walking under a ladder who, after remembering Shinn's teaching, faced the fear—and found the ladder gone when she returned.

The practical lesson is not that fear magically causes events, but that fear often makes decisions for us before we act. Someone wanting to start a business thinks "what if I fail?" and never begins. Someone wanting a better job thinks "they'll reject me" and never applies.

Shinn's counter-affirmations include:

There are not two powers. There is only one power, which is God.

And when facing a burden:

I cast this burden on the Christ within and go free.

The goal is not to eliminate fear but to act despite it. Courage, in Shinn's view, means moving forward while fear is still present.

4. the law of expectation

Desire alone is insufficient if you secretly expect the opposite. You might say "I want to succeed" while thinking "people like me never get ahead." Shinn taught that expectation matters because doubt can stand between desire and manifestation.

Her teaching was to begin preparing for what you've asked for, even before visible evidence appears. She illustrated this with a woman who had only eight dollars but refused to make those eight dollars "the picture of her entire future." Instead of wishing passively, she expected abundance and followed her intuition to return home, where she reconnected with an old family friend and eventually received thousands of dollars.

The distinction: wishing says "I hope something good happens"; expectation says "I believe something good is possible, so I will prepare myself for it."

A morning affirmation to reinforce this:

Today is a day of completion. I give thanks for this perfect day. Miracles shall follow miracle and wonders shall never cease.

5. the law of non-resistance

When we constantly fight, fear, criticize, or resent a person or situation, Shinn argued, we give it more power over our minds. Instead of resisting what we don't want, we should release resistance and focus on the good we want to see.

She illustrated this with a woman struggling financially who was constantly dealing with a man who spoke negatively about money. Instead of fighting him mentally, she blessed him and the situation, affirming:

As there is only one power, that is God, this man is here for my good and my prosperity.

Unexpectedly, through this very man, she met a woman who paid her several thousand dollars for a service. Soon after, the man moved away peacefully.

The affirmation:

Every man is a golden link in the chain of my good.

Importantly, non-resistance does not mean becoming a doormat. It means setting appropriate boundaries while refusing to allow negativity to control your inner state. As Shinn puts it: "Bless your enemy and you rob him of his ammunition."

6. the law of substitution

When something doesn't work out as expected, don't assume your good is gone—make room for something better. Shinn illustrated this with a woman deeply attached to a particular man ("A.B.") whom she believed she was meant to marry. Instead of forcing that outcome, Shinn encouraged her to ask for "the right relationship" or "divine selection," affirming:

If A.B. is the right man you can't lose him and if he isn't, you will receive his equivalent.

Eventually, the woman's feelings toward A.B. changed, she met another man who embodied the qualities she'd hoped for, and she lost interest in her original choice.

The lesson applies broadly: if you don't get a job you wanted, instead of concluding "this was my only opportunity," ask "what better opportunity could take its place?" You continue improving skills, applying elsewhere, and remaining open—not giving up on your goal, but refusing to believe one particular outcome is your only path to success.

7. give up the need to control everything

Shinn taught that we often create more resistance by trying to force our personal will onto a situation. Her teaching was not to stop caring or acting, but to release the outcome after doing what we can.

She illustrated this with a mother terrified of her daughter's planned trip. The mother argued, warned, and forbade—but this only made her daughter more determined. Shinn advised the mother to "let go and take your mental hands off the situation and put it in God's hands," offering the affirmation:

I put this situation in the hands of infinite love and wisdom. If this trip is the divine plan, I bless it and no longer resist. But if it is not divinely planned, I give thanks that it is now dissolved and dissipated.

Within days, the daughter unexpectedly decided not to take the trip. For Shinn, this demonstrated the power of releasing personal will. When we stop thinking "she has to do what I want" and instead think "I have done what I can; now I release this and trust the right outcome will unfold," we create room for what she called "infinite intelligence" to work.

8. the law of karma or cause and effect

Shinn described life as "a game of boomerangs"—the idea that our thoughts, words, and actions eventually return to us. She connected this to the biblical principle: "Whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap."

She illustrated this with a woman who impatiently told her aunt, "No more talk, I wish to eat in peace." The very next day, while lunching with someone she wanted to impress, another woman said almost exactly the same thing to her. Shinn used this to show the boomerang effect: what we give out can return to us.

This doesn't mean every misfortune is punishment for past wrongs, but rather an invitation to become conscious of the causes we create through our words, attitudes, and behavior. If you criticize constantly, you may find yourself surrounded by criticism. If you give kindness and respect, you increase the likelihood of relationships built on those qualities.

Shinn connected karma with forgiveness:

I forgive everyone for everything.

I forgive myself for all mistakes, past and present.

The purpose is to stop carrying resentment and break the cycle of returning negative energy to the same situation.

9. prosperity: stop thinking in terms of scarcity

One of the biggest obstacles to prosperity, Shinn believed, is constantly thinking from a position of lack: "I don't have enough," "There is no money," "Things will only get worse." Instead of allowing present circumstances to determine future expectations, we should recognize what she called "divine supply."

Shinn illustrated this with a woman facing a $3,000 lawsuit with no idea where the money would come from. Shinn encouraged her to shift focus: God is her supply, and there is a supply for every demand. Shinn gave thanks that the woman would receive the money at the right time and in the right way. When the deadline arrived with no money visible, the woman became frightened, but Shinn encouraged her to continue acting from faith rather than panic. According to Shinn's account, the money eventually arrived unexpectedly, just when needed.

The key shift was from "I have no money and no solution" to "There is a supply, and I am open to receiving it."

Two of Shinn's best-known prosperity affirmations:

God is my unfailing supply, and large sums of money come to me quickly, under grace, in perfect ways.

As I am one with God, I am one with my good, for God is both the giver and the gift. I cannot separate the giver from the gift.

This doesn't mean ignoring financial reality or repeating affirmations while doing nothing. It means stopping scarcity from becoming your identity. Acknowledge present circumstances without making them a permanent prediction of your future.

10. Play your own game: find your perfect self-expression

The final principle is discovering what is uniquely yours. Shinn called this "perfect self-expression" or "the divine design"—the belief that each person has a unique purpose, talent, and place that no one else can fill in exactly the same way.

The affirmation:

Infinite Spirit, open the way for the divine design of my life to manifest. Let the genius within me now be released. Let me see clearly the perfect plan.

Shinn illustrated this with a man who came to her with only seven cents, gave her one, and asked her to speak the word for his perfect self-expression and prosperity. A year later, he returned successful and happy, carrying "a roll of yellow bills." He had been offered a position in another city and was now experiencing health, happiness, and supply.

The deeper lesson: he had asked for perfect self-expression rather than trying to copy someone else's path. Perfect self-expression doesn't necessarily mean a prestigious career—it might mean becoming an entrepreneur, teacher, artist, parent, writer, or craftsman. The important thing is discovering where your abilities can be expressed fully.

Don't spend your life trying to win someone else's game. Discover your abilities, develop them, follow opportunities that align with them, and allow yourself to become the person you were uniquely capable of becoming. Perfect self-expression should feel so absorbing and natural that it almost feels like play.

The 21-day play the game method

SecretWithin offers a practical seven-step challenge to test these principles:

Step 1: Choose one goal. Be specific. Don't say "I want a better life"—say "I want to increase my income to $500,000 per year." Write it down.

Step 2: Write your desired reality. Create a short statement describing the reality you want to create (e.g., "I am creating a life of financial freedom, meaningful work, and peace"). Read it every day and imagine that this future is possible.

Step 3: Watch your words. For 21 days, become conscious of the words you use. When you catch yourself saying "I can't," "nothing works for me," or "I'll never succeed," pause and replace it with words that point toward the reality you want. This isn't about denying problems; it's about refusing to turn temporary difficulties into permanent declarations about your life.

Step 4: Visualize for five minutes. Every day, spend five minutes imagining the life you want. Don't just see the final result—imagine yourself taking the actions that create it: making the phone call, doing the work, learning the skill, improving your health, building the business, or having the conversation.

Step 5: Take one action. Every day, ask yourself: "What is one thing I can do today that moves me closer to my goal?" Then do it. One application, one phone call, one workout, one page written, one new skill learned, one product created. The action is what turns the inner game into the outer game.

Step 6: Release the need to control the outcome. Do your part, but don't become obsessed with exactly how or when your goal will happen. You may have a specific destination but remain open to different routes. Sometimes the opportunity you receive may look completely different from what you originally imagined.

Step 7: Keep a proof journal. At the end of each day, write down: (1) one thing that went well, (2) one opportunity or possibility you noticed, and (3) one action you took toward your goal. After 21 days, look back and ask: What has changed in the way I think? What has changed in the way I speak? What actions am I taking now that I wasn't before? What opportunities am I noticing that I might have overlooked?
