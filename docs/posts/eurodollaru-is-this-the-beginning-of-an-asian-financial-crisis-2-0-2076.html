EurodollarU | Is This the Beginning of an Asian Financial Crisis 2.0?

A critical look at how the offshore Eurodollar system and rising energy costs are driving a quiet but dangerous dollar shortage across Asia, echoing the mechanics of the 1997 crisis.

The illusion of safety in Asian markets

Many economists and market participants believe a repeat of the 1997 Asian financial crisis is impossible. The conventional wisdom suggests that because Asian governments now hold massive foreign exchange reserves, maintain flexible exchange rates, and regulate their banking sectors more stringently, they are insulated from systemic contagion.

However, Jeff Snyder of EurodollarU argues that these governments have learned the wrong lesson. In 2026, currencies across Asia—including the Indian rupee, the Chinese yuan, and the Japanese yen—are hitting near-record lows. Despite aggressive interventions, subsidized dollar borrowing, and capital restrictions, central bank rescues are failing to stabilize these currencies. This pattern is reflected in the declining reserve assets held in custody at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, echoing the monetary mechanics that preceded the 1997–1998 crisis.

The anatomy of the 1997 Eurodollar run

To understand the current pressure, Snyder examines the structural vulnerabilities that triggered the 1997 crisis. Prior to the crash, Asian corporations borrowed heavily in cheap, short-term dollars to fund long-term, illiquid domestic projects. This created two fatal mismatches:

  • Maturity mismatch: Short-term liabilities were funding long-term assets.
  • Currency mismatch: Corporations earned revenues in local currencies (such as the Thai baht or Indonesian rupiah) but owed debts in U.S. dollars.

This system relied entirely on the willingness of global "Eurodollar" banks to continuously roll over funding. Snyder emphasizes that the Eurodollar system is not made of physical U.S. dollars, but rather virtual bank ledger money—offshore deposits, swaps, and credit lines created on international bank balance sheets. When confidence in Thailand's economy wavered in 1997, offshore lenders withdrew their dollar credit. The resulting scramble for liquidity forced institutions to liquidate local assets, driving asset prices and local currencies into a self-reinforcing downward spiral.

The 2026 catalyst: the oil-driven dollar shock

While real estate and bad bank assets triggered the 1997 crisis, Snyder identifies imported energy costs as the primary catalyst in 2026. Major Asian economies—including South Korea, Japan, India, Indonesia, and the Philippines—are highly dependent on imported oil.

When oil prices rise, these nations face a severe dollar drain. For example, purchasing one million barrels of oil at $60 per barrel requires $60 million; at $95 per barrel, it requires $95 million. This represents a 58% increase in dollar demand for the exact same physical quantity of energy. This commodity shock rapidly morphs into a systemic dollar shortage. As local currencies weaken against the dollar, imported energy becomes even more expensive in local terms, compounding the economic strain on businesses and consumers.

The limits of central bank intervention

Snyder argues that central bank interventions are fundamentally misunderstood by the financial media. When a central bank spends billions to defend its currency, it merely redistributes existing dollars to exiting investors; it cannot force private international banks to supply new credit.

India serves as a prime example of these limitations. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has utilized direct interventions, forward contracts, and high-yield programs offering up to 7.5% interest to attract foreign currency deposits from non-resident Indians. Despite these efforts, the rupee continues to weaken.

Furthermore, published reserve figures can create false confidence. India's $106.7 billion forward position represents future obligations that must eventually be delivered or rolled over. During periods of acute market stress, the distinction between gross reserves and actual, liquid dollars becomes critical. If private lenders refuse to return, central bank interventions only temporarily shift the dollar shortage onto the official balance sheet.

Historical footprints and the modern Eurodollar constraint

During the climax of the 1997 crisis, the dollar shortage reached Japan, the primary regional hub for Eurodollar redistribution. Major Japanese financial institutions failed, and the "Japan premium"—the extra interest Japanese banks had to pay to borrow dollars—surged by 110 basis points. To secure dollar cash, Japan was forced to mobilize and sell its U.S. Treasury holdings. Snyder notes that when foreign governments sell Treasuries, it is rarely due to political posturing or a loss of faith in the U.S. debt; it is a mechanical response to an acute domestic dollar shortage.

Snyder warns that the Eurodollar system of 2026 is far less elastic than it was in the 1990s. In 1997, the offshore banking system was still robust and expanding. Today, in the post-2008 regulatory era, global banks operate under severe balance sheet constraints. Dollar funding has migrated into less stable shadow banking structures, swaps, and securities financing.

Current market indicators reflect this underlying fragility:

  • The oil curve: Front-month contracts price immediate scarcity, while longer-dated contracts remain low, signaling expectations of demand destruction.
  • Treasury yields and inflation break-evens: Rising short-term yields reflect central bank rate risks, but flat inflation break-evens suggest the market does not anticipate a lasting inflationary shock.
  • Swap spreads: Tight swap spreads indicate systemic balance sheet caution rather than consumer price inflation.

Snyder cautions that central banks risk repeating past mistakes by focusing on the visible, temporary price shocks of energy while ignoring the invisible contraction of global monetary mechanics.
