Morgan stanley's housing warning vs. market reality

Morgan Stanley released a research report arguing that the U.S. housing market is undergoing an unprecedented reset at a permanently higher barrier to entry. The bank advises prospective buyers against waiting for pre-2022 affordability levels, recommending instead that buyers purchase when personal circumstances allow. They point to high mortgage-payment-to-income ratios—currently requiring around 38% of household income—and project that affordability gains will stall as long-term interest rates stay elevated.

RE-V contends that this analysis misjudges buyer behavior and ignores key historical precedents. Rather than capitulating to higher costs, buyers are simply opting out, driving a multi-year slump in demand. Existing home sales have dropped to roughly 4.17 million annualized units—levels comparable to the 2008–2009 housing crash.

Sky-high debt ratios and buyer sentiment

Data from Fannie Mae indicates that the back-end debt-to-income (DTI) ratio for home purchase and refinance closings has reached 40%. RE-V highlights that this threshold is actually higher than the peak of 39% recorded during the 2007 housing bubble. Taking on this level of leverage leaves buyers severely house-poor.

A survey conducted on the RE-V channel shows that 72% of respondents believe a sensible mortgage payment ratio should be 25% or less of gross income. Only 7% felt comfortable spending over 35%, yet today's average buyer is forced well past that threshold, sparking widespread regret among recent purchasers.

Flaws in Morgan stanley's long-term assumptions

Morgan Stanley’s forecast assumes that mortgage rates will stabilize near 5% and home price growth will slow, rather than go negative. Under these conditions, improving affordability would rely almost entirely on wage growth. To bring mortgage costs back below 30% of income without price cuts, median household income would need to rise from $85,000 to $110,000—a 28% increase that could take six to seven years.

However, RE-V argues that ignoring potential price declines is a significant oversight. Home prices are already falling in several regions:

  • Austin, Texas: Prices are down approximately 25% from peak levels.
  • Florida: Multiple metro areas are recording double-digit price declines.
  • Other key states: Prices are actively turning negative in parts of Colorado, Arizona, Washington, and Georgia.

Historical pricing context and the rise of renting

A 130-year historical comparison of home prices and mortgage rates demonstrates that inflated home prices—not interest rates—are the core issue in today's market. Long-term historical data shows:

  • Mortgage rates: The 2026 average mortgage rate of ~6.3% is close to the 130-year historical norm of 5.6%.
  • Home prices: Inflation-adjusted home prices remain 80% to 90% above their long-term historical average.

Because buying is substantially more expensive than renting in every major U.S. metro area, renter psychology is shifting. A cited CNBC report shows that 46% of renters choose to rent because it is cheaper, while 58% cite convenience as a primary factor. Renter households are increasingly choosing to invest their down payment savings elsewhere rather than taking on mortgage debt.

Demographic headwinds and market valuation

Long-term demography presents another structural challenge for the housing market. U.S. birth rates have dropped significantly over the past 35 years to about 1% annual growth, while death rates are climbing toward 0.91%. RE-V projects these trends could intersect around 2034, leading to a net population decline from natural changes. This shift will generate an influx of estate sales and baby-boomer housing inventory without a matching surge in young buyers.
