The danger of hiking rates into an energy shock

Felix argues that the Federal Reserve's decision to raise interest rates comes at a highly dangerous moment: in the middle of a major global energy and commodity shock. With oil prices elevated and the costs of diesel, fertilizer, and agricultural inputs up significantly, producers are facing severe financial pressure. Felix highlights the example of American farmers who must pay thousands of dollars just to fuel their harvesting equipment.

When input costs rise so drastically, these expenses are ultimately passed down to consumers, driving up the price of everyday goods. Felix asserts that raising interest rates into this environment does not address the root cause of the price increases, but instead adds an extra layer of financial strain on an already struggling economy.

Why supply-side inflation cannot be cured by interest rates

According to Felix, there is a fundamental difference between demand-pull inflation and supply-side inflation:

  • Demand-pull inflation: Occurs when too much consumer demand chases too little supply. Raising interest rates is an effective tool here because it cools down consumer spending.
  • Supply-side inflation: Driven by rising costs of energy, raw materials, and geopolitical disruptions. Raising interest rates does not lower the price of oil or make fertilizer more abundant.

By raising rates during a supply shock, the central bank merely punishes borrowers—such as families with mortgages, small businesses, and farmers—forcing them to cut back on spending. This chokes economic growth while prices remain high, a phenomenon known as stagflation. Felix points out that this exact policy mistake was made during the oil shocks of the 1970s, a decade in which gold prices ultimately surged from $100 to $850 per ounce.

The sovereign debt trap: why the Fed cannot pull a Volcker

Felix explains that the US government is trapped by the sheer scale of its own debt. In the coming 12 months, approximately $8 trillion of government debt (treasuries) must be repaid and refinanced. Much of this older debt was issued at interest rates around 3.3%, but must now be refinanced at rates closer to 5%. This transition alone adds an estimated $130 billion annually to the government's interest expenses.

Many market commentators point to former Fed Chair Paul Volcker, who successfully crushed inflation in the early 1980s by raising interest rates into the double digits. However, Felix notes a critical difference: in Volcker's era, the US national debt was only about 30% of GDP. Today, the debt-to-GDP ratio stands at roughly 120%. Because the debt load is so high, Felix argues that keeping interest rates elevated for a prolonged period would bankrupt the government, meaning the Fed cannot replicate Volcker's playbook.

The quiet reset: inflating away the national debt

Because the government cannot afford high interest rates and cannot easily pay down its massive debt, Felix claims that policymakers will resort to an alternative playbook: inflating the debt away.

By allowing inflation to run hot while publicly maintaining a tough stance, the nominal size of the economy grows while the real value of the government's debt shrinks. Felix argues that this strategy rewards highly indebted institutions while quietly penalizing cash savers, whose purchasing power melts away. He views minor rate hikes as economic theater designed to project control while the underlying currency is systematically devalued.

How smart money is preparing: the central bank gold rush

Felix observes that while everyday savers are encouraged to keep their money in cash or traditional bonds, central banks and institutional investors are quietly moving in the opposite direction. Central banks are currently purchasing gold at the fastest rate seen this century.

Felix warns against the "cash trap," pointing out that since the US dollar was completely unlinked from gold in 1971, it has lost the vast majority of its purchasing power. To protect wealth from the ongoing monetary reset, he advocates for holding hard assets like gold and silver. Additionally, he cautions investors to review their stock portfolios, noting that many who believe they are diversified are actually heavily over-concentrated in a handful of expensive technology and AI stocks through standard index funds.
