SecretWithin presents a lecture by David Deutsch, an Oxford physicist, who uses a laser interference experiment to argue for the existence of parallel universes. Deutsch contends that quantum mechanics reveals not merely a single reality, but countless coexisting worlds that interact and interfere with one another. The piece traces the philosophical and experimental foundations of this claim, then explores its implications for identity, consciousness, and the possibility of time travel.

The laser experiment and interference patterns

Deutsch demonstrates a simple yet profound experiment using a laser pointer and a cardboard screen with pinprick holes. When a single hole is present, one might expect a single spot of light on the screen. Instead, a complicated interference pattern appears. When a second hole is added, the pattern changes entirely. Adding more holes—three, four, six, a hundred, a thousand—produces different patterns each time.

The puzzle deepens when the experiment is performed with individual photons sent one at a time. Despite traveling separately, the photons collectively produce the same interference pattern as a continuous beam. This should be impossible if light were purely particle-like: each photon should pass through one hole or another, leaving two simple clusters. Instead, each particle appears to pass through every hole simultaneously and somehow "knows" whether other holes are open.

The invisible light hypothesis

Deutsch argues that something undetectable must be traveling through the unopened holes and interfering with the photons we can see. This invisible phenomenon behaves exactly like light in every measurable way, yet cannot be directly detected. The only reasonable conclusion, he contends, is that invisible versions of light exist and are genuinely real—not illusions or mathematical conveniences.

This invisible interference affects not only photons but all particles, including the atoms in solid matter and molecules in our bodies. The implication is staggering: entire parallel worlds of matter, energy, and light must exist alongside our own.

The multiverse framework

Deutsch proposes that at the moment of the Big Bang, countless universes came into being, initially almost identical. Over time, through interference and interaction, they diverged. The analogy is apt: imagine countless books printed with the same first chapter, each gradually telling a different story. Our universe is simply one variation among infinite others.

Crucially, Deutsch emphasizes that these parallel universes are not imaginary or merely mathematical constructs. They are just as real as the world we inhabit. The fact that we cannot directly perceive them does not prove their absence—it only shows that human perception is not tuned to them. Deutsch compares this to the Earth's rotation: we cannot feel it beneath our feet, yet instruments like Foucault's pendulum indirectly confirm it.

Identity across the multiverse

When asked what it would mean for a person to exist in multiple universes, Deutsch reframes the question. One must think of oneself not as an entity existing in a single universe, but as an entity existing in the multiverse as a whole. There are other copies of you in other universes. Initially, these copies are completely identical. But each time a choice occurs—whether at the level of a random atomic event or a conscious decision—the copies begin to separate.

Picture walking down a street in Oxford with a choice to turn left or right. In half the universes you turn left; in the other half you turn right. Both outcomes are real because both are taken. Deutsch argues it is misleading to imagine the universe "splitting" at that moment. Instead, all those universes already exist, and our choices reveal which one we are currently living through.

This view reshapes the concept of identity. Rather than a single fixed entity, identity becomes one branch within a wider landscape of possibilities. Some variations are so slight—perhaps a single atom out of place—that they are almost invisible. Others, farther away in the multiverse, become so different that they might no longer resemble you at all.

Addressing skepticism

Skeptics often ask: if these other universes exist, why can't we see or feel them? Deutsch's response is that science is not merely about predicting experimental results; it is about finding the explanation that actually fits the facts. When it comes to quantum mechanics, the only explanation that truly works is the existence of multiple universes.

He acknowledges that the idea sounds bizarre and that new advances in science move further and further away from common sense. However, if one wants only to predict the outcomes of experiments without understanding why they occur, one need not believe in multiple universes—or even in multiple planets or anything outside the room. But if one wants to explain, one must adopt the explanation that meets the facts.

The effects of parallel universes are present all around us, revealed through interference patterns and the strange behaviors of particles. If human senses happened to work through quantum interference, we would be able to feel the influence of parallel universes directly. Deutsch compares the skeptic's position to the Inquisition telling Galileo that they do not feel the Earth moving beneath their feet—a misunderstanding of why that sensation does not occur, not proof that the motion is absent.

Time travel and the paradox problem

The existence of parallel universes naturally raises the question of time travel. Is it physically possible to build a time machine that creates a path from the present into the past? Deutsch acknowledges this remains an open question, but notes that physical processes—such as the tearing of space-time near a rotating black hole—might theoretically allow such a pathway.

However, time travel immediately confronts classic paradoxes. If you travel back and prevent yourself from building the time machine, who made the journey? How could the effect exist without the cause? These contradictions have long been seen as proof that time travel is impossible.

Yet the multiverse framework offers a resolution. Deutsch argues that the paradox disappears when we understand time travel not as altering our own past, but as entering the past of a different universe. In the multiverse, all possible histories exist side by side. If you go back and change something, that change takes effect in a parallel reality, not in the one you came from. The original timeline continues untouched. You still built the time machine and made the journey, but in the new universe, events unfold differently.

The one-way journey

When asked whether he would use a time machine, Deutsch declines. The reason is sobering: when you travel in time, you cannot get back to your original universe. At minimum, you would end up in a universe where another copy of you—the one who never entered the time machine—already exists. In your original universe, your friends would see you enter the machine and never emerge. From their perspective, you would be lost forever.

In the destination universe, a different group of the same people would suddenly have two copies of you: the one who was always there and the newcomer who just arrived from the past. They might not consider this an improvement.

Time travel would never allow us to rewrite history or undo mistakes. Instead, it would mean leaving one universe behind and entering another where a different version of events begins. The journey is one way. Once you leave, you cannot return to your original timeline. The implication is that time travel, if possible, is not about fixing the past but about stepping sideways into another branch of reality where history takes a new shape while the old one continues without you.
