Goliath: the Philistine champion

SecretWithin opens by establishing Goliath's legendary proportions. According to 1 Samuel 17:4, Goliath stood six cubits and a span tall—approximately nine feet nine inches in modern measurement. This stature was paired with formidable armor: a bronze helmet, a coat of scale armor weighing 125 pounds, bronze greaves, and a massive spear with an iron head weighing 15 pounds. His chainmail and enormous shield-bearer completed an image of overwhelming military dominance.

For forty days, Goliath marched out morning and evening, taunting the Israelites and daring them to send a champion. His boasts were backed by a formal challenge: if an Israelite could defeat him, the Philistines would become servants to Israel; if Goliath prevailed, Israel would serve the Philistines (1 Samuel 17:8–9). The psychological warfare was relentless, leaving the Israelite army paralyzed by fear.

David's unlikely rise

Meanwhile, in the hills near Bethlehem, the young shepherd David was sent by his father Jesse to deliver supplies to his three older brothers serving in Saul's army. Upon arriving at the camp, David witnessed Goliath's latest taunt and was struck by anger at the giant's defiance of "the armies of the living God." When he learned of the promised rewards for defeating Goliath, David asked what would be done for the man who accomplished this feat.

David's confidence alarmed King Saul, who protested that David was merely a boy facing a lifelong warrior. But David replied with conviction, recounting how he had defended his father's sheep against lions and bears. He declared that the Lord who rescued him from those beasts would deliver him from Goliath's hand. Moved by David's faith, Saul gave him his blessing and offered his own armor—which David quickly rejected as too cumbersome. Instead, David chose his shepherd's staff, a sling, and five smooth stones from a nearby stream.

The confrontation and victory

As David approached the valley of Elah, Goliath's contempt was immediate. The giant mocked the youth, asking if David thought he was a dog to come at him with stones, and cursed him by his gods, threatening to feed his flesh to the birds and beasts (1 Samuel 17:43–44).

David's response was a declaration of faith. He told Goliath that while the giant came with sword, spear, and shield, David came "in the name of the Lord of hosts, the God of the armies of Israel, whom thou hast defied." He proclaimed that the Lord would deliver Goliath into his hand, and that he would take the giant's head and give the Philistine corpses to the birds and beasts, so "all the earth may know that there is a God in Israel" (1 Samuel 17:45–46).

Without waiting for Goliath to close the distance, David ran toward the battle line, placed a stone in his sling, and let it fly. The stone struck Goliath's forehead with devastating precision. The giant fell face-first to the ground. David then drew Goliath's own sword and severed his head.

The Philistine army, seeing their champion defeated, fled in terror. The Israelites pursued them to the gates of Ekron and Gath, plundering the abandoned Philistine camp. David carried Goliath's head as a trophy to Jerusalem, a symbol of God's deliverance.

The Rephaim are referenced nearly twenty times in the Bible (Deuteronomy 2:20–22; Joshua 12:4). One notable figure was Og, king of Bashan, described as the last remnant of these ancient giants. Lahmi's defeat came at the hands of Elhanan, whose name means "God is gracious." Though scripture provides only a brief account, the victory suggests that Elhanan, armed with skill and trust in the God of Israel, triumphed over a seemingly unstoppable foe. Elhanan secured his place among David's mighty men, and his victory reinforced the central truth that no matter the size of the enemy, Israel's God was mightier still.

The second Goliath: a scribal mystery

The fourth giant presents a puzzle for biblical scholars. According to 2 Samuel 21:19, "there was again a battle in Gob with the Philistines, where Elhanan the son of Jarioregim, a Bethlehemite, slew the brother of Goliath of Gath, the staff of whose spear was like a weaver's beam."

If this Goliath was indeed a distinct individual, his legacy was as daunting as his famous counterpart—he carried a colossal spear described as "like a weaver's beam," a weapon requiring extraordinary strength to wield. Elhanan, a Bethlehemite, rose to the challenge and struck down this towering figure, marking another triumph for Israel.
