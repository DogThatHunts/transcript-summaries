Lesson 8: self-control and thought mastery

Napoleon Hill introduces self-control as the essential balance wheel to enthusiasm. While enthusiasm acts as the vital force that impels a person to action, self-control directs that action constructively so that it builds up rather than tears down. To illustrate the consequences of lacking self-control, Hill cites his own survey of 160,000 adult inmates in United States penitentiaries, claiming that 92% of them were incarcerated because they lacked the self-control to direct their energies constructively.

Hill shares a formative personal story about a feud with his office janitor, who would turn off the lights while Hill was working late. When Hill finally confronted the janitor with a furious tirade, the janitor responded with calm poise, asking, "Why, you all's just a little bit excited this morning, ain't you?" This interaction deeply humiliated Hill, a boasted student of psychology, and forced him to apologize. The experience taught him a core tenet of his philosophy: no one can control others until they first control themselves.

Hill argues that self-control is fundamentally a matter of thought control. Because thought is the only function over which humans have absolute and undisputed control, individuals must guard their minds against negative outside suggestions and consciously choose the positive, constructive thoughts that align with their definite chief aim.

The law of retaliation and moral poise

Hill defines the "law of retaliation" as a natural human tendency to return like for like. If treated with hostility, people naturally retaliate with hostility; conversely, if treated with kindness, they feel compelled to reciprocate in kind. Hill demonstrates this principle through a simple experiment with his young sons, Napoleon Jr. and James, showing how a gesture of sharing a treat naturally dissolved a physical conflict and prompted mutual sharing.

To highlight the power of moral poise under extreme pressure, Hill recounts the story of a New England clergyman during the 1863 Lawrence massacre. Confronted by one of Quantrell's guerrillas who intended to shoot him, the clergyman calmly admitted his political beliefs but immediately shifted the conversation to a moral issue, telling the raider he should be ashamed of his actions. Instead of executing the clergyman, the guerrilla sat down to talk, eventually returning the clergyman's valuables, apologizing, and asking to be thought well of. Hill argues that by refusing to meet physical force with physical force, the clergyman dominated the situation using a moral power the brigand could not counter.

The evolution of transportation

In an "after-the-lesson visit," Hill reflects on the law of evolution, stating that "nothing is permanent except change." He traces the history of transportation from human-drawn carts and bullocks to stagecoaches, locomotives, automobiles, and airplanes. He asserts that all human progress begins with the state of being "not satisfied," which stimulates the mind to creative action.

Hill offers a specific prophecy: within five years of his writing, every railroad crossing in the United States will be protected by an automatic, foolproof gate system operated by the weight of passing automobiles. He encourages readers to use their imagination to find ways to divert waste motion and power into useful channels, noting that progress is driven by either the urge of necessity or the desire to create.

Lesson 9: the habit of doing more than paid for

Hill argues that performing work with love eliminates fatigue and dramatically increases efficiency. He notes that while a day laborer may quickly tire of work they dislike, an artist can labor for hours without exhaustion because they love their task. Therefore, Hill advises everyone to find the work they love most.

Hill recounts his own struggles to establish the Law of Success philosophy. Despite facing intense opposition from his family, who urged him to secure a stable, salaried position, Hill remained dedicated to his unpaid research. A major turning point occurred when he delivered a passionate speech to an audience of only 13 people in Canton, Ohio. One of the attendees was publisher Don R. Mellett, who was so impressed by Hill's dedication that he became his business partner. This partnership eventually led to Judge Albert Gary of the United States Steel Corporation purchasing the course for his employees, proving that Hill's unpaid "labor of love" eventually yielded massive returns.

The law of increasing returns in action

Hill explains the "law of increasing returns" using the analogy of a farmer sowing wheat to harvest a manifold increase. He argues that by rendering more and better service than what they are paid for, individuals stand out in sharp contrast to the majority of workers who do only the bare minimum. This contrast naturally creates keen competition for their services.

Hill highlights several historical examples to support this claim: * Carol Downs: Promoted himself from a minor position to president of an automobile distribution company by voluntarily staying late to assist manufacturer W.C. Durant. * Edward W. Bok (referred to as Bach in the transcript): The editor of the Ladies' Home Journal, who rose from extreme poverty by working tirelessly at any opportunity and using every rung of the ladder to climb higher. * Napoleon Hill: Refused a $100 lecture fee at the Palmer School, which prompted the school's president to publicly endorse Hill's magazine, ultimately generating over $50,000 in subscription revenue.

Hill challenges readers to conduct a six-month experiment: render a useful service to at least one person every day without expecting or accepting monetary payment. He promises that this practice will inevitably set the law of increasing returns to work, eventually prompting the world to pay them for more than they actually do.

The master mind and coordinated mental power

Hill defines the "Master Mind" as an alliance of minds blended in a spirit of perfect harmony to achieve a definite objective. He claims that the human brain acts as both a broadcasting and receiving station for thought vibrations, and that a group of minds working in harmony can access a level of creative power far beyond that of any single individual.

Hill attributes the immense success of Henry Ford, Thomas Edison, and Harvey Firestone to the master mind they created through their close, harmonious friendship. He similarly attributes the success of Chicago's "Big Six" business leaders (including William Wrigley Jr. and John R. Thompson) to this blended mental power. Hill traces this principle back to Jesus Christ, who established the first "thirteen club" by surrounding himself with his twelve disciples, creating a harmonious alliance that allowed his teachings to endure for centuries.
