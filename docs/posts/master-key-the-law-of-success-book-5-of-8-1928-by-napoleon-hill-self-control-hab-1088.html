Master Key | The Law of Success, Book 5 of 8 (1928) by Napoleon Hill - "Self-Control & Habit of Doing More"

Hill's lessons on self-control and doing more than paid for: master your thoughts and temper, return kindness for injury, and let unpaid service compound into reward.

Book 5 of 8 of Napoleon Hill's The Law of Success (1928), read by Dan John Miller for the Master Key Society. The ideas are Hill's, reported as he states them, not endorsed or verified here. Every timestamp opens the video a few seconds early.

Book 5 holds two lessons: Lesson 8, Self-Control, and Lesson 9, the Habit of Doing More Than Paid For. Each is followed by an "after-the-lesson visit with the author": an essay on the evolution of transportation, and an essay on the Master Mind. The recording closes with the inspirational quotations printed through the book.

Hill joins the lessons deliberately. Enthusiasm, the subject of Lesson 7, is raw power, and self-control is "the balance wheel" that directs it. The central claim of Lesson 8 is that "self-control is solely a matter of thought control": a person chooses the thoughts that dominate the mind through auto-suggestion and shuts out the suggestions of others. Hill extends this into the "law of retaliation", the principle that people return like for like. Lesson 9 then asks for "the strongest sort of self-control" to apply the law of increasing returns. Service given beyond what one is paid for comes back multiplied, and the habit of giving it builds strength and reputation.

Both lessons draw heavily on Hill's own life: his humiliation by a janitor, his years of unpaid research against his family's wishes, and the audience of thirteen in Canton, Ohio, that led to his partnership with the publisher Don R. Mellett. Henry Ford, Marshall Field, W. C. Durant and Edward W. Bok are recurring examples. Lesson 9 ends on Christmas Eve with an appeal to the Sermon on the Mount.

Lesson 8: Self-Control [00:42]

Enthusiasm needs a balance wheel [00:49]

Enthusiasm without self-control, Hill writes, "resembles the unharnessed lightning of an electrical storm" [01:08]. He cites a survey he says he has just completed of 160,000 adult inmates of United States penitentiaries. By his count, 92% are in prison because they lacked the self-control to direct their energies constructively [01:37]. He points to the Bible's "law of non-resistance" [02:09] and to Lincoln, who overlooked personal disloyalty in his cabinet for the country's sake [02:32]. The main cost of lacking self-control, he says, is that its victims "suffer the loss of a great power which they need in their struggle for achievement of their definite chief aim" [03:28].

The janitor who won the argument [03:58]

A janitor in Hill's office building took to switching off the lights while Hill worked alone. One Sunday Hill stormed into the basement and cursed him for five minutes. The janitor, shovelling coal, answered calmly, "Why, you all's just a little bit excited this morning, ain't you?" [05:28]. Hill, "a boasted student of advanced psychology", had been beaten by a man who could not read, in a duel fought with a weapon of Hill's own choosing. When Hill went back to apologize, the janitor offered to keep the matter quiet, which hurt more [07:38]. Hill resolved never again to be humiliated through his own loss of control. He reports that his writing gained power and that he made more friends. The lesson he drew was "that no man can control others unless he first controls himself" [09:17].

Self-control in selling and everyday trade [10:01]

Lack of self-control is "the average salesman's most damaging weakness." Hill's examples are short. A Chicago store staffed its complaint desk with a stone-deaf woman, fed written notes stripped of the customers' anger, because no one else had the self-control for the post [10:18]. Trial lawyers anger hostile witnesses until they say too much [12:42]. On a train, a calm old man stirring an argument about Tammany boss Richard Croker turned out to be Croker himself [13:44]. "Men who control themselves usually boss the job, no matter what it may be" [14:38]. A printer lost an order worth $10,000 in profit by snapping "you Bolsheviks" at a Russian client, then blamed his Thanksgiving turkey [16:09]. A clerk who courteously looked after an old woman sheltering from rain in a Pittsburgh store was later requested by name to furnish a mansion in Scotland, because she was Andrew Carnegie's mother [19:44]. Hill also tells of a school solicitor who complained of "no opportunity". Hill arranged group talks for him, and the man enrolled fifteen students in six hours. Hill credits imagination (Lesson 6), a definite plan, self-confidence and action [25:16].

What a person of self-control does not do [25:43]

Such a person does not indulge in hatred, envy, jealousy, fear or revenge. He does not become "ungovernably enthusiastic," let self-confidence or self-sacrifice run to excess, or place his happiness wholly in another's hands. He does not let others think for him, never slanders, and does not hate those who disagree. The worst form of lack of self-control, "the habit of forming opinions before studying the facts," is left to Lesson 11, Accurate Thought [28:07].

The spending habit [28:58]

Hill calls spending beyond one's needs "alarming" since the World War. He cites an unnamed economist who warns that three generations could make the richest country the poorest [29:13]. He criticises instalment-plan automobiles bought instead of homes, and cites a survey of 600 employees in which only 9% had savings [30:12]. "The spending habit is turning America into a nation of paupers" [33:14]. A small savings account "will attract many an opportunity"; what counts is the habit [33:28]. A man whose income rises from $3,000 to $4,000 and buys a dearer car ends the year poorer [33:49]. The reader should find the "happy medium" between miser and spendthrift [34:43].

Policy toward enemies [35:31]

When a "destroyer" attacked his reputation, Hill typed a principle: "You have a tremendous advantage over the man who does you an injury. You have it within your power to forgive him" [36:11]. He decided to let his work be his "sole answer" to critics [36:43], noting that Harding, Wilson and John H. Patterson ignored their attackers [38:19]. Once, after years of a journalist's attacks, he broke his policy and wrote the most biting reply he could. At the end he felt only "compassion, of sympathy, of forgiveness". He had "unconsciously psychoanalyzed" himself [39:53]. Now he writes anger out and discards the page, since repressed hatred is like a bomb [40:30].

Thought control: the core of the lesson [42:27]

Man, "the product of at least a million years of evolutionary change", has been given thought, perhaps "the closest connecting link" between the material world and divinity [42:06]. The brain is started by two stimuli. In auto-suggestion you choose the material of your thinking; in suggestion you let others choose it [43:00]. Most people take in newspapers and gossip unexamined, and only about one in 10,000 keeps the door of the mind shut [43:50]. Then comes what Hill calls "the most important single sentence of this entire course": "Self-control is solely a matter of thought control" [45:14]. Plant thoughts that harmonize with your definite chief aim, and the mind "will transform those thoughts into physical reality" [45:54]. You are "but the sum total of your dominating or most prominent thoughts" [46:55].

The master salesman [47:06]

The man of self-control does not hear "no", even the fourth time, and "stays on the job until he breaks down the opposition and forces a yes" [47:46]. He "takes the offensive and never the defensive side of an argument" [48:50]. "We are all salesmen." The outstanding quality of successful people is the ability "to negotiate with other people without friction and argument" [50:15]. Hill shows it in the speaker, the physician, the foreman and the lawyer [51:35], and concludes that "self-control is the result of thought control" [52:58].

Desire, the creed and closing points [54:36]

You control anger the way you change your tone when the doorbell rings mid-quarrel, because you want to [53:54]. Behind all self-control is "that magic something called desire." Hill lists people whose desire, he says, became reality, from Napoleon, Lincoln and Edison to Helen Keller and James J. Hill [55:40]. He then offers a creed for the reader's code of ethics: find fault with no one sincerely trying, base expectations of reward on service, avoid procrastination, and be "courteous to men, faithful to friends, true to God" [57:25].

"All successful people grade high on self-control. All failures grade low, generally zero" [59:37]. Hill warns against talking too much [1:00:08]. He says Luther Burbank called self-control the most important of the fifteen laws, and John Burroughs ranked it near the top [1:01:20]. He reads Edward W. Bok's essay "Why I Believe in Poverty as the Richest Experience that Can Come to a Boy" [1:02:44]: an immigrant boyhood in Brooklyn, sifting coal ashes, selling lemonade to horse-car passengers. Bok's verdict is that poverty is a blessing "as a condition to experience, to go through, and then to get out of, not as a condition to stay in" [1:10:15].

The law of retaliation [1:12:18]

Hill uses "retaliate" to mean returning like for like, favours as well as injuries [1:12:28]. "Through the proper use of this law, I can get you to do whatever I wish you to do." A pleasing personality is the same law: "like attracts like" [1:13:48]. If you answer abuse in kind, "you are being drawn down to that person's mental level". If you stay calm, "you retaliate with a weapon with the use of which he is unfamiliar" [1:14:35]. When Hill's son James grabbed his brother's peanuts, Napoleon Jr. punched him. When James offered his Cracker Jack instead, Napoleon Jr. poured peanuts into James's pocket. Hill calls this his "greatest lesson in salesmanship" [1:15:28]. Enemies can be converted by unusual cordiality: "The hottest coals of fire ever heaped upon the head of one who has wronged you are the coals of human kindness" [1:18:30]. During the Lawrence, Kansas, massacre of 1863, a young clergyman admitted to one of Quantrill's guerrillas that he was an abolitionist and told the raider he ought to be ashamed. The raider returned his valuables and asked to be "thought well of" [1:18:38].

Hill allows that it may be necessary "to meet force with force until we overpower our opponent", then offer a hand [1:22:36]. He cites Germany's defeat as retaliation in kind [1:22:55], and concludes that "we receive only that which we give" [1:23:16]. The lesson's summary calls this "probably the most important major principle of psychology" [1:23:48].

After the lesson: The Evolution of Transportation [1:25:55]

The essay opens with aphorisms, including "Nothing is permanent except change" and "Honesty and dishonesty are largely matters of individual viewpoint" [1:26:01]. "Not satisfied" is the starting point of all advancement [1:27:25]. Hill separates two urges, necessity and the desire to create [1:30:29]. He predicts that every railroad crossing will be automatically protected within five years [1:32:35], warns parents that removing necessity may deprive "the world of a genius" [1:35:09], and claims that the mind expands every seven years [1:36:21].

Lesson 9: The Habit of Doing More Than Paid For [1:39:15]

Love and work [1:39:24]

Work done for love brings less fatigue and better results. Hill states "one of the most important laws of this philosophy": "a man is most efficient and will more quickly and easily succeed when engaged in work that he loves" or does for someone he loves [1:40:45]. A Swede in a Louisiana co-operative colony liked no job until he was given a wheelbarrow in the brickyard [1:41:15].

Hill's own labor of love [1:44:01]

For over a quarter of a century Hill researched the philosophy, much of it unpaid [1:44:36]. The most "disastrous opposition" came from his wife, backed by both families, who wanted him in a salaried post [1:46:50]. Edwin C. Barnes, Thomas A. Edison's associate, kept him going [1:49:10]. Hill says his debts from those years are why Lesson 4 stresses slavery through debt [1:50:12].

In Canton, Ohio, Hill gave his best speech to an audience of thirteen [1:52:43]. One listener, Don R. Mellett of the Canton Daily News, asked for his whole life story, "the fat with the lean" [1:54:16]. Hill confessed he had accumulated "nothing but experience and knowledge and a few debts". Mellett told him to know the difference "between failure and temporary defeat" [1:57:31]. They became partners. Hill's editorial "Failure" reached Judge Elbert H. Gary of U.S. Steel, who offered to buy the course for his employees [1:58:43]. Mellett was assassinated and Gary died. Even so, Hill lists firms buying or considering the course, among them the Baltimore & Ohio, Standard Oil, New York Life, Pierce-Arrow and Cadillac, and a publisher with a mailing list of 800,000 [2:00:10].

Two reasons above all others [2:02:22]

First, a reputation for doing more creates "keen competition for your services" [2:02:41]. Second, and more fundamental, strength comes only from use. A right arm tied to one's side withers, and "out of resistance comes strength" [2:03:44]. An employee with the habit can "practically set your own wages" [2:05:26], while those who merely "get by" are dismissed first [2:06:11]. Hill "never received a promotion" he could not trace to it [2:06:52]. "If you receive no pay except that which comes in your pay envelope, you are underpaid" [2:07:46]. He urges readers to buy Russell H. Conwell's Observation: Every Man His Own University [2:07:56].

The law of increasing returns [2:09:26]

The farmer sows wheat and reaps "a manifold increase", and service works the same way, with "no trickery or chicanery" [2:10:27]. Buyers of service can use it too. Hill says Ford's five-dollar day was sound business, not philanthropy, and guesses, while admitting he has no figures, that it returned $7.50 of service per $5 [2:10:56]. Marshall Field's store took back a lace waist two years late and won a customer and her word of mouth, under the slogan "The Customer is Always Right" [2:12:47]. Carol Downs (the transcript's spelling) stayed late at W. C. Durant's office unasked until Durant "got into the habit of calling on me". He became Durant's right-hand man [2:15:18]. Hill's own best promotion came from skipping a ball game to type a lawyer's urgent work. Six months later the lawyer hired him at $1,000 a year more [2:18:12]. "It was not my duty to give up my Saturday afternoon, but it was my privilege" [2:19:48]. One unpaid hour a day, he concludes, can out-earn the rest of the day [2:20:28].

Removing the mountain [2:21:26]

Hill asks for real faith, not that of the woman who ordered a mountain to move and said in the morning, "Just as I had expected" [2:21:42]. The mountain is "the feeling that you have been cheated unless you received material pay for all the service you render" [2:22:49]. Success follows laws "as immutable as is the law of gravitation."

Evidence from Hill's career [2:26:49]

A college secretary summed Hill up: "He is putting himself ahead in the world by first helping others to get ahead" [2:27:16]. As an adman, Hill rewrote firms' sales letters unasked, which "invariably" brought work [2:27:37]. At the Palmer School in Davenport, Iowa, he refused his $100 fee. Dr. Palmer told 2,000 students, and over $50,000 in magazine subscriptions followed [2:29:29]. "Let me show you the color of my service" [2:32:24]. A 1917 stenographer on $15 a week now clears over $100,000 a year lecturing, by giving fifteen free lectures before forming a paid class [2:32:38].

The hardest taskmaster [2:37:05]

The habit needs no one's permission, so the reader has no alibi [2:37:51]. "Every person should be his own hardest taskmaster" [2:38:19]. We court flattery: a man who paid Hill for an honest book review never forgave him [2:39:04]. The reader has not applied enough of Lesson 8's self-control to charge himself with his own mistakes [2:39:56], and has quit jobs rather than master obstacles [2:41:42]. Hill thanks God for his enemies [2:42:57] and quotes Emerson's Compensation: "Our strength grows out of our weakness" [2:43:35].

Ben-Hur, Ford and Christmas Eve [2:45:53]

Ben-Hur's galley oar built the arms that won his chariot race and freedom [2:47:02]. "It is an unvarying law that strength grows out of resistance" [2:47:46]. Ford's real wealth, Hill says, is his reputation for useful service at a reasonable price. By lowering prices and raising wages when others do the opposite, he has become "the richest and most powerful man in the world" [2:48:29]. Writing on Christmas Eve, Hill finds the strongest support for the lesson in the Sermon on the Mount [2:50:44]. "Compound interest upon compound interest is the rate that you will be paid for such service" [2:53:58]. "You can never become a leader without doing more than you are paid for" [2:54:30].

After the lesson: The Master Mind [2:54:42]

The essay sets Niagara's physical power against the "much more intensive" power of minds coordinated "in a spirit of perfect harmony" [2:54:58]. After a survey of elements, atoms and electrons, Hill predicts that "the next great scientific discovery" will be that every brain is "both a broadcasting and a receiving station" [2:58:55]. He adds that the ether holds every thought ever released, as "nature's bible" [3:00:00]. Fewer than a hundred people know the "chemistry of the mind" by which harmonious minds form a third mind [3:01:25]. Ford, Edison and Firestone, Hill says, have formed one, perhaps without knowing it, and it is "the cause of the success of each of the three" [3:05:48]. Twelve or thirteen minds are ideal [3:05:27]. Chicago's "Big Six", including Wrigley, Lasker and Hertz, are a second example [3:06:21]. Christ "discovered" the principle with "the first thirteen club of the world" [3:07:53].
