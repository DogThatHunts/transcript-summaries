Money principles i know today but wish i knew in my 20s

Swadia shares eleven money rules that shaped his path from growing up in Mumbai to working in companies that created over $60 billion in value. These principles prioritize psychological foundations, disciplined planning, and relationship-building over conventional financial hacks.

Rule 1: get in the right headspace (HEAD)

The first rule rests on four pillars: hope, extraction, abundance, and discipline.

Hope is the driving force that keeps you moving forward. Swadia cites Shah Rukh Khan's philosophy: whether a film succeeds or fails on Friday, by Monday he returns to work to make a better movie. This forward-looking mindset is essential for building wealth.

Extraction means removing toxic beliefs—self-doubt, the voice telling you that you'll never have enough money or that you're not good enough. Swadia recounts his own story: rejected for a US visa despite earning a full scholarship because he lacked $200 for a ticket, he felt like a failure. Yet today he works alongside powerful minds in technology and finance. The key is extracting these limiting beliefs and replacing them with positive mantras.

Abundance requires distinguishing between being rich and feeling rich. Swadia cites Rajat Gupta, former CEO of McKinsey and Goldman Sachs board member, who was worth $130 million when he became involved in an insider trading scandal. He lost everything—money, fame, reputation, influence. The lesson: know when enough is enough.

Discipline is what keeps wealth intact. Mike Tyson went from $400 million to bankruptcy through poor choices and misplaced trust. Kanye West nearly lost his $2 billion fortune. Both eventually recovered, but the pattern is clear: emotional wealth precedes financial wealth, and discipline preserves it.

Rule 2: the 25x rule—manage your burn rate

Gen Z outpaces boomers in retirement readiness: nearly 70% of Gen Z feel on track or ahead, versus only 50% of boomers. The difference lies in the 25x rule.

Calculate your annual expenses, then multiply by 25. That's the investment target needed to retire safely. The rule rests on the 4% withdrawal principle: if investments return 6% annually, you can withdraw 4% yearly without depleting capital.

Example: $40,000 annual expenses × 25 = $1 million invested. A $1 million portfolio returning 6% yields $60,000; withdrawing $40,000 leaves a $20,000 buffer.

The critical insight: 80% of financial success comes from what you don't buy. Markets fluctuate—in 2008, they fell 50%—so managing burn rate (spending) matters as much as managing earn rate (income).

Rule 3: plan for murphy's law

Anything that can go wrong will. Swadia recounts leading sales, marketing, and customer success at a company with a phenomenal product. A database bug delayed a critical product launch indefinitely. Customers lost trust, left, and the company eventually faded. He lost his job.

Defense: maintain an emergency fund of at least six months' living expenses. Don't touch it.

Rule 4: love your luck—engineer it

Luck isn't random; you can engineer it by maximizing your odds: be in the right places, develop the right skills, surround yourself with the right people. When the right time comes, you'll be ready.

Swadia shares Nick Jonas's story: at six years old, singing in a hair salon to amuse himself, a customer overheard him and connected his mother with a talent manager. Jonas had the skill; the salon visit was chance. Together, they launched an extraordinary career.

The principle: if you never swing the bat, you'll never hit a home run. Luck rewards hustle.

Rule 5: always play the long game

Start investing $400 monthly in an S&P 500 index fund at age 21, continue until 67. Final balance: over $1.7 million. Your out-of-pocket contribution: roughly $220,000. The remaining $1.5 million? The magic of compounding—money making money while you sleep.

Action steps: start early, automate investments, make it a ritual, and be patient. Growth is slow at first, explosive at the end.

Rule 6: become the CFO of your own life

Every successful company needs a Chief Financial Officer managing cash flow, burn rate, capital allocation, and metrics. Your personal finances deserve the same rigor.

Three immediate actions:

  1. Create a monthly or quarterly financial statement. You don't need to track every penny, but know where your money goes. Do you need five streaming subscriptions? That daily expensive espresso?

  2. Don't chase perfection. Use Excel, Google Sheets, a bullet list, or apps like Mint or You Need a Budget. Start simple.

  3. Set up three automations: bill payments, savings, and investing. Remove human emotion. Whatever remains is yours to spend.

Rule 7: embrace frugality

Warren Buffett still lives in the house he bought in the 1950s. The IKEA founder flies economy and drives an old Volvo. Keanu Reeves takes the New York City subway. The common thread: frugality.

Modest living is the fastest path to wealth. Turn off your consumer brain. True happiness comes from experiences and relationships, not possessions. Things you own end up owning you. Learn to find joy in what money can't buy.

Rule 8: understand good debt versus bad debt

Not all debt is bad. A CEO Swadia works with took significant debt to fund an acquisition, even though the company had cash. It was a smart move.

Timing matters: buying a house in January 2021 at a 2.7% mortgage rate was wise; buying the same house in October 2022 at over 7% was risky.

Think of debt like fire: good debt keeps you warm; bad debt burns your house down.

Rule 9: be an investor, not a gambler

Invest like you're in Silicon Valley, not Vegas. Swadia's friend, a CEO, took his company public and became wealthy. Post-IPO, the company thrived, but then the market crashed. His stock plummeted from $70 to pennies. Locked out of selling, he watched his net worth drop from $7 million to less than $70,000.

The solution: diversify like venture capitalists do. Visualize a three-tier pyramid:

  • Base: emergency fund (cash and bonds). Low returns, but a strong foundation for calculated risks.
  • Middle: moderate-risk investments (index funds, 401ks, ETFs). The largest portion, aiming for steady long-term growth.
  • Top: 5–10% of your portfolio in higher-risk bets. Greater reward potential, but higher risk. Swing the bat here.

Never invert the pyramid—a thin base with excessive top-tier exposure is a recipe for disaster. Investors spread risk; gamblers go all in.

Rule 10: Invest in financial education

High school students who took personal finance classes ended up with higher credit scores as adults. A few early lessons compound over decades.

Commit to learning: courses, YouTube, books, podcasts, mentors. Treat it as R&D—research and development for your financial future. A biotech CEO Swadia knows invests 89% of budget into R&D. You can't do that with income, but even 10% of your time building financial knowledge yields huge returns.

Rule 11: Invest in your relationships

Your most valuable asset isn't your car, house, or stocks—it's your network.

When Swadia graduated during a major recession, jobs were scarce. He focused on networking: calls, emails, coffee meetings, LinkedIn outreach. After six months, someone in his network hired him. Every upward career move since has stemmed from relationships, not just skills. Your network compounds your skills; it's a multiplier.

LinkedIn data shows 85% of jobs are filled through networking, yet many people spend hours refining resumes for sites no one reads. Instead, spend 2–4 hours weekly building relationships: connect with alumni, peers, industry leaders, mentors. Reach out without shame.

The deepest insight: don't just build relationships, invest in them. Focus on giving more than receiving. That's the proven path to financial success.
