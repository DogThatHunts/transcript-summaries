The most overlooked way to get rich in America

In this episode, host Chris sits down with franchising expert Alex from Franzy to unpack why franchising is America's most overlooked vehicle for wealth creation. Boasting an approximate 85% success rate compared to just 50% for independent startups over a five-year period, franchising offers a structured, de-risked playbook for aspiring business owners. Alex breaks down four highly lucrative, low-overhead B2B concepts—including freight brokerage, commercial facility management, mobile oil filtration, and fleet fuel delivery—while revealing how to spot red flags in franchise disclosure documents (FDDs) and leverage capital to build multi-unit empires.

Five key insights

  1. Franchising dramatically outpaces independent business success: Over a five-year period, franchises maintain an approximate 85% success rate, compared to a mere 50% survival rate for independent small businesses.
  2. The "magic number" for franchise systems is 50 units: Once a franchise brand surpasses 50 open locations, it typically becomes self-sustaining on royalties alone, making the franchisor more selective with operators and less reliant on upfront franchise fees.
  3. Transfers can be a hidden metric for closures: Sophisticated franchisors often use Item 20 transfers—passing a failing location to corporate or another franchisee—to mask outright closures on their franchise disclosure documents (FDDs).
  4. "Grandparent businesses" offer high margins with low liability: Niche service concepts like senior accessibility infrastructure (installing ramps and stairlifts) allow owners to perform quick, high-ticket, insurance-backed installations without the ongoing labor headaches of direct care.
  5. Chick-fil-A operates more like a high-paying job than true business ownership: While the average Chick-fil-A operator takes home $650,000 annually, the corporate parent owns the land, builds the store, and claims 50% of profits, limiting the operator's ability to scale an independent equity portfolio.

Four high-potential, low-cost franchise concepts

Alex highlights four B2B and route-based franchise models that offer high revenue potential relative to their initial startup costs:

  • Freight brokerage: This home-office-based B2B sales model pairs shippers with carriers. The average franchisee generates over $1 million in revenue at a 40% gross margin. The startup cost ranges from $75,000 to $193,000, with a 30% royalty taken on the gross margin (not gross revenue). This concept is mature in the UK and Europe but is newly expanding into the US.
  • Commercial facility management: Operating essentially as an unbundled property manager focusing strictly on maintenance (landscaping, HVAC, janitorial, parking lot striping), this model relies entirely on managing subcontractors. The average franchisee does $9 million in revenue with a 33.5% gross margin ($3.3 million). Startup costs range from $229,000 to $410,000, with a 5% royalty on gross sales.
  • Oil filtration: A route-based B2B service that filters and recycles commercial fryer oil for restaurants, turning waste oil into biofuel. Single territories average $517,000 in revenue, while multi-territory operators average over $1.5 million. Startup costs range from $140,000 to $163,000.
  • Mobile fuel delivery: This B2B service delivers diesel exhaust fluid (DEF) and fuel directly to commercial truck fleets and heavy machinery on-site. The average revenue is just under $5 million, with roughly $1 million in gross profit. Startup costs range from $172,000 to $490,000. The franchisor charges a 9% gross profit royalty, which is waived for the first six months to help operators navigate the initial "J-curve."

Navigating the franchise lifecycle and red flags

When evaluating a franchise disclosure document (FDD), Alex advises looking closely at Item 20, which tracks store openings, closures, and transfers. A high number of transfers in a young system (under 10 years old) is a major red flag, as franchisors frequently use transfers to corporate ownership or other franchisees to hide failing locations.

Additionally, prospective buyers must understand the shifting value dynamic between franchisors and franchisees. Early on, the franchisor provides immense front-loaded value (training, tech, brand setup) while receiving little in royalties. Years later, as the franchisee becomes an expert, they may experience "royalty fatigue," questioning why they continue to pay fees. Thoughtful franchisors solve this by continually investing in proprietary technology, lead generation, and national marketing campaigns that franchisees could not build on their own.

The reality of big-name brands: Chick-fil-A, Subway, and Crumble

Alex and Chris contrast the realities of household franchise names against public perception:

  • Subway: Described by Alex as "the worst franchise deal in America" today. He notes that the brand has historically engaged in cannibalistic development practices, opening locations blocks apart and letting franchisees "fight it out" to see which one survives.
  • Crumble Cookies: Characterized as a struggling fad. Alex points to declining store volumes and "Frankenstein" menu innovations—such as introducing pies, dirty sodas, and pre-packaged ice cream—as clear indicators that the core cookie model is losing steam amid broader health trends.
  • Chick-fil-A: While highly profitable, with operators averaging $650,000 in take-home income on a tiny $15,000 initial fee, it is not a true business. Because the corporate entity retains 50% of profits, owns the real estate, and prevents operators from building multi-unit equity portfolios, it is effectively a highly competitive, glorifed general manager job.

The rise of "grandparent" and mission-driven businesses

Some of the most lucrative, quiet wealth builders are what Chris terms "grandparent businesses"—ventures where you enter, perform a high-margin service, and leave without the long-term liability of managing ongoing care.

An example is accessibility infrastructure for seniors (installing stairlifts and wheelchair ramps). Driven by an aging baby boomer population, these businesses face massive demand and virtually zero supply, with some franchisees generating over $10 million in annual revenue. Similarly, autism behavioral therapy (ABA) clinics represent highly profitable, mission-driven models. Because services are funded by insurance companies rather than out-of-pocket by parents, they enjoy highly stable, recurring revenue streams.

How to get started with little to no capital

For those looking to enter franchising with limited personal capital, Alex highlights the accessibility of Small Business Administration (SBA) loans. Traditional SBA loans can finance 70% to 90% of startup costs, requiring as little as 10% cash down. For faster deployment, the SBA Express loan program offers up to $500,000 in funding in under 60 days.

Alternatively, individuals with strong operational backgrounds but no capital can partner with passive investors. Many "empire builders" scale to dozens of units by acting as the operating partner, securing meaningful equity upside in exchange for managing the day-to-day business while investors provide the necessary capital.
