Peptides Are LEGAL Now?! Here's the Catch

Peptides are now legally accessible in the U.S. — but only temporarily, with strict conditions and a long road ahead.

Are Peptides Legal Now?

Yes, but only for six specific peptides and under strict conditions. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) has issued a policy stating it will not enforce penalties against licensed pharmacies or physicians compounding these peptides, as long as 10 specific conditions are met. This does not make the peptides FDA-approved or permanently legal.

What Peptides Are covered?

The policy covers six peptides: - BBC 157 - TB 500 - MOTC - CEMACS - Epitalon - KPV

Peptides like GHK-CU, CJC-1295, Ipamorelin, DCIP, and Tessamoralin are not included and remain outside this framework.

Key conditions for legality

To be legally compounded, each vial must meet the following: - Be the exact molecule listed in the appendix, including salt form - Be delivered via only approved routes (e.g., injection, oral, topical) - Be compounded by a licensed 503A pharmacy with a patient-specific prescription - Be labeled as a compounded drug product, not for resale - Include a certificate of analysis (COA) with at least 20 categories of testing - Be made from an FDA-registered manufacturer of the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) - Include the physical address of the API facility on the COA

Testing requirements

The policy mandates orthogonal testing methods for each ingredient, meaning at least two independent tests using different scientific principles must agree. Testing categories include: - Identity - Potency - Peptide content - Impurities - Residual solvents - Heavy metals - Residual TFA (a chemical used in peptide purification) - Bacterial endotoxins - Nitrosamines - Bio-burden

What about Blends and stacks?

Blends are explicitly forbidden. For example: - The 'Wolverine stack' (BPC-157 and TB-500) cannot be combined in a single vial - It requires two separate vials and two prescriptions

Device and delivery rules

  • Only FDA-cleared syringes are allowed
  • Preloaded auto-injectors are not permitted
  • No injectable KPV is allowed
  • No oral epitalon is permitted
  • Oral BPC exists but requires proof of human absorption

Who is involved?

Dr. Alex, a medical advisor and former FDA witness, testified at the FDA in July on all seven peptides. He helped design clinical protocols for Superpower, a company that has published a full peptide master plan and a patient program.

Superpower offers: - A 152-peptide library mapped to body functions, with physician-reviewed evidence - A program for BPC-157, TB-500, and MATC, prescribed by licensed clinicians and compounded via FDA-registered pharmacies - A 40+ biomarker blood panel for each treatment - Monthly pricing: BPC-157 and TB-500 at $149, MATC at $199 - A 30-day refund policy if a clinician determines the peptide is not appropriate

Is this permanent?

No. The policy is temporary and expires when the FDA finalizes a rulemaking process. The FDA could still ban any peptide or revoke the policy if a safety signal emerges. Historically, only six of 15 years of peptide applications have made it to final approval.

A public comment docket is open until November 13, allowing patients and advocates to submit real-world stories that may influence future FDA decisions.

Important caveats

  • These peptides are not FDA-approved
  • Legal access does not equate to WADA compliance
  • Research use only (RUO) vials are not covered and have significantly lower testing standards
  • The policy creates no binding rights and reserves HHS’s right to change its mind

Final thoughts

Dr. Alex calls this the best development in peptide access in his career — but one with an unmarked expiration date. For patients, it means real access through licensed providers and the first systematic collection of safety data. For prescribers and pharmacists, it means strict adherence to the policy and the appendix.
