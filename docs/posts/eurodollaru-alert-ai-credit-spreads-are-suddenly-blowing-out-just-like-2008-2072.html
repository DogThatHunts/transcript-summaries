As tech giants pour billions into artificial intelligence, the debt markets are signaling caution through rising credit default swaps and weaker bond demand.

The shift from equity dreams to debt reality

While the public focuses on the stock market fluctuations of the artificial intelligence boom, the real story is shifting to the credit and debt markets. Unlike the dot-com bubble of 1999, which was primarily fueled by equity valuations of companies with little to no revenue, today’s AI build-out is being financed by massive debt issuance. By early July 2026, AI-related debt issuance reached $270 billion—nearly double the total for all of 2025. Major technology giants like Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Oracle, and Meta account for $194 billion of this total. These companies are not borrowing because their core businesses are failing; they are borrowing because the immense cost of the AI arms race is overwhelming even their massive cash flows. Jeff Snyder argues that the AI race has become a financing race, where the ultimate winner will not be the one with the most exciting technology, but the one that can generate enough dependable cash to service its debt.

The surge in credit default swaps

A key signal of growing market anxiety is the sudden rise in credit default swap (CDS) premiums for major tech firms. CDS contracts act as insurance against default, and rising premiums reflect increased demand for protection.

  • Oracle: CDS spreads reached approximately 215 basis points in July 2026, up from 145 basis points at the end of 2025, marking a multi-year high.
  • SpaceX: Swaps surged to 185 basis points, rising by more than 50% since trading began just a month prior.
  • Nvidia, Meta, Amazon, and Alphabet: All saw their CDS premiums reach new highs.

These rising premiums do not mean default is imminent, but they signal a major behavioral shift: investors are transitioning from asking how much they can buy to asking how they can protect themselves.

Weaker demand in corporate bond offerings

The cooling enthusiasm is also visible in recent high-profile bond sales:

  • Amazon's $25 billion bond sale: Although initially attracting $62 billion in orders, demand fell to $41 billion after banks reduced the offered spreads. A demand-to-cover ratio of only 1.6x is exceptionally weak for a blue-chip borrower like Amazon, which typically sees ratios closer to 4x. Amazon also had to offer elevated new issue concessions of 18 to 21 basis points on its longer-term bonds.
  • BlackRock’s $12.5 billion data center offering: Designed to finance a Texas data center project leased to Meta, this offering took nearly a week to close and faced weak demand. It ultimately priced at a yield of 7.53%, one of the highest yields for blue-chip data center financing since the AI borrowing boom began.

Alphabet's historic negative free cash flow

Alphabet (Google) reported a negative free cash flow of $5.9 billion in its latest quarter—its first negative reading since going public in 2004. This was driven by capital expenditures doubling year-over-year to $45 billion. Alphabet has raised its 2026 capital spending budget to between $195 billion and $205 billion, with expectations of further increases in 2027. This highlights a dual risk: whether AI can generate enough incremental revenue to cover these costs, and whether inflation in infrastructure inputs (such as land, turbines, transformers, and power connections) means each new dollar invested buys less actual computing power than the last.

Complex project financing and hidden risks

To keep massive debt off their balance sheets, tech companies are turning to complex, off-balance-sheet project financing structures. In the BlackRock-backed Texas data center project, BlackRock entities hold 80% of the equity and issued the $12.5 billion in bonds, while Meta holds 20% and guarantees the lease payments. While not inherently unethical, these highly complex structures make it difficult for investors to determine where the ultimate credit exposure lies. Furthermore, banks are using credit derivatives to hedge their exposure, meaning the rising activity in the CDS market may reflect underlying anxieties that are not visible on corporate balance sheets.

The capital cycle trap: lessons from the dot-com era

The true parallel to the dot-com era lies in the capital cycle. At the peak of a cycle, record profits encourage record investment, which dramatically increases supply. Eventually, this oversupply pressures prices and returns. This dynamic is already playing out:

  • SK Hynix: Despite reporting a six-fold increase in quarterly profit and operating margins above 80%, its shares fell 19% after it announced a 50% increase in capital spending to $31 billion. Investors interpreted this massive spending as a sign of overcapacity risk at the top of the cycle.
  • Chinese lithography progress: Reports of advanced lithography developments in China have heightened fears of future global chip oversupply.

Snyder concludes that while AI technology will undoubtedly be historically important, the massive investments made in its name today are highly vulnerable to producing terrible financial returns if incremental revenues fail to match the soaring costs of debt and infrastructure.
