How cellular cleanup works — and why overdoing it can backfire on your health.

What is Autophagy?

Autophagy is the process by which cells clean out damaged parts and recycle them. It's sometimes called 'eat thyself' and won a Nobel Prize in 2016 when scientists discovered how it works by starving yeast and watching them consume their own components.

Think of each cell as a kitchen in a restaurant. Throughout the day, it cooks, makes energy, and repairs things — just like a real kitchen. This creates messes: grease fires, burnt food, broken equipment, and piled-up dishes. Autophagy is the cleanup crew that labels trash, bags it, and drops it into recycling bins.

The bins are lysosomes — not just disposal units, but recycling centers. They break down trash into amino acids, fats, and sugars, then send those materials back to the kitchen as fresh ingredients. This process turns waste into usable building blocks.

What happens when Autophagy fails?

If the cleanup crew never shows up, serious problems arise. In a 2006 study, researchers in Tokyo deleted autophagy from healthy mice's brain cells. The mice became clumsy due to garbage piling up in their brains, and one group died within 28 weeks.

Other studies showed that when cells lacked parts of their cleanup crew: - Broken mitochondria piled up - They leaked DNA, triggering a major inflammatory cascade - The mice had worse survival during infections

This demonstrates that autophagy is not optional — it's essential for preventing mitochondrial damage, inflammation, and aging.

The balance between mtor and AMPK

Autophagy is governed by a balance between two protein complexes: - mTOR acts as the body's master growth switch. It turns on when nutrients are available, signaling cells to build, grow, and divide. - AMPK is the master fuel gauge. It activates when cellular energy is low, signaling the body to conserve resources and trigger autophagy.

These two forces act like a seesaw: - When energy is high, mTOR turns on and builds - When energy is low, AMPK takes over and initiates cleanup

During fasting, low energy triggers AMPK, which kicks mTOR off its perch on the lysosome — allowing the cleanup crew to activate.

In the cytoplasm, a membrane forms around tagged material and seals into a structure called an autophagosome. This bag is then transported to the lysosome, where the trash is broken down into building blocks. These materials are reused to create new cellular parts.

This process removes: - Damaged mitochondria that can't produce energy - Sticky protein aggregates that build up in diseases like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's - Old organelles, redundant proteins, and damaged components

When materials run low, the cell scavenges from disposable tissues — including muscle.

The myth of 'more is better'

A common belief is that more autophagy is always better. But this is flawed. Cells aren't dusty attics that need constant cleaning. They're active kitchens that operate best with rhythm.

If autophagy is turned on indefinitely, the body becomes cachectic — wasting away. This is not a state wellness enthusiasts typically experience.

Chronic fasting and dieting can lead to a slow, insidious trade of lean tissue for frailty. This happens because prolonged suppression of growth signals (via mTOR) causes the hypothalamus to shut down systems like muscle building and thyroid function.

Dr. Ashley Frazee emphasizes that mTOR is not an 'evil' to be turned off. Without it, a kitchen only cleans — it never serves a meal. Building and healing require mTOR.

Ultimately, the ideal state is rhythm: cook, clean, cook, clean.

Five practical ways to support Cellular Cleanup

  1. Move hard Exercise turns on autophagy in muscle and does more than just send cleaners — it remodels and expands the cellular 'kitchen' by increasing lysosome production.

  2. Skip the extremes Multi-day fasts lack scientific proof for significant autophagy and may harm brain function, lean tissue, and mental well-being.

  3. Eat quality building blocks When you eat, prioritize protein. Amino acids supply the materials needed for muscle and tissue repair. This supports the rebuilding phase after cleanup.

  4. Prioritize sleep Sleep is when most cellular cleanup occurs. During sleep, growth signals drop, stress hormones quiet, and cells shift into maintenance mode. Studies show sleep-deprived animals have suppressed autophagy and accumulate damaged proteins and mitochondria.

  5. Focus on timing The liver operates on a circadian rhythm and expects nutrient availability at specific times. Food intake timing influences when the liver produces enzymes for metabolism and when it shifts to autophagy and fat burning during fasting and sleep. Rather than focusing on how long you fast, a consistent rhythm that works for your body may be more important.
