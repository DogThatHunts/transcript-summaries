Master Key | The Law of Success, Book 2 of 8 (1928) by Napoleon Hill - "A Definite Chief Aim & Self-Confidence"

Lessons Two and Three: how a written, daily-repeated chief aim and a friendly alliance turn desire into reality, and how a signed formula overcomes the six basic fears.

Book 2 of 8 of Napoleon Hill's The Law of Success (1928), read by Dan John Miller for the Master Key Society. The ideas are Hill's, reported as he states them, not endorsed or verified here. Every timestamp opens the video a few seconds early.

This book contains two lessons, Lesson Two, "A Definite Chief Aim", and Lesson Three, "Self-Confidence", both under the motto "You can do it if you believe you can." The first third of the recording is a general preface placed ahead of Lesson Two. In it Hill explains how to read the course, defines success as power and power as organized effort, retells his interview with Andrew Carnegie about the Master Mind, and reports that 95 percent of the more than 16,000 people he analyzed were failures, chiefly because they had no definite chief aim.

Lesson Two holds that a person's acts always follow the dominating thoughts of the mind. A chief aim that is written down, read daily and fixed in the subconscious by auto-suggestion steers its owner toward it and attracts the people and means it needs. The lesson ends with exact instructions: write the aim and the plan, and form a "friendly alliance" to apply the Master Mind. Lesson Three supplies the faith needed to carry those instructions out. It names fear as the enemy, lists the six basic fears and traces them to "social heredity", and prescribes a signed Self-Confidence Formula to be repeated until it becomes a habit. The book closes with Hill's essay "Discontentment" and a set of the original volume's full-page maxims.

The argument running through both lessons is that thought, held persistently, becomes action and then reality. Auto-suggestion is the mechanism, habit fixes it, and an alliance of harmonious minds multiplies it.

Lesson 2: A Definite Chief Aim [00:40]

How to read the course [01:24]

The value of the philosophy lies "entirely in the thought stimuli it will produce." Hill compares the mind to Jack Dempsey's training, in which every form of exercise builds something different, so the mind also needs varied stimuli [02:11]. Read with a notebook and pencil and record every idea that comes; after two or three readings the notes may "transform his or her entire life plan" [03:53]. "Humility is a forerunner of success" [05:28]. To know others, study their posture, voice, eyes and words, and watch them in many moods before judging [06:16]. In the deepest sense this is a course in "master salesmanship": selling one's way through life [08:21].

Open minds and testing before judging [09:59]

Most people "prefer illusions to realities." Hill quotes an American Magazine editorial telling, from Carl Lomen, "the reindeer king of Alaska", of a Greenland Eskimo who was branded Sagdluk, "the liar", for describing New York truthfully, while a later traveller, Mitek, kept his honor by telling only of hunting seals on the Hudson [10:11]. "For every ten men who are physically lazy, there are ten thousand with stagnant minds" [13:17]. Hill asks no one to accept a statement untested, and cites what he gives as Herbert Spencer's warning against "contempt prior to examination" [16:19]. That warning applies above all to the Master Mind, which he says Alexander Graham Bell, Charles P. Steinmetz, Luther Burbank, John Burroughs and Elmer Gates endorsed [17:38].

Success, power and Carnegie's Master Mind [20:40]

"Success is the development of the power with which to get whatever one wants in life without interfering with the rights of others," and "power is organized energy or effort" [20:48]. The course promises whatever you want, "within reason" [21:37]. "Nearly twenty years ago", Hill writes, Carnegie told him that his money came from "a mastermind" of more than a score of managers, accountants and chemists: "the sum total of the minds in the group, coordinated, organized, and directed to a definite end in a spirit of harmonious cooperation" [23:22]. That mind could have built the same fortune in coal, banking or groceries; "the steel business... was but an incident" [25:03]. Charles M. Schwab is quoted to confirm that Carnegie had no technical knowledge of steel and led through "something deeper than any faculty of judgment" [26:19]. Success, Hill concludes, "is solely a matter of correctly applying laws and principles which are available to all" [28:30].

Organized effort [29:05]

Hill's illustrations of coordination are propaganda breaking up enemy forces, General Foch's unified Allied command [29:27], the railroad bridge, and the fable of the bundle of sticks that seven quarrelling sons could not break [30:19]. Ford rose while a shopmate said to be the better workman still earns under $100 a week [32:43]. In Shelby, Ohio, churches and businesses formed an alliance in which no member could fail [33:23]. Churches, schools and newspapers combined could make the golden rule the only workable business standard within a generation [35:23].

What 16,000 analyses showed [39:31]

Hill reports that of more than sixteen thousand people analyzed in fourteen years, 95 percent were failures and 5 percent successes [40:00]. The failures lacked a definite chief aim, while the successes had definite purposes and plans [40:46]. The successes did work they liked and saved money systematically; the failures did neither [41:34]. Each student will analyze himself later with the questionnaire that comes with the sixteenth lesson [38:35].

The psychology of a chief aim [42:05]

"The keynote of this entire lesson may be found in the word definite." The foundation is that "a person's acts are always in harmony with the dominating thoughts of his or her mind" [42:40]. An aim held with determination "saturates the entire subconscious mind" until it directs the body, so it should be chosen carefully, written out and seen at least once a day [43:06]. The method is auto-suggestion, "a degree of self-hypnotism", which Hill credits for the rise of Napoleon, Edison, Lincoln and Theodore Roosevelt. It is safe if the aim brings "hardship and misery to no one" [43:37]. The peanut vendor across the street works all day, but he is a drifter whose self-suggestion keeps him at the roaster [45:08].

A friend of Hill's, now a well-known writer and speaker, sat Lincoln, Washington, Emerson, Napoleon and Elbert Hubbard at an imaginary council table each night and asked each for his chief quality. Over months he grew "a personality which was a composite of their personalities" [46:50].

Like attracts like [49:30]

The saturated subconscious is a magnet. An acorn draws only what makes an oak, and people gather with their own kind: "you will attract to you people who harmonize with your own philosophy of life, whether you wish it or not" [49:46]. It is "your privilege, nay, your duty to aim high" [51:39]. Louis Victor Eytinge, a life prisoner in Arizona expected to die of tuberculosis, decided to beat the "white plague", won within a year, and then resolved on freedom until "the prison walls melted from around him" [52:07]. Crowds, children and even "a balky horse" yield to someone with a definite aim [53:33].

The economic side [54:41]

A ship without a rudder burns its fuel going in circles, and a house built without blueprints ends in chaos [54:44]. If success is power and power is organized effort, then a definite purpose is the first step in organizing [56:35]. A reading glass focused on one spot burns a plank, and connected dry cells drive machinery [57:04]. Carnegie's advice was to put "all your eggs in one basket and then watch the basket" [58:15]. In the fable of the legless man riding the blind man, "out of allied effort comes greater power" [59:56]. Wanting money is not a definite purpose; you must decide the business, the place and the policies. "To be of as much service to the world as possible and earn a good living" is "about as definite as a frog's conception of the size of the universe" [1:01:55].

Decision, concentration and the roll call [1:03:37]

Every one of the more than 100 outstanding people Hill observed was "a person of prompt and definite decision", and a chief aim breeds both decision and concentration [1:03:37]. He then gives a long roll call [1:04:33]: Woolworth's five-and-ten chain, Wrigley's gum, Henry L. Doherty's utilities, Ingersoll's dollar watch, Statler's hotels, Edwin C. Barnes and the Edison dictating machine, Woodrow Wilson's twenty-five years fixed on the White House, Lincoln on freeing the slaves, Martin W. Littleton's law practice, Rockefeller, Ford, Carnegie, Gillette, Eastman, Russell Conwell's "Acres of Diamonds", Helen Keller, the Kaiser ("concentrated on war and got a big dose of it"), Marshall Field, the Wright brothers, Pullman and the Anti-Saloon League. "Millions of people are concentrating daily on poverty and failure and getting both in overabundance." Find the "one thing that you can do better than anyone else", most likely the work you like best [1:08:34].

From desire to fulfilment [1:09:14]

Every voluntary movement is directed by thought, and an idea in consciousness urges matching muscular action, as the thought of winking makes the eyelid wink [1:09:32]. Desire chooses the aim, and it must be "a burning desire" [1:10:55]. "Science has established, beyond the slightest room for doubt", that deep desire makes the mind a magnet for its object. Merely wishing for an automobile brings nothing, but a burning desire leads to the action that pays for one [1:12:01]. The steps are burning desire, its crystallization into a definite purpose, and sufficient appropriate action [1:12:59]. Hill's examples are a poor girl who developed an attractive personality and so won a wealthy husband, his own ten years of character research, and George S. Parker's fountain pens [1:13:19].

The lesson is not complete until you write your aim down and look at it every morning and night [1:16:00]. "Know what you want, when you want it, why you want it, and how you intend to get it", which Hill calls the "WWWH formula" [1:17:42]. Read the lesson four times, a week apart, and "do not set up your own rules of study" [1:17:57].

Instructions for applying the lesson [1:19:02]

The steps are to decide the major aim, write a clear statement of it, write the plans for reaching it, and form an alliance with people who will help carry them out [1:19:34]. A wife belongs in a married man's alliance; others may be parents, siblings, friends or a sweetheart. "This is no joke" [1:20:17]. Members sign the statement and keep copies, but the aim is otherwise kept secret from "doubting Thomases" [1:20:57]. Those who believe in prayer should pray for the aim every twenty-four hours [1:21:53]. The "most essential" rule is that an ally must tell you at least daily, in the most positive terms, that you will succeed [1:22:30]. Continue until the aim is reached, change plans without hesitation, and replace any member who loses faith. Carnegie said he replaced nearly all his original group [1:23:19]. Any failure will be because a member lacked "faith, loyalty, and sincerity of purpose" [1:25:07].

Persistence [1:25:22]

Make the aim your hobby: "sleep with it, eat with it, play with it." Wishing is not believing [1:25:26]. Hill states that Edison made more than 10,000 experiments before recording "Mary had a little lamb", and that the word to remember is "persistence" [1:26:26]. "Nature cannot be tricked or cheated"; her price is "continuous, unyielding, persistent effort" [1:27:23]. Self-confidence comes next, and the lesson closes with the poem "Opportunity" ("Master of human destinies am I") [1:28:09].

Lesson 3: Self-Confidence [1:28:58]

The fear demon and the six basic fears [1:29:11]

"Skepticism is the deadly enemy of progress." Thought is "the most highly organized form of energy known to man" [1:30:49], and auto-suggestion can throw off "a thousand generations of fear", which is "the chief reason for poverty and failure" [1:31:17]. The six basic fears are poverty, old age, criticism, loss of love of someone, ill health and death. The student should sort his own fears under them [1:32:34].

Physical and social heredity [1:33:19]

Through physical heredity man evolved "from the amoeba." Fears cannot be inherited physically, though physical heredity gives them "a most favorable lodging place." Hill floats the theory that the "cell intelligence" of eaten animals passes on their fear, then calls it possibly untrue [1:35:07]. The real source is social heredity, the beliefs one generation imposes on the next through schooling, religion, reading and storytelling [1:36:14]. An idea planted in a child's mind "never can be eradicated", and Hill says religionists use this to fix their creeds [1:37:23].

The fears one by one [1:39:20]

  • Poverty: man "does not eat his fellow men bodily. He gets more satisfaction out of eating them financially." Poverty is "an unforgivable sin", though there is enough for all but for "the swinish habit" of hoarding [1:39:38].
  • Old age: the fear that age brings poverty, and "false and cruel sectarian teachings" of fire and brimstone [1:42:35].
  • Criticism: rooted in justifying theft by attacking character [1:44:34]. Hill says men go bald from tight hatbands worn for fear of criticism, and that clothing makers change styles to exploit the fear [1:45:07].
  • Loss of love: from man's urge to steal his fellow's mate. "By nature all men are polygamists", and jealousy is a form of "dementia praecox" [1:47:47].
  • Ill health: kept alive, Hill suspects, by sellers of health methods [1:49:23].
  • Death: charged "directly to religious fanaticism", sects selling "a ticket" to heaven. "No man knows... where we come from at birth or where we go at death" [1:50:26].

Social heredity in animals [1:54:54]

After Hill handled her fearless chicks, a mother ruffed grouse gave them a "lecture", and they hid from him from then on [1:55:03]. His pet crow, scolded by wild crows, left for good [1:58:17]. A fox needs one lesson from a skunk [2:00:35], and a bullfrog that feels the hook once never bites again [2:01:38]. His chicken-killing Airedale was cured by one fight with a mother hen [2:02:23]. "The most impressive lessons are those learned by the young from the old, through highly colored or emotionalized methods of teaching" [2:05:36].

Auto-suggestion misapplied [2:06:30]

Fear of poverty drives men to overwork until they bring on what they dread, just at forty, when they begin "to mature mentally" [2:06:37]. Hill gives three portraits of self-suggestion used against oneself: the disappointed man who decides all men are untrustworthy (with a reference back to Lesson Two's law of attraction), the educated man who stays at the back for fear of seeming egotistical, and the man born poor who accepts "once poor, always poor" [2:08:12]. Unused faculties "atrophy and wither away" [2:11:23].

Struggle versus inherited ease [2:11:37]

Mrs. E. B. McLean's son, heir to about $100 million, was so guarded by servants that the first time he ran out of the gate alone an automobile killed him [2:11:46]. Two sons of Hill's former employer, given $100 a month, came home with "capacities for strong drink"; one died of delirium tremens and the other went to an asylum [2:12:58]. "Forced idleness is far worse than forced labor" [2:15:56].

The advertising solicitor [2:17:14]

A timid clerk at the Philadelphia North American listed twelve merchants no one could sell. He read the list a hundred times in the park, telling himself "You will purchase advertising space from me before the end of the month", and signed eleven of them [2:18:09]. He called on the twelfth every morning for a month until, after thirty refusals, the merchant bought as "tuition" for the lesson in persistence. The clerk became a millionaire [2:19:17]. "All rivers and some men are crooked because of following the line of least resistance" [2:21:05].

A word to wives [2:21:15]

A wife can send her husband out each day full of self-confidence. Hill's father, a farmer, became a prominent Virginia dentist after his wife urged him on: "I know him well, for he is my father" [2:21:58]. Another man credited his success to a creed his wife wrote, beginning "I believe in myself" [2:23:26], which Hill says to memorize and repeat daily [2:25:43].

The Self-Confidence Formula [2:29:07]

The student is "instructed to copy the following formula, sign it, and commit it to memory." It has five points. The first is that I can achieve my purpose, so I demand persistent action of myself. The second is that dominating thoughts become reality, so I will spend thirty minutes daily picturing the person I intend to be. The third is that I will spend ten minutes daily demanding of myself the factors of the sixteen lessons. The fourth is that I have written a five-year purpose and set a price on my services for each year [2:29:13]. The fifth is that I will engage in no transaction that does not benefit all it affects, and I will replace hatred, envy, jealousy, selfishness and cynicism with love for humanity [2:30:42]. It is to be repeated aloud once a day. "Back of this formula lies a law that no man can explain," Hill concedes, but it works, and like electricity it can serve or destroy [2:31:52].

The power of habit [2:33:09]

Habit is the groove of a phonograph record and the mind the needle [2:33:34]. "Voluntarily, and by force if necessary, direct your efforts and your thoughts along a desired line" until habit takes over, as it did when you learned to write [2:34:44].

Proofs of self-belief [2:37:45]

Lee Cook of Louisville, legless from birth, became a millionaire, while an able-bodied legless man begs on Fifth Avenue [2:37:45]. Ford threw off "the poverty consciousness" [2:38:35]. Milo C. Jones, paralyzed, directed his family from bed and built Little Pig Sausage into a fortune [2:39:05]. "If you want to get more, be sure to demand more of yourself." Hill recommends memorizing "If you think you are beaten, you are" [2:40:43].

The tramp and the looking-glass [2:43:37]

A tramp, ruined when the 1914 war cut off his raw materials and close to suicide, came to Hill holding Hill's book Self-Confidence. Hill told him he could do nothing for him but would introduce him to a man who could, and pulled back a curtain on a full-length mirror [2:45:35]. The man wept. Days later he had "sold" himself at $3,000 a year, and Hill says he now heads a continent-wide firm [2:48:17]. At that same mirror Hill fixed his own definite purpose: to help people discover "the forces that lie sleeping within them" [2:50:10]. A schoolteacher abandoned his analysis chart after one question showed her what was wrong, and later earned $10,000 a year writing advertising copy [2:50:58].

"Day by day", telepathy and egotism [2:53:34]

Hill keeps the sign "Day by day, in every way, I am becoming more successful" over his typewriter and says it lifted him from the coal mines [2:53:43]. He cites the mustard seed of faith in Matthew [2:54:56]. "Others will believe in you only when you believe in yourself": by "the law of mental telepathy" you are "continuously broadcasting what you think of yourself" [2:57:45]. Self-confidence rests on knowledge, egotism on wishes, so let others discover your confidence through your deeds [2:58:18]. "Believe in yourself, but do not tell the world what you can do. Show it" [3:00:12].

After the lesson: "Discontentment" [3:00:26]

The mind always reaches for what it lacks. Woolworth vowed to outbuild the Metropolitan tower, and his building is "a monument to the vanity of man" [3:02:38]. Raises are spent at once [3:05:40], and the disillusioned turn to competing sects [3:07:28]. "Anticipation is sweeter than realization"; lasting happiness is the pursuit of "some yet unattained object" [3:10:54]. The recording ends with the book's maxims [3:12:36].
