China just handed the cartels a weapon that changes everything

Overview

John Nores, a retired California game warden with nearly 30 years of service, discusses his career fighting cartel-controlled marijuana grows and the emerging threat of Chinese organized crime collaborating with Mexican cartels. What began as environmental crime in California's forests has evolved into a sophisticated international criminal enterprise involving poisoned cannabis, fentanyl precursors, and the systematic invasion of America's public lands and private property.

Five key insights

  1. Cartel grows are military operations. Nores describes encountering armed Sinaloa cartel growers displaying tactical proficiency—hand signals, cover positions, and weapon discipline—comparable to trained military units. The first officer-involved shooting in U.S. marijuana enforcement history occurred during a 2005 raid when a cartel gunman shot and severely wounded a young game warden.

  2. EPA-banned nerve agents are poisoning the cannabis supply. Cartels spray marijuana plants with carbofuran and other neurotoxins banned in the U.S. for over 20 years. These chemicals kill wildlife, contaminate water supplies, and sicken law enforcement and civilians who ingest the poisoned weed. Cartel growers call it "El Diablo" (the devil) because they know how lethal it is.

  3. China is now a direct partner with Mexican cartels. Rather than competing, Chinese criminal organizations are collaborating with Sinaloa and other cartels to launder black-market cash, supply fentanyl precursors from mainland factories, and dominate the cannabis trade in multiple U.S. states. This represents a geopolitical threat disguised as a drug problem.

  4. Regulation has paradoxically worsened the black market. California's Prop 64 legalization watered down penalties from felonies to misdemeanors and made juvenile cultivation an infraction. This removed deterrence just as cartels were scaling up operations, allowing them to undercut legal growers through poisoned, cheaper product.

  5. The Northern border is now the primary trafficking corridor. With the Southern border tightened under recent administrations, cartels are moving fentanyl and cannabis through Canada and down Montana, Washington, and other northern states. The 5,000-mile border is largely unpatrolled and heavily forested, making it ideal for trafficking.

Nores' path to becoming a game warden

Nores grew up in Silicon Valley in a conservation-minded family. His grandfather survived Pearl Harbor; his father was a hunter and military veteran. At age nine, Nores began hunting waterfowl with his father, instilling a deep respect for wildlife and wild lands. Initially pursuing an engineering degree at San Jose State, he had a transformative encounter during winter break: while backpacking in Henrico State Park, a game warden appeared seemingly by chance to check on the group. That warden's job—protecting wildlife, waterways, and wild lands in remote terrain—captivated Nores. He switched majors to criminal justice and entered the California Department of Fish and Wildlife academy in 1992.

Nores emphasizes that game wardens receive full peace officer training plus specialized wildlife forensics and exotic weapon identification. They are equipped to handle everything from active shooters to environmental crimes. Unlike traditional police, he notes, game wardens interact primarily with conservationists and gun owners—"allies" rather than adversaries in most encounters.

The first cartel grow discovery (2004)

In April 2004, Nores and a biologist friend, GI, were investigating a diverted creek in Henrico County that was killing threatened and endangered steelhead trout and red-legged frogs. They discovered a hand-built dam made of dirt and rocks, lined with thick visqueen plastic, diverting all water through a single garden hose into black polyethylene pipe—the hallmark of cartel irrigation systems.

Following the water line downstream, they found 18-inch marijuana plants terraced on both sides of the creek. Then they encountered two armed growers in Vietnam-era olive drab uniforms moving tactically through the brush—silent, using hand signals, with one carrying an AK-derivative rifle. The growers were Sinaloa cartel operatives conducting their afternoon watering rounds. Nores and GI remained concealed as the growers passed within 15 yards, then retreated to alert authorities.

Three weeks later, a multi-agency task force of 30 officers raided the site, eradicating 7,000 plants. However, the task force did not restore the creek or remove the water diversion infrastructure—work that would have taken only two hours. This failure to reclaim the land meant the cartels could return the following year. The experience taught Nores that environmental restoration, not just plant eradication, was essential to deterrence.

The august 5, 2005 gunfight

One year later, Nores led a seven-person team—two young game wardens (Mojo and Bulldog), three sheriff's deputies including a Marine Corps veteran named Apache, an unarmed park ranger, and another deputy—to raid a cartel grow in the Los Gatos foothills overlooking Silicon Valley. The site was one of five major grows on a mountainside that law enforcement later determined was run by approximately 20 armed growers and defenders.

As the team moved through a skirmish line clearing tall marijuana plants, a single shot rang out—not from their weapons but from an AK or SKS rifle. Mojo, a one-year academy graduate on his first raid, was hit. The round, a steel-core military bullet, penetrated his left leg, exited, tumbled, and struck his right leg in multiple places, creating four entry and exit wounds with a grapefruit-sized cavity of missing tissue. Despite severe bleeding, Mojo drew his Glock and provided covering fire while scooting to safety.

Nores returned fire as the gunman appeared around a corner. The engagement lasted 8–15 seconds. All officers held their positions and no further shots were fired. Mojo was airlifted by helicopter after three hours of field treatment, barely avoiding shock and death from blood loss. A grand jury later exonerated all officers involved. The incident made national news—the first time a law enforcement officer had been shot by a marijuana grower in U.S. history.

The shooting catalyzed change. Nores and his allies began embedding with SWAT and sniper teams, training at their own expense and on their own time. This grassroots effort eventually led to the formation of the Marijuana Enforcement Team (MET) in 2013, a dedicated tactical unit with proper equipment, trauma medical support, and integration with military special forces paramedics.

Environmental devastation and booby traps

Nores describes the environmental toll of cartel grows: water diversions that dry up creeks for miles, killing steelhead trout and endangered frogs; thousands of gallons of water stolen daily; and massive amounts of trash—propane tanks, fertilizer, human waste, old clothes, and sleeping bags—left behind.

More disturbing are the booby traps. Nores encountered Vietnam-era punji pits—sharpened bamboo stakes buried in concealed holes—recreated by cartel growers. In one case at Whiskeytown National Park, a cartel had coated punji pit spikes with carbofuran, the EPA-banned nerve agent. A canine handler's dog, Phoebe, detected the trap and alerted before a sniper could step into it. The poisoned spikes were designed as anti-personnel weapons to protect grow sites from law enforcement and wildlife.

Nores argues that these tactics represent guerrilla warfare. Vietnam veterans he has spoken with recognize the parallels to tactics used against American soldiers in Southeast Asia.

The poison weed problem

Carbofuran and other organophosphate insecticides banned by the EPA over 20 years ago are being smuggled into Tijuana and distributed throughout California's grow sites. Cartels mix these neurotoxins with water and spray them on plants or place drops in tuna cans around camps to kill wildlife. The poison dries invisibly within 24–48 hours, making it undetectable.

Law enforcement officers who have entered contaminated grows report going blind for days, experiencing severe nerve pain, respiratory problems, and vision loss lasting 8–10 days. Wildlife—bears, deer, and other animals—die from ingesting even small amounts. Nores presents photographs of a mother bear and her cub, both dead from poisoning, the cub having climbed into an oak tree as it died.

Cartels call this poison "El Diablo" because they know how lethal it is, yet they continue using it because it is so effective at protecting plants and killing predators. Federal OSHA eventually required law enforcement to wear hazmat suits and use specialized protocols before entering contaminated sites.

California's Prop 64 and the unintended consequences

When California legalized recreational cannabis under Proposition 64 in 2016, Nores and other law enforcement hoped regulation would eliminate the black market. However, the law watered down penalties: illegal growing went from a felony to a misdemeanor, and juvenile cultivation became merely an infraction—equivalent to a seatbelt violation.

Nores presented to growers' associations and legislators, warning that without strong deterrence, cartels would continue to dominate. His predictions proved accurate. The regulated market's high overhead—10–11 permits costing $80,000–$200,000 just to sell the first plant—made legal cannabis more expensive than cartel product. Cartels, using poisoned weed and Chinese chemicals, undercut legitimate growers and captured market share.

Nores argues that if cannabis had been regulated like tobacco and alcohol—with quality control, taxation, and age restrictions—most of the black market would have been eliminated. Instead, California became a cautionary tale that other states copied, spreading the problem nationwide.

China's entry into the cannabis trade

Around 2018, Nores began noticing Chinese criminal organizations moving into remote Northern California counties like Siskiyou. Initially puzzling—why would Chinese groups enter a market dominated by Mexican cartels?—the answer emerged through DEA intelligence and a debrief with a captured cartel plaza boss.

The Chinese saw the black-market cannabis trade as an opportunity to launder money and accumulate wealth outside the U.S. dollar system. Rather than competing with cartels, they partnered with them. The arrangement works as follows:

The money laundering scheme: Mexican cartels lose approximately 6% of their black-market revenue to money laundering costs. Chinese organizations offered to reduce this to 5% in exchange for investment in Chinese banking institutions and legitimate businesses. Cartels would convert their cash through these channels, and Chinese groups would gain access to the lucrative black-market cannabis trade.

Fentanyl precursor supply: Chinese chemical factories produce fentanyl precursors that are so tightly regulated they are not even used domestically in China. Yet these precursors are exported to Canada and Mexico for cartel use. In return, cartels purchase these chemicals and invest in Chinese financial institutions, creating a symbiotic relationship.

Geographic expansion: Chinese groups are buying land in remote U.S. counties, particularly in Northern California and now spreading to Oklahoma, Maine, and Michigan. They are growing cannabis using Chinese-supplied neurotoxins and exporting the poisoned product nationwide.

Nores argues this represents a form of soft power—a long, slow erosion of American public health and national security that benefits both China and the cartels while harming U.S. citizens and wildlife.

The Northern border crisis

With the Southern border tightened under recent administrations, cartels have shifted their primary trafficking corridor to the Northern border. Nores, who now lives 30 miles from the Canadian border in Montana, describes a 5,000-mile frontier that is largely unpatrolled, heavily forested, and ideal for trafficking.

Unlike the Southern border, which has walls and increased security, the Northern border consists of simple two-track roads and international boundary signs. Snow-covered glaciers in Glacier National Park mark the border—a line thinner than a power line on Nores' property.

Fentanyl precursors from China arrive at Canadian ports, are synthesized in dirty labs in Canada, and are then carried by backpack across the border into Montana, Washington, and other states. A single trafficker can carry up to one million fentanyl pills. Nores notes that border patrol agents, while dedicated, lack the resources and manpower to patrol 5,000 miles of remote terrain.

The fentanyl crisis is particularly acute in rural Montana. Narcan inhalers are free at every pharmacy in Nores' small hometown. The pills produced in dirty labs are inconsistent in potency and often lethal. Unlike the cartel's approach to marijuana (where they want customers to survive), fentanyl production is indiscriminate—dirty labs produce pills that kill users.

The broader national security threat

Nores argues that this crisis should be the nation's top priority, above many foreign threats. He emphasizes that Americans must be safe at home first. The poisoned cannabis, fentanyl, and cartel violence represent an existential threat to communities nationwide.

He has testified twice before Congress (2024–2025) on the "Protect Our Borders, Save Our Parks" hearing, focusing on environmental impacts and the broader cartel threat beyond the Southern border. He is also producing a documentary series called "Change Agents" with Ironclad, hosted by Andy Stump, that will expose the origins of the problem in California and its evolution into a national and international crisis.

Nores calls for:

  • Naming this a national priority and allocating resources comparable to foreign military threats.
  • Educating children in schools about the real dangers of cartel-produced cannabis and fentanyl.
  • Securing the Northern border with technology and personnel.
  • Reforming funding mechanisms so law enforcement is rewarded not just for plant counts but for environmental reclamation, weapons seized, and arrests made.
  • Considering nationwide cannabis regulation to eliminate the black market, though acknowledging the tension between corporate consolidation and small-scale organic growers.

Conservation vs. preservation

Throughout the conversation, Nores emphasizes the distinction between conservation (active management of wildlife populations through hunting and fishing) and preservation (protection without harvest). He argues that hunters and anglers, through license sales and the Pittman-Robertson Act (established over 100 years ago), fund the vast majority of wildlife management in America.

He uses examples like California's protected mountain lion population (approximately 5,000 with no hunting pressure) and Montana's balanced approach to mountain lions, wolves, and grizzly bears (where hunting is allowed and populations remain stable). Nores contends that ethical, legal hunting is essential to maintaining ecological balance and preventing overpopulation, which leads to predation on livestock and pets.

He acknowledges the emotional divide between hunters and animal rights advocates but argues that both groups share a common goal: protecting wildlife and wild lands. The method differs, but the heart is in the right place.
