Michael Burry issues final warning: "it's like 1929 all over"

RE-V examines Michael Burry's latest market warning, arguing that the U.S. is experiencing an unprecedented "dueling asset bubble" where both stocks and housing are trading at unsustainable valuations relative to GDP. The analysis reveals that Nvidia—the world's largest company—faces severe downside risk due to extreme customer concentration, while the broader economy remains artificially propped up by inflated asset prices driving consumer spending.

Key insights

  • Historic dual bubble: For the first time in U.S. history, stocks (250% of GDP) and housing (146% of GDP) are simultaneously in bubble territory, with valuations disconnected from underlying economic growth.

  • Nvidia's vulnerability: The world's $5 trillion company derives 64% of its accounts receivable from just three customers, creating unsustainable concentration risk that Burry argues could trigger a collapse worse than its previous crashes (56% in 2018, 67% in 2021, 43% in 2025).

  • Earnings growth masks fragility: While S&P 500 earnings are up 38% year-over-year, this metric is highly predictive of recessions; any slowdown in earnings growth would signal imminent stock market decline and consumer spending contraction.

The dueling asset bubble

RE-V presents data showing that stocks and housing have never been in a major bubble simultaneously in U.S. history. In 2006, housing peaked at 163% of GDP while stocks remained moderate; in 2000, stocks peaked at 160% of GDP while housing was around 100%. Today, both are inflated, with the stock market valued at roughly $76 trillion (Wilshire 5000) against a $30 trillion economy.

The Shiller-Cape P/E ratio shows stocks trading at a 41x trailing 10-year earnings multiple—the second-most expensive valuation ever, second only to 1999 and approaching 1929 levels before the Great Depression.

Nvidia's concentration risk

Burry's Substack analysis reveals that Nvidia's top three customers account for 64% of the company's accounts receivable. While the largest customer is likely Microsoft, others probably include Google, Meta, OpenAI, and Oracle. RE-V questions how a $5 trillion company can sustain operations when two-thirds of revenue depends on three customers, especially given the "incestuous relationship" among tech giants who buy and sell services to each other while using debt to finance growth.

The AI spending paradox

RE-V notes that Burry argues AI token demand is peaking as companies complete initial model training. Additionally, the cost of AI compute has become prohibitively expensive—autonomous AI agents now cost $100,000–$300,000 annually, and recent reporting suggests AI is more expensive than hiring human workers. This contradicts the narrative that AI will drive widespread job losses and raises questions about the sustainability of the AI capex buildout.

Consumer spending and the wealth effect

Personal savings rates have plummeted to 2.6%, meaning Americans are spending nearly all their income. This spending is increasingly dependent on the "wealth effect"—consumers feeling confident because their 401(k)s and home valuations (Zestimate) are rising. If stock and housing prices decline, consumer spending will contract, leading to lower corporate earnings, which will further depress asset valuations in a vicious cycle.

Retail sales and S&P 500 earnings growth remain strong, but RE-V warns that earnings growth is a leading indicator of recession. Once earnings growth slows from current 38% year-over-year levels to flat or negative, a recession becomes imminent.

Interconnected risks

The economy is now tightly coupled: AI capex drives Nvidia revenue, which inflates tech stock prices, which makes consumers feel wealthy and spend more, which drives retail sales and corporate earnings. If any link breaks—if data center demand slows, if token spending declines, or if companies cut AI budgets—the entire chain could unwind.

Housing market exposure

RE-V identifies geographic areas most vulnerable to a stock market correction: regions with high shadow inventory (over 10% of housing stock held off-market) and expensive second/third home markets, particularly in Florida, the Northeast (Maine, Vermont, upstate New York), Arizona, Colorado (Aspen, ski towns), and California (Lake Tahoe). Tech-dominated markets with median home prices exceeding $700,000—San Jose, San Francisco, Jackson Hole, parts of Utah, Steamboat Springs, Breckenridge—also show elevated shadow inventory and would likely see the steepest price declines if the stock market falters.

The timing question

RE-V emphasizes that an immediate crash is unlikely as long as earnings growth remains strong. However, the longer valuations remain disconnected from fundamentals, the greater the pressure on companies like Nvidia to deliver unsustainable growth. Any slip in revenue growth or earnings could trigger a cascade of selling, consumer spending contraction, and housing market weakness in vulnerable regions.
