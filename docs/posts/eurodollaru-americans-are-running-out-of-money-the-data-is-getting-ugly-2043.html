Rising fears of joblessness and falling demand show that Americans are bracing for economic weakness, even as the Federal Reserve misinterprets high prices for inflation.

The 44.4% warning: rising anxiety over unemployment

According to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York’s latest survey of consumer expectations, the perceived probability that the US unemployment rate will be higher one year from today has climbed to 44.4%. Eurodollar University highlights that this is the highest reading since April 2020, a period marked by a historic employment collapse.

This anxiety is compounded by the belief among consumers that finding a new job after a layoff would be exceptionally difficult, with confidence levels hovering around lows last seen in 2012. This shift in sentiment alters consumer behavior long before layoffs show up in official statistics. When workers fear that a job loss could lead to prolonged unemployment, they immediately adopt defensive financial postures—delaying major purchases, building cash reserves, and cutting discretionary spending.

The housing market reality: inventory rises as demand collapses

The defensive behavior of households is highly visible in the housing market. Existing home sales for August fell below an annualized rate of 4 million, marking one of only two times this has occurred since late 2024.

While industry groups like the National Association of Realtors (NAR) have historically blamed a shortage of available properties for weak transaction volumes, the data no longer supports this narrative. The supply of previously owned homes rose 5.9% year-over-year to 1.62 million in August—the highest level since November 2019. At the current sales pace, this represents 4.9 months of supply, the highest inventory level in more than a decade. The fact that transactions are falling while inventory is rising suggests that demand, driven by labor market anxiety and income insecurity rather than just mortgage rates, is the true constraint.

Credit card caution: open-ended debt vs. pulled-forward loans

At first glance, the Federal Reserve's July consumer credit data—showing a $15 billion increase in total credit—suggests robust consumer confidence. However, Eurodollar University notes that separating this figure into revolving and non-revolving credit reveals a very different story:

  • Non-revolving credit (auto loans, student loans, and fixed-schedule financing) drove the vast majority of the increase. This borrowing was likely pulled forward by consumers fearing higher future interest rates, representing a shift in the timing of purchases rather than an expansion of organic purchasing power.
  • Revolving credit (primarily credit cards) grew by a meager $2.8 billion. Because consumers cannot easily pull forward everyday expenses like gasoline and groceries, revolving credit is a much more accurate gauge of current cash flow and income confidence. The stagnation in credit card borrowing signals deep consumer caution and tighter bank lending standards.

Necessity price shocks vs. broad inflation: the demand destruction mechanism

Consumers are acutely aware of rising costs for necessities. The New York Fed survey shows households expect significant price increases over the next year: gasoline up 4.6%, food up 5.3%, rent up 6.6%, medical care up 9.1%, and college up 6.1%.

Crucially, consumers are not translating these individual price shocks into expectations of a broader, self-reinforcing inflationary spiral. Median one-year inflation expectations actually declined for a third consecutive month to 3.58%, while three-year and five-year expectations remained stable at 3.2% and 3% respectively.

This distinction is vital: when the cost of unavoidable necessities rises while employment prospects weaken, the result is demand destruction rather than inflation. Because households must pay more for food and fuel, they have less money left for discretionary purchases. This process is inherently disinflationary for the broader economy, as businesses eventually lose the pricing power to pass on additional costs.

The bond market's verdict: flat curves and policy errors

The bond market continues to align with consumer behavior rather than the Federal Reserve's hawkish rhetoric. If investors genuinely feared a sustained, wage-price inflation spiral triggered by energy shocks, long-term inflation break-evens would rise and the yield curve would steepen. Instead, the yield curve remains historically flat and inflation break-evens remain benign.

Eurodollar University warns that the Federal Reserve risks mistaking "impoverishment for inflation." Rate hikes cannot produce more oil, speed up refineries, or lower grocery bills. By continuing to treat supply-side price shocks as demand-driven inflation, policymakers are tightening credit into a household sector that is already losing confidence and bracing for a weaker labor market.
