RE-V | Meta CEO warns of AI bubble. it's like 1999, but worse.

An analysis of the massive AI infrastructure spend, warning signs of overcapacity from Meta and Google, and the systemic risks facing the US economy.

The AI CapEx surge and its economic weight

Nick from reVenture highlights that the artificial intelligence boom has reached unprecedented levels, with capital expenditure (CapEx) on data centers and computer equipment accounting for nearly 50% of US GDP growth in the first quarter of 2026. This massive spending is highly concentrated, with just seven companies—Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle, Coreweave, and Tesla—representing 90% of the total investment.

According to Nick, the vast majority of this capital is directed toward purchasing expensive GPU chips rather than physical infrastructure, with the physical data center shells accounting for only 3% to 5% of total costs. He argues that this extreme concentration of economic growth in a single sector makes the broader US economy highly vulnerable to any sudden slowdown in tech spending.

Signs of overbuilding: Meta and Google's financial strain

The narrative of unlimited demand for compute is showing its first cracks, Nick argues, pointing to recent actions by major hyperscalers: * Meta's excess capacity: In mid-2026, Meta announced plans to sell its excess AI computing power to outside competitors, including Anthropic and OpenAI. Nick views this as a clear admission that Meta has overbuilt its data center capacity, signaling a potential slowdown in its future CapEx growth. * Google's negative free cash flow: For the first time since going public in 2004, Alphabet recorded negative free cash flow in the second quarter of 2026. This was driven by a massive $45 billion CapEx spend—double what it spent the previous year—which outpaced its $39 billion in operating cash flow.

Revenue growth versus corporate cost-cutting

While bullish observers point to surging revenues at frontier AI labs to justify the infrastructure buildout, Nick suggests this growth may not be sustainable. He cites estimates from Ticker Trends Research showing annualized revenue run rates (ARR) reaching $70 billion for Anthropic and $37 billion for OpenAI.

However, corporate clients are beginning to push back against ballooning AI costs. Citing reporting from the Wall Street Journal, Nick notes that businesses are increasingly adopting a mix of cheaper, lower-priced models—including open-source alternatives and models built in China—rather than relying solely on premium offerings from OpenAI and Anthropic. This shift could cause revenue growth for the leading frontier labs to slow down or even decline.

The broader economic risk and stock market valuations

Nick warns that the US economy is currently functioning like a "house of cards," supported primarily by AI infrastructure spending and resilient consumer spending. However, consumer resilience is heavily propped up by the wealth effect of a highly inflated stock market, even as personal savings rates hover near historic lows and inflation outpaces wages.

Using the 150-year cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio of the S&P 500, Nick points out that stock valuations are currently trading at nearly 40 times earnings—more than double the historical average of 17.9. Historically, the only times valuations reached these heights were in 1929 and 1999, both of which preceded catastrophic market crashes. If tech giants begin scaling back their AI CapEx, Nick warns it could trigger a sharp stock market correction, drag down consumer spending, and ultimately push the US economy into a recession.
