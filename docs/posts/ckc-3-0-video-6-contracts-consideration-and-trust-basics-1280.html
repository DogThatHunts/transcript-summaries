Overview of contracts and consideration

Brandon Joe Williams defines a contract as a promissory agreement between two or more parties that creates, modifies, or destroys a legal relationship. He emphasizes that a contract cannot exist without "consideration"—the legal value, motive, or price that induces a party to enter the agreement.

Williams contrasts contracts with torts, which he defines as civil wrongs or injuries independent of a contract. He argues that many legal professionals fail to understand this distinction, mistakenly equating torts merely with general damages rather than recognizing them as actions operating outside of contractual boundaries.

The argument against credit card agreements

Williams applies his definition of consideration to critique modern financial systems, specifically credit cards and loans. He argues that credit card agreements lack "adequate consideration." According to Williams:

  • Users "manufacture currency" for banks by signing unconditional promises to pay.
  • Banks receive 99% of the value of these instruments while only returning a fraction (such as 1% cash back) to the user.
  • This disparity constitutes a form of "peonage debt slavery" rather than a fair exchange of value.

To counter this, Williams claims he uses limited powers of attorney in his lawsuits to force automatic discharges of debts, though he acknowledges financial institutions generally refuse to discuss these strategies.

Trust basics and splitting ownership

Williams seeks to demystify trusts, dismissing complex terminology like "ecclesiastical" or "spendthrift" trusts as marketing gimmicks. He outlines the core components of a trust:

  • Settler/grantor: The individual who writes and establishes the trust.
  • Res: The asset or assets held within the trust.
  • Trustee: The party who holds legal ownership and administers the assets.
  • Beneficiary: The party who holds beneficial ownership and enjoys the economic benefits (income, profits, or usage) of the assets.

Williams strongly favors irrevocable trusts over revocable ones, asserting that irrevocable structures completely sever the grantor's connection to the assets, reducing the risk of external interference or blackmail.

Constructive trusts and certificates of title

Williams characterizes state-issued certificates, such as a vehicle's "certificate of title" from the DMV, as "constructive trusts." He argues that a certificate of title is merely an abstract representing a title held elsewhere, rather than actual ownership. He views this system as a fraudulent trust arrangement where the state acts as a trustee over private property.

Navigating banks with certificates of trust

To maintain privacy, Williams advises against showing a trust's private "indenture" (the founding contract) to banks. Instead, he recommends using a "certificate of trust" (or certification of trust), which provides only the necessary operational details.

He claims that major banks like BMO and Chase developed their own certificate of trust templates in response to his followers ("pickles") challenging bank demands for settlers' Social Security numbers (SSNs). Williams asserts that under these banking templates, providing an Employer Identification Number (EIN) for the trust is sufficient, eliminating the need for a settler's SSN.

Obtaining an EIN as a foreign entity

Williams outlines his method for obtaining an EIN from the IRS using Form SS-4 without disclosing an SSN or ITIN:

  • Line 7b (Responsible party): Williams instructs applicants to write "foreign" or "NA," claiming that as non-US citizens/persons, they are ineligible for an SSN.
  • Submission strategy: He recommends faxing the completed SS-4 form to the IRS's international fax number multiple times until it is approved, rather than waiting weeks for a single submission to process.
  • Operational mindset: Williams advises establishing stable banking relationships using standard trust accounts before attempting more aggressive legal battles or debt discharge strategies.
