New Homes Now Cheaper Than Old Ones: How the Federal Reserve Flipped the Housing Market

How rapid interest rate hikes inverted the housing market, why national averages hide local realities, and why banking on home equity can be an expensive illusion.

In this episode of the Between the Lies podcast, host Nikki P is joined by Luke Tatum and Rob Brayton of Perfect Spiral Capital to examine the rare economic inversion where new construction prices have fallen below the median price of existing homes. The discussion traces how aggressive post-2020 monetary expansion fueled dramatic price spikes across real estate, followed by rapid Federal Reserve rate hikes in 2022 that cooled new home development and triggered quarterly price drops. Rather than taking national statistics at face value, the hosts dissect regional development variations—such as urban revitalization in Cleveland versus outward suburban expansion in the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex—while reframing housing not as an infallible store of wealth, but as an illiquid, physically depreciating asset that requires careful strategic balance.

Key insights

  • Rate hikes broke the builder pricing premium: Successive Federal Reserve rate hikes starting in 2022 sharply increased mortgage rates toward 7.5%, forcing homebuilders in select markets to slash prices by up to 5% quarter-over-quarter, while existing home prices remained sticky due to locked-in low rates.
  • National medians obscure regional extremes: Aggregate data does not mean an identical new home is universally cheaper than an adjacent older home; the disparity reflects regional land values, builder incentives, and varying development dynamics across the country.
  • Sprawl versus infill alters construction costs: In expanding Southern and Sunbelt markets like Dallas–Fort Worth, builders can access cheaper perimeter land, whereas cities like Cleveland see targeted urban revitalization projects surrounded by distressed neighborhoods.
  • Physical structures are depreciating assets: While land and monetary inflation drive real estate sticker prices upward, the actual physical structure deteriorates over time and constantly demands maintenance capital.
  • Home equity is largely phantom wealth: Paper gains on an owner-occupied residence offer limited utility unless downsizing or leveraging, because selling into an inflated market still requires buying into an equally inflated replacement market.

The monetary catalyst behind the price inversion

Luke Tatum and Rob Brayton attribute the unprecedented pricing dynamic to dramatic shifts in Federal Reserve policy over the last several years. The unprecedented expansion of the M2 money supply following 2020 sparked 15% to 20% annualized price surges across real estate as ultra-low mortgage rates artificially stimulated demand.

When the Fed aggressively lifted interest rates in the second half of 2022—pushing the federal funds rate above 4% within months—the impact was immediate. Builders holding large pipelines of speculative inventory and facing buyer resistance had to adjust pricing downward across multiple quarters. In contrast, owners of existing homes holding sub-3% fixed mortgages refused to sell, choking resale inventory and keeping existing home prices buoyed above newly constructed properties.

Why national data paints an incomplete picture

The panel cautions against applying national median numbers to individual buying decisions. Tatum notes that statistical medians mash together wildly disparate housing submarkets. Rob Brayton highlights that the widely cited $25,000 national gap between new and existing homes swings dramatically by region, widening significantly in some areas while vanishing entirely in others.

The location and quality of land account for much of this variance. In high-growth regions like Texas, homebuilders purchase cheaper perimeter farmland to expand master-planned communities. In legacy Rust Belt metros like Cleveland, Nikki P points out that new construction often relies on cheap, urban infill lots positioned directly adjacent to distressed neighborhoods, skewing list prices lower due to underlying location risks rather than pure building efficiency.

Recency bias and the myth of permanent low rates

The conversation turns to the broad behavioral trap of recency bias. Tatum observes that many consumers and financial commentators operated under the assumption that near-zero interest rates were permanent because they persisted for the majority of younger investors' adult lives.

By viewing the market through a longer historical lens—citing the inflationary periods of the 1960s, 1970s, and 1980s—the group argues that cycles of tight money are standard economic reality. Tatum critiques internet financial influencers for pushing rigid formulas, reminding listeners that generational wealth planning requires multi-decade perspectives rather than reacting to short-term trends.

Reassessing homeownership as liquid wealth

Nikki P and the panel challenge the conventional wisdom of treating an owner-occupied home as an investment portfolio. Physical properties are depreciating assets that absorb substantial upkeep capital, while paper equity remains inaccessible without taking on expensive debt or relocating.

Brayton and Tatum advise listeners against locking all available net worth into illiquid real estate. Instead of fixating on sensationalized national housing headlines, prospective buyers and homeowners should tune out macro noise unless actively transacting, focusing instead on building liquid, productive assets capable of generating sustained income.
