The deepening mortgage demand recession

According to new data reported by CNBC, the U.S. housing market is experiencing a severe demand contraction. Refinance demand has dropped 18% for mortgages, reaching its lowest level since 2025, while purchase applications fell 0.4% week-over-week. These figures represent some of the lowest mortgage demand levels seen in the last 30 years.

Although a temporary rise in pending sales during April and early May led some to believe a recovery was underway, the host argues that the market remains mired in a historic demand recession. This lack of demand is starting to force sellers to lower their asking prices. The Wall Street Journal recently reported that home price growth slowed significantly in March, with the Case-Shiller National Home Price Index dropping to just 0.7% growth year-over-year.

A bifurcated market and the "double peak" mortgage rate lock

The current housing downturn is highly localized, creating a bifurcated market across the United States. While cities like Seattle have seen prices drop 2.5% year-over-year, markets like Chicago and Cleveland continue to experience price appreciation.

An analysis by JP Morgan suggests that the housing market is beginning to unfreeze in specific regions where inventory is rising and prices are dropping. The report also highlights a "double peak" in the distribution of mortgage rates held by existing homeowners: * Peak 1: A large concentration of homeowners holding low rates between 2.5% and 3.5%. * Peak 2: An increasing number of homeowners holding rates between 5.5% and 7%.

The host notes that buyers will find much more room for negotiation with sellers who hold higher, more recent mortgage rates, as they lack the incentive to hold onto their properties compared to those locked into historically low rates.

The affordability crisis and the political debate over home values

The U.S. home price-to-income ratio currently stands at approximately 4.3, a level of unaffordability not seen since the 2006 housing bubble. Bloomberg reports that for affordability to return to historical averages, home prices would need to fall by 15% to 20%, or incomes would need to rise by 15%.

The host addresses comments made by Donald Trump, who stated during a cabinet meeting that he wants to protect home values for existing homeowners rather than let prices fall. The host counters this perspective, arguing that the crisis is not about a lack of hard work from buyers, but rather a fundamental mathematical disconnect between stagnant incomes and inflated home prices. Furthermore, the host shares a channel poll showing that 85% of respondents—including a majority of existing homeowners—actually want home prices to decline to alleviate secondary financial pressures, such as rapidly rising property taxes.
