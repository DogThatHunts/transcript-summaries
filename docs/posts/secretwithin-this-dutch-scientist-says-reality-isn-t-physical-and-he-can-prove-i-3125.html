The illusion of materiality and the rise of analytic idealism

Under the traditional framework of materialism, the physical world is primary, and consciousness is treated as an emergent byproduct—likened to steam rising from boiling water. Dr. Bernardo Kastrup, a computer scientist and philosopher, argues that this foundational assumption is modern science's greatest misstep. Through his theory of analytic idealism, Kastrup posits that consciousness is the fundamental, irreducible container of reality.

In this view, physical matter does not generate mind; rather, matter is simply what mental states look like when observed from the outside. For example, the physical brain is not the creator of thoughts, but rather the external, material representation of an individual's inner mental life. Kastrup argues that we never directly interact with an objective physical world. Every experience—from the coldness of a glass of water to the blue of the sky—is entirely processed and contained within conscious awareness.

The dashboard metaphor: why physical matter is a sensory interface

To explain how we navigate a reality that is fundamentally mental, Kastrup introduces the metaphor of an airplane dashboard. A pilot flying through clouds cannot look at the sky directly; instead, they rely on a dashboard of dials and sensors reporting altitude, airspeed, and direction. While these dials are highly useful and keep the plane aloft, they are merely representations of the sky, not the sky itself.

Kastrup argues that physical matter is our evolutionary dashboard. When no conscious observer is present to read the interface, the dashboard representation (matter) does not exist, even though the underlying reality being represented remains. This perspective aligns with the work of cognitive scientist Donald Hoffman, who argues in his 2020 book The Case Against Reality that evolution did not design human senses to perceive objective truth. Instead, human perception evolved as a simplified, user-friendly interface designed purely to keep us alive, hiding the complex machinery of reality behind simple sensory "icons."

Dissociation and the concept of "mind at large"

If reality is entirely mental, the question arises: why do we experience ourselves as separate individuals rather than a single, unified mind? Kastrup explains this through the psychological concept of dissociation. He suggests there is only one universal field of awareness, which he terms "mind at large." Individual living beings—humans, animals, and plants—are localized, dissociated aspects of this single field.

To ground this theory, Kastrup points to clinical studies of Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID), such as a 2014 review published in The Lancet Psychiatry. These studies demonstrate that a single human brain can host multiple distinct centers of consciousness, each possessing unique memories, beliefs, and even handwriting, separated by mental barriers. Kastrup scales this phenomenon globally, arguing that the apparent boundaries between individuals are dissociative membranes within the universal mind. When these boundaries thin—whether through meditation, trauma, psychedelics, or death—the individual consciousness begins to reintegrate with the whole.

Redefining divinity as universal consciousness

Kastrup does not shy away from theological implications, explicitly identifying the universal field of consciousness as God. However, he rejects the traditional, anthropomorphic depiction of a deity who judges, plans, or experiences human-like moods.

Under analytic idealism, God is defined as "being itself"—an omnipresent, omniscient, and omnipotent mind. Because this universal consciousness is the only thing that exists, everything in nature is an excitation or pattern within it. Kastrup explains that while the universal mind is omniscient, its knowledge is dissociated; what it experiences through one individual is temporarily closed off from what it experiences through another.

Telepathy, time, and the limits of free will

By placing consciousness at the center of reality, Kastrup's model provides a framework for phenomena often dismissed by materialist science:

  • Telepathy and paranormal experiences: Kastrup argues that because our individual minds are separated only by imperfect dissociative boundaries, these membranes can occasionally leak. Under intense emotional states like grief or love, information can pass between connected minds, making telepathy a plausible, natural occurrence rather than a miracle.
  • The nature of time: Rather than an external, physical dimension, Kastrup views time as a cognitive filing system used by the mind to organize experiences. This is supported by a 2024 study in Current Biology showing that the brain constructs its own perception of time based on internal markers, memory, and emotion, rather than tracking an objective clock.
  • The limits of free will: Kastrup challenges the concept of libertarian free will—the idea that one could have chosen otherwise in any given situation. He argues that while we possess agency and make genuine choices, those choices are strictly determined by who we are at that exact moment. Because we cannot be anyone other than who we are, our choices are inevitable, serving as a unfolding revelation of our true nature.
