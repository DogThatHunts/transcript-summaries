The global bond market is fracturing

The global financial system is showing synchronized signs of stress across major economies. Felix points out that Japan's 30-year government bond yield has reached its highest level in history. This is particularly critical because Japan carries a debt load of approximately 260% of its annual GDP. For decades, the Bank of Japan maintained near-zero interest rates by purchasing its own debt, but the bond market is now forcing yields upward as buyers back away.

This instability is not isolated to Japan: * Europe: A government bond sell-off starting in France is spreading to Italy, Belgium, and Greece. Crucially, even German yields are rising, indicating that traditional European safe havens are also experiencing pressure. * United States: Mortgage applications have plummeted to their lowest levels since 1995. The US government now spends $1.25 trillion annually on debt interest alone, rivaling its expenditure on Social Security.

Felix argues that mainstream explanations attributing rising yields to economic strength are incorrect. Instead, synchronized rising yields in weakening economies point to a systemic issue: excessive global debt and a loss of bond market confidence in sovereign issuers.

Why gold falls at the start of a crisis

Many investors are confused by the recent drop in gold prices during a period of market instability. Felix explains that gold falling at the onset of a debt crisis is a historical feature rather than a bug, driven by three primary factors:

  1. The strong dollar: Gold is priced in US dollars. When expectations persist that interest rates will remain higher for longer, the dollar strengthens, making gold mechanically more expensive for international buyers and depressing the paper price.
  2. Crowded trades: Following massive runs—with gold up over 60% and silver up over 100% in a year—the market became highly leveraged and crowded, leaving it vulnerable to corrections.
  3. Liquidity needs and margin calls: During market panics, institutional funds face margin calls and must raise cash immediately. Because gold is highly liquid, investors sell it first to cover losses elsewhere.

Felix highlights historical precedents for this behavior. During the 1973–1974 stock market collapse, gold went flat and then fell before embarking on an 8x rally. Similarly, in the 2008 financial crisis, gold fell 30% in the initial panic before tripling over the subsequent three years once central bank money printing accelerated.

The mechanics of financial repression

Governments facing unpayable debt loads rarely default outright. Instead, Felix explains, they deploy "financial repression"—a deliberate strategy to inflate the debt away by keeping interest rates below the rate of inflation. He references historical admissions of this strategy, citing a 2016 statement by Joe Biden asserting that a government never has to default because it can print money, as well as similar sentiments previously expressed by former Federal Reserve Chairman Alan Greenspan.

The consequences of this policy include: * Purchasing power erosion: At a modest 5% annual devaluation rate, savers lose half of their purchasing power in just ten years, even though the nominal balance in their bank accounts remains unchanged. * The hours of work index: To bypass manipulated official inflation metrics, Felix developed an index measuring asset prices in terms of labor hours. Since the year 2000, the labor hours required for an average worker to purchase a standard basket of assets have more than tripled.
