The foods, drinks, and daily habits wrecking your brain

Five key insights

  1. Microglia, not amyloid, are the key. When the brain detects inflammation anywhere in the body, supportive M2 immune cells (microglia) shift into destructive M1 mode, eating away at synapses and neurons. This shift is the fundamental mechanism in all neurodegenerative conditions, head injury, long COVID, PTSD, and depression—not beta-amyloid accumulation.

  2. The amyloid hypothesis is built on fraud. Perlmutter argues that the original foundational study showing amyloid causes Alzheimer's, published in Nature, had altered images and was retracted. A 2019 study found 75% of cognitively intact seniors had significant amyloid in their brains, proving amyloid alone doesn't cause dementia. FDA-approved anti-amyloid drugs show "trivial" effectiveness and carry a 25% risk of brain hemorrhage.

  3. Ultra-processed foods are a direct threat. One serving per day of ultra-processed foods increases Alzheimer's risk by 13%; ten or more servings daily increases risk 2.7-fold. The problem isn't just sugar and carbs—it's modified fats, additives, and the inflammatory cascade they trigger.

  4. Lifestyle intervention beats pharmaceuticals. A study of Alzheimer's patients showed 70% stabilized or improved cognitive function through diet, exercise, and stress reduction—without drugs. The Poynter study found even unsupervised lifestyle changes prevented cognitive decline in metabolically compromised older adults.

  5. Ketones, DHA, and 40 Hz light are emerging tools. Raising ketone levels (via MCT oil, ketone esters, or GLP-1 drugs) gives microglia the energy to repair rather than destroy. High-dose DHA supports this. 40 Hz light therapy, pioneered by MIT's Li-Wei Tsai, shows promise for reducing neuroinflammation and may become standard care.

The amyloid hypothesis is dead

Perlmutter opens by challenging the mainstream narrative. He notes that a Cochrane analysis of 20,342 people across 17 studies found that monoclonal antibodies targeting beta-amyloid are "potentially very dangerous, if not life-threatening," with only trivial cognitive benefit. The original 2013 study supporting the amyloid hypothesis was found to have altered images and was retracted. Yet the FDA approved drugs based on amyloid reduction anyway—a shift in goalposts that Perlmutter calls "pharmaceutical marketing" at its worst.

Asprey adds: "It's like treating the smoke. Smoke doesn't cause fire." Perlmutter agrees, noting that amyloid is actually an antimicrobial peptide that helps the brain fight viruses and bacteria. Removing it may be counterproductive.

What actually causes neurodegeneration: the microglial shift

The real culprit, Perlmutter argues, is the shift of microglia from M2 (supportive) to M1 (destructive) phenotype. This happens when the brain senses inflammation from:

  • Metabolic dysfunction (obesity, diabetes, hypertension)
  • Gut permeability and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from bacterial toxins
  • Alcohol (which increases gut permeability)
  • Ultra-processed foods
  • Head injury
  • Chronic stress and unresolved trauma

Once shifted, M1 microglia stop nurturing neurons and synapses and start destroying them. They also shift to glycolysis—a far less efficient energy pathway—meaning mitochondrial function collapses. This is the fundamental mechanism in Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, frontotemporal dementia, long COVID, PTSD, and major depression.

Asprey notes this aligns with his bioenergetic model: "Any cell allocates energy based on how much energy it has. If you can give it more energy, it's gonna be better off."

Head injury and the chronic inflammation trap

Asprey shares his own experience: after taking a titanium knee to the head at high speed, he had a small brain bleed and lost his "perfect meditator's brain." His EEG showed scrambled patterns, and he became uncharacteristically angry (he even sent an angry email to Tim Ferriss that he later regretted).

Perlmutter explains why head injuries can worsen over time. The initial injury triggers M1 microglia activation—necessary for acute repair. But if preexisting metabolic issues (diabetes, obesity) are present, the microglia stay locked in destructive mode. A study showed type 2 diabetics had longer hospitalizations and worse outcomes after identical head injuries compared to non-diabetics.

The solution is to revert microglia back to M2 mode through:

  • Hyperbaric oxygen
  • High-dose DHA
  • Anti-inflammatory diet
  • Ketone elevation (via MCT oil, ketone esters, or GLP-1 drugs)

Asprey notes that football players and MMA fighters are at particular risk, and that raising ketones beforehand can help prevent long-term damage.

GLP-1 drugs: promise and caution

Asprey asks the critical question: are GLP-1s good or bad for brain inflammation? Perlmutter argues they're likely beneficial because improved metabolism leads to less microglial shift. A 2024 study in the New England Journal of Medicine found that Parkinson's patients on a GLP-1 agonist (lixosenatide) stabilized or slightly improved over a year, while placebo patients declined—a "home run" for a neurodegenerative disease.

However, Perlmutter emphasizes that GLP-1s treat the underlying metabolic problem, not the symptoms. An oral semaglutide trial for Alzheimer's didn't reach cognitive endpoints but did reduce C-reactive protein by 30%—a significant anti-inflammatory effect.

Both speakers acknowledge GLP-1 side effects are real (48% of Parkinson's trial participants had significant GI issues) and that long-term safety at high doses is unknown. But Asprey argues the risk-benefit math favors GLP-1s for neurodegenerative disease: "If you're gonna not know your name, require care, and put the car keys in the refrigerator, I think I would wanna do things that had more risk than I would if I was 20."

Perlmutter agrees: "I am today supportive of as low a dose as you need with enough protein and exercise and appropriate thyroid and testosterone."
