Chase Hughes argues that our smartphones are meticulously engineered to capture and control our attention, operating like a pervasive, self-purchased cult. He details how these devices exploit fundamental psychological principles, from seizing focus through notifications to leveraging variable ratio reinforcement (like a slot machine) and designing interfaces (infinite scroll, red dots, typing indicators) to keep us engaged. Hughes contends that phones fulfill the four criteria of a cult – controlling focus, authority, tribe, and emotion – but are far more effective due to their data-driven, personalized manipulation. He highlights the stark reality that despite spending a significant portion of our waking lives on phones, they are almost entirely absent from our dreams, suggesting our brains perceive this time as largely inconsequential to our core experiences.

The value of attention

Chase Hughes emphasizes that human attention is more valuable than gold, literally. He states that companies worth more than the GDP of entire countries base their balance sheets on this single asset: our attention. Statistically, the average person checks their phone 96 times a day, or once every 10 waking minutes, with heavy users exceeding 6,000 touches daily. This translates to scrolling the height of the Statue of Liberty with a thumb each day, performing free labor for these companies. The average US screen time is 4-5 hours daily, accumulating to 60 days per year. Hughes calculates that a 30-year-old, at current rates, will spend 8-10 years of conscious existence on their phone by age 80, a price he notes no one explicitly agreed to pay.
