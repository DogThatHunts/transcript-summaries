Consultancy dead? Consultancy lives.

Summary

VanClief argues that AI hasn't killed consultancies but has fundamentally disrupted their pricing models. The real problem isn't that AI can do the work—it's that large consultancies have built unsustainable employee bases that their AI-accelerated deliverables can no longer justify. The future belongs to smaller, leaner firms that compete on genuine opinion and expertise rather than billable hours and headcount.

Five key insights

  1. The pricing model, not the model itself, is breaking. AI makes deliverables faster and cheaper to produce, but major firms like McKinsey and Deloitte still have massive payrolls. The gap between what they can now charge and what they must pay employees is unsustainable.

  2. Assurance is the last moat. When a board hires McKinsey and a strategy fails, responsibility diffuses across the organization. AI can't yet replace this liability shield—you can't blame an algorithm the way you can blame a consultant.

  3. Technical opinion is irreplaceable. Even when AI writes the code, the decision about which technology to use, how to integrate it, and why it fits a specific business remains a human judgment call. That opinion—productized and delivered fast—is where technical consultancies create real value.

  4. Smaller firms can outcompete on speed and cost. A lean consultancy that teaches clients to use Claude or builds a "second brain" system can deliver strategy faster and cheaper than a firm paying hundreds of junior consultants to research and synthesize.

  5. HR and people consultancies are shifting from compliance to judgment. As automation handles hiring, onboarding, and payroll, the real value moves to helping companies make better people decisions and foster authentic engagement—which directly affects business outcomes.

The three core consultancy services

Advice consultancies (McKinsey model) - Deliver strategy, business plans, and research-backed recommendations - Historically charged by billable hours and training time - AI threat: Research and synthesis now happen faster, reducing billable hours and the value of templated knowledge - The real problem: Too many employees to justify lower per-project revenue

Build consultancies (IBM, technical agencies) - Create custom software, integrations, and technical solutions - Charge for both the artifact and the labor to build it - AI threat: Website creation, CRM setup, and basic development are now nearly instantaneous - Opportunity: Shift to more complex integrations and "second brain" systems that organize organizational knowledge

Assurance consultancies (compliance and risk) - Take on legal, compliance, and strategic risk so executives aren't personally liable - Charge for the insurance value of having a recognizable firm's name behind a decision - AI threat: Minimal—you still can't blame an algorithm the way you blame a consultant - Moat: Regulatory and reputational protection remains valuable

Where consultancies operate: Strategic areas

Strategy and business planning - Traditional domain of firms like McKinsey - AI opportunity: Smaller consultancies can now offer both strategic advice and technical implementation (teaching clients to use Claude, building AI-powered workflows) without the overhead of large teams - Competitive advantage: Speed, lower cost, integrated delivery

Management and operations - Handles organizational structure, employee workflows, process design - AI opportunity: "Second brain" or "company brain" systems—platforms that connect organizational charts, SOPs, and decision frameworks so employees can self-serve guidance - Shift: From consultants managing people to consultants building systems that enable self-management

Technical consultancies - The strongest position for competing across all categories - Why: Technical opinion (which language, which architecture, which integration approach) remains subjective and valuable even as AI handles coding - Example: Choosing between React and C# for a front end isn't a technical problem—it's an opinion problem shaped by team expertise, timeline, and business constraints - Moat: Productized judgment delivered faster than competitors

Financial consultancies (accounting, KPMG model) - Strong compliance moat but vulnerable to governance failures - AI risk: Scaling AI across thousands of employees creates surface area for mistakes; smaller firms can build compliance-first from the start - Barrier to entry: Regulatory requirements and laws that only large firms can afford—protects incumbents but slows innovation

HR and people consultancies - Traditionally handle hiring, compliance, payroll, legal protection - AI opportunity: Automate the paperwork and process work; redirect human effort toward authentic engagement, judgment calls, and employee fulfillment - Strategic shift: HR becomes a profit center (better judgment → better people → better business outcomes) rather than a cost center - Challenge: Most CFOs don't yet see people decisions as bottom-line financial drivers

The pattern: interaction and accessibility

Across all categories, the winning consultancies will be those that get their opinion into clients' hands faster, easier, and better. This means:

  • Smaller teams with deep expertise over large teams with shallow knowledge
  • Systems that amplify human judgment rather than replace it
  • Ability to teach clients to use AI tools rather than doing everything for them
  • Productized opinion that can scale without proportional headcount growth

What happens to the giants

VanClief notes that major consultancies are already experiencing the squeeze: - Lawsuits over AI misuse and pricing model issues (KPMG falsified information for major clients) - Layoffs across Deloitte, McKinsey, and others - Inability to justify current employee counts with AI-accelerated delivery - Governance challenges: Adopting AI across thousands of employees is slow, expensive, and risky

Smaller competitors can build compliance and AI governance into their foundation rather than retrofitting it.

The real question for consultancies

Not "Can AI do this work?" but "Can we deliver expert opinion faster and cheaper than our competitors while maintaining the trust and liability protection clients need?"

The firms that survive will be those that: 1. Right-size their teams to match their actual delivery capacity 2. Compete on genuine expertise and judgment, not process or headcount 3. Use AI to amplify human decision-making, not replace it 4. Build systems (second brains, technical platforms, HR automation) that create recurring revenue and scale without proportional labor costs
