The statutory scope of federal income tax

Champion states that since the enactment of the modern income tax in 1913, Congress has imposed the tax on only three distinct categories of persons: 1. Non-resident aliens receiving U.S.-source income. 2. Foreign corporations receiving U.S.-source income. 3. U.S. citizens residing abroad who earn foreign income and import it back into the United States.

Champion argues that treasury regulations and treasury decisions have consistently remained within these statutory guardrails for over a century to avoid constitutional scrutiny in federal court. He claims the post-World War II era expanded public propaganda to convince average citizens that their personal labor and private earnings were subject to federal income tax.

Constitutional precedents and excise taxes

According to Champion, the 16th Amendment did not create a new direct tax power, but rather corrected the Supreme Court's earlier ruling in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co. Champion points to Chief Justice Edward Douglass White's decision in Brushaber v. Union Pacific Railroad Co., which clarified that the income tax operates as an indirect excise tax on government-granted privileges.

Champion explains that an excise tax requires a voluntary activity involving a government privilege, such as international cross-border financial transactions or foreign commerce. Because earning a private living is an inherent right rather than a privilege, Champion argues that wages represent private property rather than taxable corporate profits or excise privileges.

Judicial influence and administrative tax forms

Champion highlights how administrative mechanisms reinforce widespread compliance among domestic workers. He states that Form W-4 is legally designed for individuals subject to mandatory withholding—such as foreign entities or workers within exclusive federal territorial jurisdiction—rather than private sector employees in the 50 states. However, employer policies and fears of non-compliance lead workers to sign the forms under penalty of perjury.

To explain why federal courts routinely reject tax code challenges, Champion points to judicial conferences organized by the Department of Justice. He claims that DOJ tax division prosecutors present seminars at these resort conferences, effectively training federal district and appellate judges to reject statutory and constitutional challenges to the tax system.
