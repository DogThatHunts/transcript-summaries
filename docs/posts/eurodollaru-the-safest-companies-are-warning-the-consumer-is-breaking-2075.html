The divergence between the Fed and the bond market

Jeff Snider highlights a massive disconnect between the Federal Reserve's rhetoric and the broader financial marketplace. While the Fed, the ECB, and mainstream media are hyper-focused on inflation risks due to rising oil prices, almost the entire marketplace—including the bond market, swap curves, and the US dollar exchange value—is looking in the opposite direction.

The Fed's hawkish stance is built on the assumption of a "resilient" economy. However, Snider argues that this resilience is an illusion. When the Fed threatens to raise rates, short-term yields rise, causing the yield curve to flatten because long-term yields are not pricing in future growth or inflation. This flattening is a clear signal that the market expects the Fed's policy to drag down the economy rather than respond to genuine economic expansion.

Corporate warnings and consumer demand destruction

Steven Bregman and Snider point to real-world corporate earnings as proof that the consumer is hitting a wall. Nestle recently shocked the market by reporting that when they tried to raise prices in North America, their sales volumes fell by 0.6% instead of rising as projected.

Bregman notes that consumers are becoming highly selective, cutting back on incremental discretionary purchases—such as grabbing an ice cream bar when filling up on gas—just to get through to the next payday. This behavior is also visible in the housing market, where pending home sales have experienced a significant summer crash. Rather than indicating a resilient economic background, these corporate reports validate that higher input costs are leading directly to demand contraction.

What the oil and TIPS curves are actually pricing

Although oil prices have surged recently due to geopolitical tensions in the Middle East, Snider explains that this rise is confined entirely to the front end of the futures curve. The back end of the curve (contracts for late 2026 and 2027) has barely moved, indicating that the market expects oil prices to be substantially lower in the future. This is not due to expectations of supply normalization, but rather expected demand destruction.

Furthermore, the Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) market supports this view. TIPS break-even rates have remained virtually unchanged during the recent oil spike. Snider points out that if there were a genuine long-term inflation risk, the TIPS market would price it in. Instead, the decoupling of TIPS from oil prices shows that the market expects any CPI increase to be short-lived and ultimately crushed by economic slowdown.

The labor market and the inevitability of rate cuts

Bregman argues that while headline labor numbers like initial jobless claims remain historically low, underlying indicators show a clear deceleration. ADP employment reports show a continued week-over-week slowdown in job gains, and average hourly earnings are on a long-term downward trend.

The Fed's current model dictates that they must focus on inflation until the labor market explicitly breaks. Bregman and Snider warn that by the time the Fed acknowledges job losses, the economic damage will already be done. They predict a repeat of previous cycles where the Fed aggressively hikes rates into a weakening economy, only to be forced into rapid rate cuts just weeks later as the labor market deteriorates.
