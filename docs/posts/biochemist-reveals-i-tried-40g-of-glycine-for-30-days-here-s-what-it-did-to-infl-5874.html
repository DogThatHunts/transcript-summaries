Biochemist Reveals: I Tried 40g of Glycine for 30 Days – Here’s What It Did To Inflammation

Biochemist Joel Brind answers audience questions on glycine dosing, cancer research, blood pressure, creatine interactions, and his personal 40-gram self-experiment.

Summary

In this Q&A episode, biochemist Joel Brind — author of The Glycine Miracle and creator of the Sweetamine supplement — responds to listener questions about glycine supplementation. He addresses recent research linking sarcosine (a glycine metabolite) to cancer cell behavior, explaining that the findings rely on artificial cell-culture models that do not translate to whole-body physiology. Brind argues that systemic inflammation — driven by a widespread glycine deficit of roughly 8–10 grams per day — creates a pro-cancer environment, and that restoring glycine balance likely delays cancer onset and progression. He also covers practical topics: glycine’s minimal effect on blood pressure (except in rare paradoxical responders), its role as a creatine precursor, the need to pause supplementation before kidney-function blood tests, and the limited glycine contribution from trimethylglycine, glutathione, or magnesium glycinate. Brind recounts his personal 30-day trial of 40 grams daily, noting no dramatic changes until acute stressors — a tailbone fracture and a severe sunburn — healed with almost no inflammation. He describes similar resolution of chronic periodontitis, painless shingles, and a mild COVID-19 course, attributing these outcomes to glycine’s ability to curb excessive immune overreaction. Throughout, Brind emphasizes that glycine is safe for the vast majority, but urges anyone with serious medical conditions to consult a clinician rather than self-experiment.

Key insights

  • Sarcosine–cancer link is an artifact of cell-culture models. Brind explains that the widely cited study used RWPE cells — “pseudo-cancer” controls that turn malignant only when stimulated with high sarcosine or androgens — and that the authors later retracted the finding that glycine itself had this effect.
  • Systemic inflammation from glycine deficiency fuels cancer development. Brind argues that a chronic 8–10 g/day glycine gap creates a methionine-rich, glycine-poor milieu that selects for methionine-dependent, glycine-efficient cancer cells; restoring glycine rebalances this terrain.
  • Glycine does not raise or lower blood pressure in most people. Rare individuals may experience transient hypotension (fainting, dizziness) from rapid absorption of free glycine; bone broth or collagen peptides avoid this by slowing absorption.
  • Supplementation can skew kidney-function tests. Glycine raises urea and creatinine synthesis, potentially mimicking impaired renal function on standard metabolic panels; Brind recommends stopping glycine 72 hours before blood draws.
  • Personal high-dose trial revealed anti-inflammatory effects only under stress. At 40 g/day for a month, Brind felt no baseline difference, but a tailbone fracture and severe sunburn healed with minimal pain and no lingering inflammation — plus resolution of periodontitis, painless shingles, and a non-respiratory COVID-19 case — suggesting glycine prevents immune overreaction to injury and infection.

Cancer research and the sarcosine paper

  • Brind details why the prostate-cancer cell study does not implicate dietary glycine: the control cells (RWPE) are derived from a benign tumor that becomes invasive only when artificially stimulated; the sarcosine concentrations used were far above physiological levels; and the authors later revised their abstract to state that glycine did not induce malignant behavior.
  • He emphasizes the leap from petri-dish conditions to whole-body dynamics, noting that cancer ecology involves Darwinian selection of rogue cells in an inflammatory, methionine-high/glycine-low environment — a terrain glycine supplementation helps correct.
  • Animal data show glycine-supplemented animals still develop cancer but die later, supporting a delay rather than absolute prevention.

Blood pressure, thyroid, and absorption concerns

  • Brind discloses his own hypertension (hyperaldosteronism from a gene mutation) managed with losartan; he states glycine has no general effect on blood pressure up or down.
  • For low blood pressure, his daughter takes glycine without issue; only rare paradoxical responders might feel faint from rapid glycine absorption.
  • Thyroid and vitamin-absorption questions are deferred to medical professionals; Brind reiterates he is a PhD scientist, not a clinician.

Creatine, trimethylglycine, and related supplements

  • Glycine is a direct precursor of creatine; supplementation may raise endogenous creatine/creatinine, which can elevate the creatinine-based kidney-function algorithm — hence the 72-hour washout before blood tests.
  • Taking creatine supplements does not replace the need for glycine’s anti-inflammatory role; the body makes creatine from glycine, not vice versa.
  • Trimethylglycine (betaine) helps low-methylators (10–20% of population, often MTHFR C677T mutation) by feeding an alternative methionine-regeneration pathway, but it yields glycine inefficiently and does not close the glycine gap.
  • Glutathione (~20% glycine by weight) and magnesium glycinate (~86% glycine) contribute meaningful glycine — e.g., 6 g glutathione ≈ 1.2 g glycine; two 300 mg magnesium glycinate tablets ≈ 2 g glycine — but still fall short of the 8–10 g target.
  • N-acetylcysteine (NAC) supports glutathione recycling (useful with acetaminophen) but supplies no glycine.

Kidney disease, low platelets, and serious illness

  • Anyone with diagnosed kidney disease should test renal function off glycine first, because supplementation can falsely elevate creatinine/urea markers.
  • For complex cases (e.g., thrombocytopenia, prior heart attack, active cancer), Brind insists on physician supervision; nutritional advice assumes generally healthy physiology.
  • He notes zero reported harms in 15 years and millions of Sweetamine servings, but cautions against substituting supplements for medical care.

Dosing, timing, and personal 40-gram experiment

  • Brind’s standard dose: 8 g/day (one Sweetamine packet), taken all at once or split morning/evening; timing is flexible unless using glycine for sleep support (then take at night).
  • Glycine acts as an inhibitory neurotransmitter and promotes core-temperature drop, aiding sleep onset for many; paradoxical responders may feel stimulated instead.
  • 40 g/day for 30 days: no noticeable baseline changes, but two “aha” events — a 4-foot fall onto concrete (tailbone bruise only, danced that night, fine next day) and a severe June solstice sunburn that faded without pain or peeling — revealed glycine’s power to suppress inappropriate inflammation.
  • Subsequent observations: periodontitis resolved (cleanings no longer bloody), shingles at 64 was painless, COVID-19 in 2021 caused fever and malaise but zero respiratory symptoms (no cough, wheeze, or shortness of breath).
  • Brind frames these as evidence that glycine deficiency causes immune overreaction to injury, infection, and commensal bacteria (e.g., oral microbiome, herpes zoster, SARS-CoV-2 cytokine storm).
