EurodollarU | Is the U.S. Secretly Bailing Out the Japanese Yen?!

An analysis of why joint US-Japan currency interventions fail to rescue the yen, as underlying economic risks and structural debt continue to drive capital away.

In this episode of Eurodollar University, hosts Jeff Snider and Steve Van Meter analyze the ongoing depreciation of the Japanese yen, which recently fell past 163 against the US dollar. Despite an estimated $53 billion in recent interventions and rumored coordination between US and Japanese authorities, the hosts argue that these short-term fixes cannot overcome structural economic realities. They explain that the Bank of Japan's rate hikes and the government's fiscal stimulus packages are backfiring; instead of attracting capital, they signal economic desperation and increase systemic risk. Ultimately, Snider and Van Meter contend that global investors are shunning Japanese assets on a risk-adjusted basis, meaning that further government intervention may paradoxically accelerate the yen's eventual collapse.

Key insights

  • Interventions only provide temporary relief: While rumored joint US-Japan interventions briefly pushed the yen from 163 back to the 159 range, Steve Van Meter notes that these moves merely offer short-sellers a more attractive entry point to resume shorting the currency.
  • The textbook interest rate model is failing: Traditional monetary theory dictates that raising interest rates should strengthen a currency, but the Bank of Japan's rate hikes have failed to stop the yen's decline because market-driven risk factors are rising faster than nominal returns.
  • Rate hikes are driving bond flight: Jeff Snider points out that the Bank of Japan's rate hikes are prompting major domestic bondholders to abandon Japanese Government Bonds (JGBs) in search of safer, higher-yielding foreign assets, further weakening the yen.
  • Fiscal stimulus signals economic weakness: The Japanese government's attempts to stimulate the economy through tax cuts and deficit spending validate the market's negative outlook, signaling to investors that the underlying economy is in deep trouble.
  • The risk-adjusted return of Japan remains unfavorable: Despite rising nominal yields and a booming Nikkei index driven by the AI bubble, the massive debt burden and weak economic fundamentals mean Japan's risk-adjusted returns continue to deteriorate.

The failure of short-term currency interventions

Despite Japan burning through an estimated $53 billion in reserves to prop up the yen, the currency's recovery has been short-lived. Steve Van Meter argues that central bankers mistakenly blame speculative hedge funds for the yen's weakness, believing that a coordinated "squeeze" will normalize exchange rates. While joint efforts between the US and Japan temporarily moved the yen from 163 back to 159, Van Meter explains that this does not change the structural trend. Instead of deterring short-sellers, these artificial spikes simply provide speculators with a better entry point to short the yen again, creating a continuous cycle of intervention and depreciation.

The paradox of rising interest rates and capital flight

According to textbook monetary policy, when a central bank raises interest rates, capital should flow into the country and strengthen the domestic currency. However, the Bank of Japan's rate hikes have had the opposite effect. Jeff Snider notes that even though Japanese Government Bond (JGB) yields have risen and closed the gap with US Treasuries, the yen continues to weaken.

This paradox is driven by large Japanese institutional investors, such as life insurance companies, who are actively moving their money out of Japan. Because domestic rate hikes increase the volatility and risk of holding JGBs, these institutions prefer to sell their yen and purchase US dollars to invest in higher-yielding foreign assets. Consequently, the Bank of Japan's policy tightening is directly fueling the capital flight it was intended to prevent.

Fiscal desperation and the risk-adjusted return problem

Both hosts emphasize that global capital flows are driven by risk-adjusted returns, not just nominal yields. While Japan is offering higher nominal returns than before, its risk profile is deteriorating due to massive government debt, demographic challenges, and high energy costs.

When the Japanese government announces new fiscal stimulus packages or consumption tax cuts, it intends to project strength and stability. However, Snider argues that the market receives the opposite message: the government is desperate and acknowledges the economy is failing. This perceived risk offsets any nominal yield increases, making Japanese assets highly unattractive to global investors. Rumored policy coordination with US Treasury figures like Scott Bessent is viewed by the market as merely repackaging the same failed, debt-fueled economic strategies under a new name.

The systemic threat of a yen collapse

There is a growing concern that the yen is approaching a cliff edge. If the currency continues to slide, it may not do so in a gradual, linear fashion, but could instead suffer a rapid, uncontrolled plunge.

Van Meter warns that Japan's economic struggles are a leading indicator for the rest of the global economy, which is also heavily weighed down by debt. Furthermore, the rise of the Nikkei index—largely driven by the global AI bubble—represents a highly concentrated risk. If the AI bubble bursts, Japan will lose its primary source of speculative capital inflows, potentially triggering a domino effect that impacts global financial systems. Rather than preventing this outcome, persistent government interventions may paradoxically accelerate the yen's descent by proving to the market that authorities have run out of viable solutions.
