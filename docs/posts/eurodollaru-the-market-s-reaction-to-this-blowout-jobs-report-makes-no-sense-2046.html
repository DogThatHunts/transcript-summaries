EurodollarU | The Market’s Reaction to This Blowout Jobs Report Makes NO Sense

The bond market's muted response to a seemingly strong jobs report exposes the growing disconnect between unreliable government statistics and a struggling real economy.

One-paragraph summary

In this episode of Eurodollar University, host Jeff Snyder and co-host Steve analyze the market's surprisingly flat reaction to the August payroll report. Despite a headline "blowout" jobs number, interest rates and bond yields barely budged, signaling that the market no longer trusts the Bureau of Labor Statistics' (BLS) initial estimates. Snyder and Steve discuss how the BLS's outdated methodology consistently overstates job growth during economic transitions, leading to massive downward revisions months later. They argue that underlying indicators—such as stagnant average hourly earnings, dismal hiring rates in JOLTS and ADP data, rolling-over manufacturing order books, and falling labor force participation—reveal a far weaker "forgot how to grow" economy that the bond market is accurately pricing in, even as mainstream financial media remains focused on transient headline numbers.

Key insights

  1. The bond market ignored the blowout headline payroll number because investors have grown accustomed to massive downward revisions that eventually erase these initial "shocks."
  2. The BLS methodology is structurally flawed for the current economic environment, as its birth-death model assumes either a standard expansion or a recession, failing to account for a prolonged, low-growth "forgot how to grow" transition.
  3. Underlying labor metrics contradict the headline strength, with average hourly earnings flatlining, weekly hours stagnant, and the JOLTS hiring rate hitting its third-worst level of the current economic cycle.
  4. A "K-shaped" business economy is emerging where large corporations remain highly profitable, but small and medium-sized businesses are struggling with squeezed margins, leading them to freeze hiring rather than execute mass layoffs.
  5. International data corroborates the global slowdown, as seen in Canada's sharp August payroll decline and falling labor participation rates across the US, UK, and Canada as discouraged workers exit the labor force.

The illusion of the blowout payroll report

Jeff Snyder and Steve point out that "payroll Friday" used to be a highly anticipated, market-moving event. However, the market's lack of reaction to the August report reflects a profound skepticism toward official government statistics. Snyder highlights a pattern of massive downward revisions from previous months: for instance, May 2024's initial print of 272,000 was eventually revised down to just 78,000, and August 2024's initial 142,000 was revised down to virtually zero. Because these "wow" numbers are regularly revealed to be statistical illusions months later, the bond market now looks past the initial headline figures to focus on the broader, softer macroeconomic trend.

Structural flaws in BLS methodology

Snyder explains that the BLS's statistical models, particularly the birth-death model, are designed for a binary economic environment: either a robust post-war expansion or an outright recession. The current environment of stagnation—which Snyder terms the "forgot how to grow" economy—confuses these models. The adjustments systematically overstate job creation during the initial releases, forcing the agency to make quiet, substantial downward revisions later on when actual tax and benchmark data catch up.

Divergent indicators and the real economy

Steve emphasizes that if the labor market were truly booming, employers would be bidding up wages to retain staff. Instead, average hourly earnings for production and non-supervisory workers have flatlined, and average weekly hours worked remain stagnant. Other data sources confirm this weakness:

  • ADP private payrolls show a clear deceleration.
  • The JOLTS hiring rate has fallen to its third-worst level of the entire cycle, indicating that while mass layoffs are not yet widespread, hiring has ground to a halt.
  • ISM manufacturing data reveals that while inventory building has kept headline numbers positive, new order books are rolling over, and the services sector's employment sub-index is in contraction.

The squeezed consumer and small business reality

To illustrate the pressure on the average American, Snyder shares data from Circle K convenience stores. While nominal gasoline revenues jumped 33% year-over-year due to higher prices, physical fuel volumes actually fell by 1%. This means consumers are paying significantly more for less, which Snyder describes as "the very definition of impoverishment."

This consumer squeeze directly impacts small and medium-sized businesses. Unlike large corporations, these smaller enterprises do not have the luxury of excess capital. Steve notes that these businesses are running bare-bones operations; they stopped hiring years ago because they cannot generate the revenue to justify expanding their staff, further depressing the overall labor market.

Global labor trends and participation rates

The hosts observe that this economic deceleration is not unique to the United States. Canada's August payrolls showed an unexpected decline of over 40,000 jobs, alongside a drop in the labor force participation rate. Snyder explains that falling participation rates in the US, UK, and Canada mask the true extent of unemployment. When jobs are scarce, discouraged workers simply stop looking and drop out of the official labor force, which artificially keeps the headline unemployment rate looking stable and resilient. Ultimately, the bond market recognizes these systemic weaknesses, anticipating that central banks will eventually be forced to continue cutting interest rates.
