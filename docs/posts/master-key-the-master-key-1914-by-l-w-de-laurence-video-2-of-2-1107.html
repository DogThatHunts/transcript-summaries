A 1914 self-help classic teaches mental discipline, memory cultivation, public speaking, and the power of concentrated thought to shape health, wealth, and character.

Part 4: mental culture and will training

Chapter 23, lesson 21: the nature of volition

  • Volition is the self-realization of an idea with which the self is identified; it is more than mere attention — it is the "cognitive spirit" that drives one until the end is reached.
  • An idea must be definite, identified with the self, and held at the focus of consciousness while inhibiting competing ideas.
  • The initial impulse calls upon the whole organism; attention is merely the attendant of this deeper striving force.

Factors that influence volition

  • Emotions and instincts: Emotions follow a sequence — perception, mental shock, bodily disturbance. They persist and attack any presenting object. Instincts are innate, independent of education, and modify only slightly through environment.
  • Desires and motives: A desire is a conscious longing for an end; its strength lies in the totality of one's desires. A motive induces action; real motives are often hidden and must be analyzed.
  • Suggestion: A process of accepting a proposition without logical grounds. Susceptibility depends on the suggester's personality, relation to the subject, the subject's knowledge, and receptiveness. Non-personal suggestions (books, ads) often wield greater power.

Types of will

Ten types are described; the Ideal Type is commended — it uses reason, impulse, assertion, habit, and reflection in balanced proportion.

Freedom of the will

  • Determinists and libertarians represent opposite sides of the same truth; self-determination combines both. One is free insofar as the self allows; resistance comes from within.

Chapter 24, lesson 22: education of the will

  • Know your type: The dominant type reveals your mental attitude; strive toward the Ideal Type.
  • Analyze motives: Real motives drive success; conflict between false and real motives retards progress.
  • Control emotions and impulses: Simulate the opposite emotion (bodily changes + memory) or analyze the emotion. Check bad impulses instantly during the brief reflection interval.
  • Qualities to cultivate: Industry (fight indolence with schedule), hope (faith in self, set milestones), cheerfulness (habit of mind, smile practice), politeness and courtesy (study models), charm (observe and analyze).
  • Self-reliance: Set the teeth (engages pugnacious instinct), develop self-assertion (concentrate on boldness, courage), avoid self-deprecation.
  • Will in action: Choose a definite life part, study it, and act it now; life is the stage manager.
  • Auto-suggestion: Used unconsciously by all; conscious use requires faith and acting the part. Affirmations must be meant and lived.
  • Influencing others: Conviction in tone, sense of power, control of unconscious language; prepare in private.
  • Exercise: Three daily breathing/movement drills (10 reps each) to maintain nervous tone.
  • Diet: Ten commandments — eat only when hungry, chew thoroughly, stop when appetite says enough, avoid meat for strength, fixed meal times.
  • Willpower in business: Decision, manner, repose, clear aim, specialization, faith, persistence, interest, pleasant evenings, avoid late-night problems, cultivate sleep.
  • Willpower in study: Balance enthusiasm/despondency, time-chart, tackle hard subjects at peak mood, one-hour max per subject, use odd minutes, leave work with appetite.

Part 5: memory and public speaking

Chapter 25, lesson 23: memory

  • No absolutely bad memory: Everyone remembers well what interests them; repetition + interest explains the paradox.
  • Memory defined: Active (conscious recall) and passive (associative revival); dependent on will and independent of will; physical (retention traces) and psychical (factors aiding recall).
  • Native retentiveness: Congenital, cannot be increased by training; only directed to full capacity.
  • No pigeonholes: Ideas persist as neural dispositions, not stored objects; recall = stimulation of traces.
  • Forgetting is possible: Brain lesions can destroy recall; memory is multiple, sense-specific.
  • Memory ≠ efficiency: Extraordinary memory can hinder; efficiency = seeing essentials, discarding non-essentials.
  • Mental attitude: Objective + interest + faith in ability = easy acquisition; doubt multiplies needed repetitions.
  • Imagination: Fills gaps in recall; witnesses unreliable; record events immediately.
  • Factors influencing retention: Emotion, vividness, recency, attention, associates, rhythm, health.
  • Association: By similarity, contiguity (forward/backward), unity/interest, repetition.
  • Recall: Spontaneous revival (worry), explicit revival/reinstatement, implicit revival (shadowy, imagination fills gaps). To recall a fact, visit its associates (name: appearance, context, initial letter).
  • Mental imagery types: Visual, auditory, motor (predominant type matters); experiments to discover yours.
  • Repetition: Distributed > massed; first repetition most important; conscious attention essential; rational learning yields savings decades later.
  • Reading rate: Fast reading aids immediate memory; guard against rapid forgetting with notes and review.
  • Rate of forgetting: Rapid at first, then stabilizes; revise early and often.
  • Decay with age: Gradual after 50; exercise controls it; proper names go first.
  • Art of forgetting: Essential for progress; disregard non-essentials, concentrate on essentials. Three rules: withhold attention, withhold repetition, substitute opposing idea.

Chapter 26, lesson 24: cultivation of memory

  • Fundamental laws: Concentrated attention, conscious repetition, clear aim/interest, utility, complexity (more associations), understanding, association.
  • Aids to remembering: Avoid mnemonics (irrational, decay fast) except for immediacy; use rational linking, ingenuity methods for figures, scoring method (pair learning).
  • Learning poetry: Whole method for ≤240 lines; mixed method for longer; talk about subject to reveal gaps; mark books, make notes.
  • Most economical learning: Mixed method — read whole, divide into logical sections, learn hard sections mixed, return to whole.
  • Minimum fatigue: Scoring method (pair accenting) — less fatigue, better results; apply to languages, sciences, etc.
  • Language study: ≤15 min sittings (Sir Richard Burton's method).
  • Most useful memory: Everyday affairs (where things are, names, appointments). Cure: make acts conscious ("I put my purse here"), associate acts with journey stages.
  • Words of advice: Defects, health, mental attitude, imagination, retention, association, recall, imagery, repetition, time rate, forgetting (precis writing), rational learning, economy (scoring method), attention.
  • Cautions: Learning verses doesn't improve memory generally; don't overlearn; avoid fatigue.
  • Key: Interest — multiplies associations, aids retention, makes mind inventive.

Chapter 28, lesson 26: the art of public speaking

  • Eloquence vs. speaking: Eloquence = study + intelligent concentration; modern appeal is to intellect, not emotion.
  • Set speech: Immerse in subject, master opposing view, talk with others, write full speech, condense to 3–4 key points, make speaking notes (quarter-sheets, red/black ink), learn striking phrases, don't become slave to notes.
  • Delivery: Notes openly on flat box; begin quietly, slowly, naturally; pause for absorption; intensity > loudness; natural gesture only; know time limit; no self-deprecation; simple diction, homely illustrations, humor sparingly; handle interruptions with wit/fairness.
  • Debating reply: Highest test — quick analysis, expose fallacies, single shaft of criticism; cannot rely on notes.
  • Don'ts: 50+ specific prohibitions (born orator myth, envelope notes, shouting, cheap laughs, fidgeting, over-quoting, over-perorating, over-time, reading, etc.).
  • Concentration & eloquence: Oratory = tone + time; sound logic = sinews; concentration sends flood of wisdom; Webster, Lincoln, Dickens speeches as models.

Chapter 29, lesson 27: concentration in composing a speech

  • Audience analysis: Words must fit audience; clarity of style essential (Matthew Arnold: "have something to say and say it as clearly as you can").
  • Everyday practice: Forge phrases aloud, read masters aloud, rehearse, get accustomed to own voice.
  • Nervousness: Curable by concentration/perseverance; rehearse aloud, look at audience beforehand.
  • Voice & expression: Distinct articulation, proper breathing, modulation, emphasis, pause (Newman's electric pause).
  • Fluency & conviction: Cicero — instruct, please, affect powerfully; Hamlet's advice to players.
  • Sincerity: "Cor ad cor loquitur" — heart speaks to heart; conviction triumphs even in failure.
  • Method: Write and learn (safest for beginners) or outline/improvise; never apologize; suit wit to audience; blend understanding and imagination (Dickens).

Chapter 30, lesson 28: after-dinner speaking

  • Personality: The intangible magnetism that moves audiences; printed speeches lose it.
  • Modern oratory: Moderation, restraint, understatement; short speeches (15 min); variety of themes.
  • Types of speakers: Memorizer, mere funny, sentimental, mouthy, flippant, pompous, interminable, mismatched.
  • Effective after-dinner speech: Capture at once; humor or lofty sentiment; naturalness, spontaneity; Lowell's "winged prose."
  • English vs. American: English = more preparation expected; American = event-driven, values speaking ability over service.
  • Lecturing: Plant germinal ideas, arouse curiosity, not satisfy it; define clear points, emphatic repetition; form own method; manage nervousness (sign of desire to do well).
  • Guard against: Inaudibility, shouting, apologies, false knowledge, condescension, pedagogic tone, anticipatory chuckles, unnegotiable climaxes, statistics, undue humility, quotations, forced playfulness, rhetorical outbursts, over-long, imitation, waiting for applause, self-congratulation, reading, insincerity.

Chapter 31, lesson 29: rational and moral education

  • Determinism: Moral education aims to make individual accept ideas determining future conduct.
  • Two slaveries: Slave of passions (evil) vs. slave of moral idea (good/liberty). Epicurus: "Become a slave to philosophy, and you will enjoy real liberty."
  • Authority vs. persuasion: Authority doesn't develop moral clairvoyance; persuasion respects apparent liberty.
  • Intelligence: Intelligere = to understand; ethical intelligence (distinguish good/evil) > technical brilliance.
  • Religion vs. rational morality: Dogmatic authority losing grip; rational morality appeals to those rejecting dogmas; Christians also need ethical intelligence.
  • Obstacles: Heredity, atavism, fortuitous influences, contagion (speech, reading, example); education = first degree (kindergarten), self-education = higher degree.
  • Self-education: Not voluntary wish; attraction from prior cultivation fixes attention; moral ideas = inherited ethical capital; ideal = idea carried to infinity (kindness, humility, patience).
  • Faith in self: Master key; turns reason into sense; obedience to good thoughts = liberty.
