Part 4: will culture, memory, and personal magnetism

Volition and the education of the will

L.W. defines volition as the self-realization of an idea with which the self is identified. He rejects the notion that volition is merely attention, arguing instead that attention is merely an attendant to the "cognitive spirit"—the striving, driving element within that refuses to be beaten.

L.W. classifies the will into ten distinct types: * The impulsive type: Acts on the spur of the moment, dominated by instincts. * The imperative type: Dominated by assertiveness, with clearly marked ends. * The confident type: Believes in its ability to succeed but often fails by neglecting necessary means. * The contrary type: Characterized by "pure cussedness" and acting contrary to advice. * The automatic type: The creature of habit and routine. * The unconscious type: Guided by subliminal, hidden genius. * The fatalistic type: Believes success or failure is entirely predetermined. * The indecisive type: Wavers constantly between competing ideas. * The thoughtful type: Subjects all decisions to prolonged reason. * The ideal type: Understands and utilizes impulses, confidence, and thoughtful review to guide action.

L.W. urges students to transition into the "ideal type" to minimize pain and maximize perseverance. He advises controlling negative impulses by immediately dismissing them and simulating desired emotional states through physical posture and facial expressions.

The mechanics of memory and retention

L.W. asserts that native retentiveness is congenital and cannot be improved by any training system; memory training can only teach students to use their natural capacity to its limit. He dismisses the "pigeonhole" theory of memory, explaining that ideas do not exist permanently in the brain but leave "residual dispositions" along neural pathways that can be reactivated through stimulation.

To optimize memory, L.W. recommends: * The scoring method: Learning facts or vocabulary in pairs, accenting the second word, and using skeletonized notes to prompt recall. * Distributed repetition: Spreading repetitions over a longer period (e.g., two repetitions over twelve days) rather than cramming them into a short window. * Understanding imagery types: Identifying whether one is a visual, auditory, or motor learner, and tailoring study habits accordingly. * The art of forgetting: Purposely disregarding non-essentials to keep the mind unencumbered, and actively starving unwanted, emotionally painful memories of attention and repetition.

Personal magnetism and the human aura

L.W. describes personal magnetism as a silent, invisible soul force. He references the work of Dr. Walter John Kilner, who claimed to make the human "aura" visible using chemical screens. L.W. argues that this etheric envelope radiates different colors reflecting an individual's character.

According to L.W., personal magnetism is generated and diffused through unselfish living, sympathy, and a spirit of service. He advises against imposing one's will on others, warning that moral dishonesty or "inharmony" disrupts the vibratory system and weakens one's personal power.

Part 5: oratory, public speaking, and moral education

The art of public speaking and composition

L.W. distinguishes between public speaking and true eloquence, noting that modern speaking has shifted from emotional grandiloquence to an intellectual, unemotional appeal to reason. He provides practical guidelines for delivering a set speech: * Thorough preparation: Immerse oneself in the subject, write the speech out fully, and condense it into bold, clear, progressive notes. * Natural delivery: Begin quietly, speak slowly, and utilize the power of the pause to let points sink in. * Intensity over loudness: Avoid shouting; instead, project quiet intensity and sincerity, which L.W. asserts is the only real bond of sympathy between speaker and audience. * Handling interruptions: Never argue with an interrupter; either ignore them or deliver a good-tempered, nimble-witted retort.

After-dinner speaking and lecturing

L.W. characterizes after-dinner speaking as a highly difficult art requiring brevity, wit, and the appearance of spontaneity. He notes a cultural difference: in England, after-dinner speakers are expected to have prepared thoroughly months in advance, whereas Americans value entertainment value and a winning platform personality over distinguished credentials.

L.W. argues that the true object of lecturing is not to impart dry information, but to plant germinal ideas, arouse curiosity, and rescue the audience from "muddled thinking."

Rational moral education and mental efficiency

L.W. views moral education through a determinist lens, stating that its goal is to present moral ideas so clearly that they automatically determine the individual's future conduct. He argues that "moral clairvoyance" allows a person to willingly become a "slave to the moral idea," which Epicurus and Schiller equated with true liberty.

Turning to mental hygiene, L.W. identifies "psychasthenia" (mental debility) as the true root of neurasthenia. He asserts that people do not learn how to think, and that schools burden the memory with useless knowledge rather than developing practical judgment. He estimates that one in 500 people in the United States is a "mental wreck" due to unchecked bad mental habits like worry, jealousy, and suspiciousness.

L.W. maintains that fear and expectancy make the body susceptible to contagious diseases, whereas a confident, fearless mind establishes a defensive barrier that renders an individual virtually immune.

Prenatal culture and maternal concentration

To support this claim, he points to the biblical account of Jacob using pilled rods to influence the visual environment of conceiving sheep and goats, causing them to bring forth speckled offspring.
