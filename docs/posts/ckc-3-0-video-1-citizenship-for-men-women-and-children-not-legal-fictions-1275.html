Dred Scott v. sandford and the distinction between people and persons

Williams analyzes Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857), focusing on Chief Justice Roger B. Taney's majority opinion. Williams argues that the decision established a sharp legal distinction between two terms in constitutional text:

  • We the people: Williams argues this term referred exclusively to the sovereign political body composed of state citizens who created the Constitution and held governing authority.
  • Persons: Williams notes that the term "persons" was used in clauses such as Article I, Section 9 (the slave trade clause) to refer to non-citizen populations, specifically enslaved African populations without constitutional rights.

Williams asserts that while the core holding barring Black Americans from citizenship was later overturned, Taney's analysis of state citizenship as membership in state sovereignty remains historically significant to understanding early constitutional structure.

Post-Civil War legislation and the creation of federal citizenship

Williams traces the legal shifts following the Civil War, detailing the dynamic between state Black Codes and congressional reconstruction efforts:

  1. The Thirteenth Amendment (1865): Abolished slavery, prompting Southern states to enact Black Codes restricting the rights of freedmen.
  2. The Civil Rights Act of 1866: Passed by Congress to override the Black Codes, declaring all persons born in the United States to be citizens of the United States. President Andrew Johnson vetoed the bill, arguing Congress lacked authority to confer state citizenship, but Congress overrode the veto.
  3. The Fourteenth Amendment (1868): Enacted to cement federal citizenship into the Constitution, establishing that all persons born or naturalized in the U.S. and subject to its jurisdiction are citizens of the United States and of the state wherein they reside.

Williams references congressional debates involving Thaddeus Stevens and a 1967 Congressional Record entry discussing ratification disputes to argue that Fourteenth Amendment citizenship created a new federal statutory class distinct from pre-existing state citizenship.

The slaughter-house cases and dual citizenship jurisprudence

Williams examines the Slaughter-House Cases (1873), citing Justice Samuel Freeman Miller's landmark majority opinion establishing the legal doctrine of dual citizenship. Williams highlights several core holdings:

  • Distinct citizenships: The ruling affirmed that U.S. federal citizenship and state citizenship are distinct statuses with separate rights and characteristics.
  • Privileges and immunities: The Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause protects only rights tied directly to national/federal citizenship, rather than the fundamental civil rights derived from state citizenship.
  • Scope of protection: Miller noted that the Fourteenth Amendment was primarily designed to secure the freedom and civil rights of newly emancipated slaves against state discrimination.

Williams also references Bradwell v. Illinois (1873) and United States v. Cruikshank (1876) to argue that Fourteenth Amendment rights do not automatically incorporate all state-level rights or constitutional guarantees.

United States v. anthony and Fourteenth Amendment scope

Williams highlights United States v. Anthony (1873), a federal case involving women's suffrage advocate Susan B. Anthony presided over by Supreme Court Justice Ward Hunt. Williams emphasizes Hunt's ruling that:

  • The Fourteenth Amendment created and defined federal U.S. citizenship.
  • The rights of state citizens remained separate and governed by pre-existing constitutional provisions and state law.
  • The protection of privileges and immunities under the Fourteenth Amendment applied strictly to federal rights, not state political rights such as voting qualifications.

Williams argues this line of jurisprudence demonstrates that state citizenship relying on Ninth Amendment unenumerated rights operates independently of Fourteenth Amendment federal citizenship.

Licensing, incorporation, and the introduction of corporate personhood

Williams discusses how legal rights were selectively extended to federal citizens and expanded to legal fictions during the late 19th century:

  • The Incorporation Doctrine: Williams explains that federal citizens draw rights from specific constitutional amendments incorporated by courts or statutes enacted by Congress, while arguing that state citizens rely directly on the Ninth Amendment's reservation of unenumerated rights.
  • Regulatory licensing: Williams claims that because federal citizens lack Ninth Amendment protections, activities like driving, fishing, or practicing law require government licenses, whereas he asserts state citizens hold inherent rights under the Ninth Amendment.
  • Corporate personhood: Williams cites Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad Co. (1886), where Chief Justice Morrison Waite affirmed that corporations are "persons" under the Fourteenth Amendment, expanding federal citizenship protections to artificial entities.
