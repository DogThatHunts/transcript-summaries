Code of conduct for United States judges

Brandon introduces the Code of Conduct for United States Judges (specifically federal canons, though state equivalents exist).

  • Nature of the canons: He emphasizes that these are ethical guidelines rather than strict, binding statutory laws.
  • Practical application: While litigants cannot directly enforce these rules against a judge in court, Brandon suggests they can be gently referenced if a judge's behavior becomes exceptionally egregious, or used when drafting formal reports to advisory committees.
  • Key canons: He highlights Canon 1 (upholding the integrity and independence of the judiciary) and the mandate to avoid impropriety and the appearance of impropriety.

Local rules of the California Superior Court

The discussion shifts to local court rules, using the California Superior Court and Los Angeles County Local Rules as primary examples.

  • Court structure and operations: These rules govern internal operations, including the roles of presiding judges, case assignments, electronic filing, and sanctions.
  • Trial conduct: Brandon notes that local rules dictate granular courtroom behaviors, such as how to address the judge, standing to object, marking exhibits, handling bulky evidence, and approaching witnesses.
  • Division-specific rules: Litigants must look at the specific division rules—such as the family or civil division—depending on their case type.

Appellate division and court of appeals rules

Brandon contrasts the rules for limited and unlimited civil appeals in California.

  • Limited vs. unlimited cases: Limited civil cases, misdemeanors, and traffic infractions are handled by the internal appellate division of the Superior Court. Unlimited cases go to the external Court of Appeals.
  • The power of a panel: Brandon highlights that appeals are decided by a three-judge panel rather than a single judge, requiring a concurrence of at least two judges. He notes this panel structure is one of the main reasons he is always willing to appeal.
  • Navigating the process with AI: He advises using AI tools to quickly understand procedural steps, e-filing requirements, timelines, and the structure of appellate briefs (opening, answering, and optional reply briefs). He also mentions specialized writs like mandate (mandamus), certiorari (review), and prohibition.

Reflections on modern legal complexity

Brandon shares a candid reflection on his personal struggles with complex procedural rules, contrasting them with historical legal practices.

  • Procedural burnout: He admits that his co-leader, Joey, loves procedural details, whereas they drive Brandon crazy. He attributes many of his past legal losses to ignoring these technicalities, which eventually forced him to learn them.
  • Nostalgia for 1800s jurisprudence: Brandon expresses a preference for 19th-century case law, which focused heavily on the substantive merits of a case, local community needs, and witness testimony, rather than the dense web of modern procedural rules.
