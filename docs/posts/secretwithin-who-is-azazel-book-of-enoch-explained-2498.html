Who is Azazel? Book of Enoch explained

Overview

Azazel's identity shifts dramatically across Jewish, Christian, and Islamic texts. In the Hebrew Bible, he appears as an obscure recipient of the scapegoat on Yom Kippur; in apocryphal literature, he becomes a principal architect of humanity's corruption. SecretWithin traces these portrayals through canonical and non-canonical sources, revealing how a single name accumulated layers of theological meaning and moral weight across centuries.

Azazel in Leviticus: the scapegoat and the wilderness

In Leviticus 16, Azazel appears three times in the Day of Atonement ritual. God instructs Aaron to cast lots over two male goats: one is sacrificed to the Lord, the other designated for Azazel. Aaron confesses the sins of Israel over the second goat's head, then releases it into the wilderness carrying those sins.

SecretWithin notes that this pairing—Lord versus Azazel—suggests Azazel is "the polar opposite of the biblical God, possibly even an evil entity." Some rabbinical interpretations propose that the name itself encodes meaning: Azaz (strong, rugged) and El (God) may refer to the jagged cliff from which the scapegoat was pushed. The wilderness location implies Azazel dwells outside civilization, a repository for human transgression.

The Book of Enoch: Azazel as corruptor and teacher of war

Azazel's role expands dramatically in the Book of Enoch, an apocryphal text detailing the fall of the Watchers before the Great Flood. While absent from the initial list of rebellious angels in Chapter 6, Azazel emerges as uniquely culpable in Chapter 8.

The Watchers' transgression: The angels, led by Samyaza, lust after human women and swear an oath to take them as wives. Samyaza warns that God will not approve and he alone will bear the penalty, but the others convince him to join. These unions produce the Nephilim—violent, chaotic giants who devour humanity and eventually consume one another.

Azazel's specific sin: While other Watchers teach astronomy, herbalism, and cosmetics, Azazel is credited with introducing metalwork and weaponry. SecretWithin emphasizes: "Azazel is uniquely noted for showing mankind how to wage war." This distinction—teaching the instruments of destruction rather than knowledge—marks him as exceptionally transgressive.

Divine judgment: In Chapter 9, the archangels Michael, Uriel, Raphael, and Gabriel witness the devastation and accuse Azazel before God, stating: "Thou seest what Azazel hath done, who hath taught all unrighteousness on earth, and revealed the eternal secrets which were preserved in heaven." They also rebuke Samyaza for failing to restrain the Watchers.

God's response is severe. Raphael is commanded to bind Azazel hand and foot, cast him into darkness in the desert of Dudail, cover him with jagged rocks, and ensure he remains imprisoned until the final judgment, when he will be cast into fire. Gabriel is tasked with inciting the giants to destroy one another, while Michael binds Samyaza and the other Watchers for 70 generations.

SecretWithin observes that Azazel bears "the brunt of divine wrath" because he uniquely divulged forbidden knowledge—specifically, the secrets of warfare. In Chapter 13, Enoch, now a scribe, reprimands the Watchers, cementing Azazel's role as the principal corruptor.

Third Book of Enoch: Azazel in heaven

The Third Book of Enoch (3 Enoch) presents a paradox. Despite his binding in the first book, Azazel appears in heaven during Rabbi Ishmael's ascent. When Enoch (now transformed into the angel Metatron) arrives, Azazel challenges his authority. God intervenes, declaring: "I delight in this one more than all of you, and hence he shall be the prince and a ruler over you in the high heavens." This divine favor renders Azazel submissive, and the other celestial beings prostrate themselves before Metatron.

SecretWithin notes the tension: Azazel has been judged and bound, yet he still occupies heaven and must acknowledge a mortal's elevation above him.

Apocalypse of Abraham: Azazel as tempter and adversary

The Apocalypse of Abraham, a first-century extra-canonical Jewish text, offers additional narratives. In one episode, an unclean bird disrupts Abraham's sacrifice. The angel identifies it as Azazel and condemns him: "This is ungodliness, this is Azazel." The angel declares that Abraham's lot is in heaven while Azazel's is on earth, and that Azazel has chosen his dwelling place in uncleanness.

In a later vision (Chapters 23–32), Abraham witnesses the fall of Adam and Eve. Behind the tree stands a serpent-like being with human hands and feet, and six pairs of wings—Azazel. God reveals: "He who stands between them represents ungodliness and their journey to perdition, Azazel himself." This casts Azazel as the serpent-tempter, a figure central to humanity's original sin.

The Messiah and betrayal: In Chapters 28–32, Abraham sees a figure (the Messiah, though unnamed) who will be worshipped and will combat God's enemies. Azazel approaches, kisses this figure on the face, and stands behind him. SecretWithin draws a parallel to Luke 22:48, where Judas betrays Jesus with a kiss, suggesting the Apocalypse of Abraham may encode the Passion narrative with Azazel representing the betrayer.

In the text's conclusion, God warns Abraham that the wicked will "beautify in the belly of the crafty worm, Azazel, and be burned by the fire of Azazel's tongue." This imagery establishes Azazel as an instrument of final judgment and eternal punishment.

Islamic interpretations: Azazel as Iblis or a hybrid being

Azazel does not appear explicitly in the Quran, but Islamic scholars have proposed various identifications. SecretWithin surveys several theories:

Azazel as the devil's pre-fall name: Many interpreters believe Azazel (or Azazil) was the devil's name before expulsion from heaven. Some scholars suggest he was an archangel; others propose he was a jinn (an evil spirit or demon in Islamic theology). The Tafsir and other Islamic sources hint at a hybrid nature—part jinn, part angel.

The refusal to bow to Adam: A prominent Islamic narrative recounts that Azazel, originally favored by God, refused to bow to Adam (whom God had created from earth), claiming superiority because he was made from fire. This defiance led to his expulsion and eventual return to flames. This mirrors the Quranic account of Iblis, though Iblis is not always identified with Azazel.

The Brethren of Purity: An 8th-century Iraqi philosophical group proposed that Azazel was a jinn captured and elevated by angels for his moral integrity. When commanded to bow to Adam, he refused and was cast out—a narrative that blends angelic and demonic elements.

Al-Halaj's interpretation: The 9th-century Persian poet Al-Halaj, in his Tawassin, theorized that Azazel was called Iblis only after his fall. Before that, he had dual missions: preaching good works to angels and warning humans about evil deeds by jinn. Al-Halaj suggests Azazel's corruption of humanity was not malicious but negligent—he told humans about bad actions without providing the contrast of good actions. He also analyzed the name's phonetics and recorded Azazel's argument with God over his refusal to bow to an earthly creature.

Synthesis and contested claims

SecretWithin acknowledges that many accounts of Azazel derive from non-canonical texts and modern interpretations lacking substantial religious backing. The figure evolves from a wilderness receptacle for sin (Leviticus) to a fallen angel and teacher of warfare (Enoch) to a tempter and adversary of the Messiah (Apocalypse of Abraham) to a pre-fall devil or hybrid jinn-angel (Islamic sources).

Across these traditions, Azazel can be likened to Satan or understood as another name for Satan—an entity that opposes God and seeks to corrupt humanity. Yet the specifics vary: in some texts he is bound and imprisoned; in others he remains active in heaven or on earth. The Apocalypse of Abraham suggests God and Azazel are cosmic rivals, with Azazel ultimately serving as an instrument of divine judgment against those who follow him.

SecretWithin notes that while these narratives are theologically rich, many modern interpretations lack scholarly consensus or substantial religious grounding, and invites viewers to contribute overlooked or alternative accounts.
