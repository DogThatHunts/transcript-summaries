Why Bulgaria Is Sitting on the Most Unexplained Land in Human History

Bulgaria hides mysteries that stretch from ancient gold to vanished early humans, with answers that keep disappearing as we dig deeper.

The first gold on earth

In 1972, an excavator driver near Varna discovered a grave containing gold objects, marking one of the oldest gold treasures ever found. The site, the Varna Necropolis, holds 294 graves dating to 4,600 to 4,200 years before Christ, with around 3,000 gold objects and 6 kilograms of gold. The most striking find is grave 43, where a single individual was buried with 990 gold objects and a golden scepter—more gold than any other site from that era. This suggests a person of immense power in a pre-kingdom society.

What is stranger is that 30% of the cemetery remains unexcavated. The graves with no skeletons—filled with gifts but no bodies—are the richest of all, with masks made of unbaked clay. Their meaning remains unknown.

The origin of this wealth is tied to salt. Around 40 kilometers inland, archaeologists found salt springs and trade routes, suggesting that salt was the currency of this early society.

The first town in Europe

Near Provadia, the settlement of Solnitsata dates to between 4700 and 4200 years before Christ. It is believed to be the oldest town in Europe, with two-story houses and a stone wall three meters high and two meters thick. The wall is among the earliest and most massive fortifications in prehistoric Europe.

Salt was the economic backbone of this community. It was traded with neighboring groups, and in that context, salt was more than seasoning—it was wealth. This trade network may have fueled the rise of the gold-rich culture in the Varna Necropolis.

Yet, the town’s history ends abruptly. Between 4200 and 3900 years before Christ, over 600 settlements in eastern Bulgaria and the lower Danube region were abandoned. The reasons remain enigmatic—climate change, exhausted land, war, or steppe migration—yet no written record explains the collapse.

The first Europeans

In 2015, a team re-excavated Bacho Kiro Cave near Drianovo and found human remains dated to between 42,500 and 46,000 years ago. These were Homo sapiens, modern humans, among the oldest known in Europe.

Genetic analysis revealed two surprising facts: - The people had a Neanderthal ancestor five to seven generations back—making them one of the closest known human encounters between Homo sapiens and Neanderthals. - Their genetic legacy did not survive in Europe. Instead, it persists today in people living in East Asia and the Americas.

This suggests that the first Europeans in the region did not contribute to the genetic makeup of later European populations. They lived in the cave, made tools, wore pendants carved from cave bear teeth, and intermarried with Neanderthals—before vanishing from the continent.

The mysteries that keep vanishing

Bulgaria’s mysteries are not just unexplained—they vanish as we uncover them.

  • The forest of stone columns at Pobiti Kamani, near Varna, stand in sand with no foundation. Scientists propose theories involving methane gas and microbial cementation, but no consensus exists. Locals believe giants hammered them into place.
  • The Devil’s Throat in Trigrad Gorge is a river that flows into a cave, drops 42 meters, and then vanishes. Dye takes over an hour to travel the stretch, and objects never reappear. Two cave divers vanished in 1970, and the cave is known in ancient times as a portal to the underworld.
  • The Madara Horseman, carved into a cliff 23 meters above ground, dates to around the 700s. It is the only rock relief of its kind in Europe. Historians debate whether it represents a Bulgar Khan or a Thracian or Iranian deity. The details of the carving remain contested.
  • In Sozopol, graves contain men with iron objects driven through their chests—deliberate post-mortem acts to prevent them from rising as vampires. These were not outcasts but aristocrats and clergymen, buried near churches. The practice continued into the early 20th century.
  • In the village of Bulgari, a ritual called the 'nestinari' involves dancers walking into a fire every June. The tradition blends Christian and pagan roots and is recognized by UNESCO as intangible heritage. The dancers claim to be channels of the saints’ will.

The pattern of disappearing histories

Bulgaria is not unexplained because it has been overlooked.

It is unexplained because every time we dig, the story cuts off before its end.

The first gold, with a third of the cemeteries unopened.

The first town, abandoned.

The first Europeans, gone from the continent they found.

Europe begins here, and Bulgaria keeps the endings to itself.

The ancestors of most Europeans today had to come from somewhere—somewhere even stranger than this.
