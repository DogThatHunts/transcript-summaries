EurodollarU | The Diesel Crisis Nobody Is Talking About

While stable crude prices mask the crisis, a severe bottleneck in global refining capacity has sent diesel soaring, triggering a quiet wave of consumer demand destruction.

The downstream shift: why crude oil prices lie

According to Jeff Snyder of EurodollarU, the global energy crisis is no longer primarily about crude oil. Instead, the critical bottleneck has shifted downstream to refining capacity—the physical infrastructure required to convert raw crude into finished products like diesel, gasoline, jet fuel, and heating oil. This explains why crude oil prices remain relatively tame while diesel prices hover near record highs. The world has sufficient crude, but refineries cannot process it fast enough to meet demand. If the conversion system is maxed out, additional crude has nowhere to go, leaving raw oil relatively cheap while finished diesel becomes extraordinarily scarce.

Understanding the crack spread and physical bottlenecks

The profitability of refining is measured by the "crack spread," such as the standard 3-2-1 benchmark (which estimates the margin of turning three barrels of crude into two barrels of gasoline and one barrel of distillate). For diesel, this spread has exploded past $100 per barrel. While this historic margin offers an unprecedented economic incentive to produce more fuel, refiners cannot respond.

US refineries are already operating at 98% capacity—effectively full physical tilt. Refineries require maintenance, experience unexpected shutdowns, and cannot run at 100% indefinitely. Consequently, the price signal remains trapped downstream in refined product margins rather than bidding up the price of crude.

Declining fuel demand as a symptom of economic strain

Despite tight supplies, US gasoline demand during the peak summer driving season has declined for two consecutive years, averaging 8.9 million barrels per day in August (down from 9.05 million in 2025 and 9.12 million in 2024). Snyder argues this decline is not merely a result of fuel efficiency, but rather a reflection of price sensitivity and income constraints. With retail gasoline averaging $4 per gallon and diesel above $5.70, consumers are actively consolidating trips, shortening vacations, and cutting back on discretionary spending.

The "no-hire" labor market and consumer income constraints

Snyder connects falling fuel consumption directly to a weakening US labor market. Recent JOLTS data shows the monthly hiring rate falling below 5.1 million, with net labor market turnover remaining negative for three consecutive months. Furthermore, voluntary quits have declined to 3.06 million, signaling that workers lack the confidence to leave their current jobs.

In this "no-hire" economy, companies are keeping job listings open but failing to convert them into actual payrolls. This lack of job mobility and weak income growth leaves households unable to absorb higher energy costs, forcing them to reduce consumption.

A historic supply deficit ahead of peak seasonal demand

The global refined product market is missing an estimated 2 to 3 million barrels per day of supply. The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates lost refinery throughput at 4.7 million barrels per day compared to last year, driven by Middle Eastern disruptions, Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian refineries, Russian export suspensions, and Chinese export restrictions.

To fill this gap, the US has exported massive volumes of diesel, drawing domestic distillate inventories down to approximately 103 million barrels. This represents the lowest late-August inventory level in the historical EIA record dating back to the early 1980s. This deficit is colliding with the peak autumn and winter demand season, characterized by agricultural harvesting in the Northern hemisphere, planting in the Southern hemisphere, and winter heating needs.

The demand destruction loop: why this energy shock is deflationary

While standard economic theory suggests that rising diesel costs should trigger an inflationary spiral as businesses pass costs to consumers, Snyder argues the opposite is occurring. Because consumers are financially constrained, businesses lack pricing power.

Major retailers like Walmart and Kroger are aggressively cutting prices and increasing promotions to protect market share. When businesses absorb higher energy costs rather than passing them on, they face squeezed margins. To compensate, they reduce employee hours, slow down hiring, or implement layoffs. This feedback loop—where high energy costs lead to lower retail margins, weaker hiring, and further drops in consumer spending—ultimately transforms an energy shock into economic disinflation and demand destruction rather than persistent inflation.
