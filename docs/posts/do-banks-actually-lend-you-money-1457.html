Do Banks actually lend you money?

Economist Richard Werner breaks down the legal reality of banking, explaining how commercial institutions create money out of thin air rather than lending existing funds.

Economist Richard Werner challenges the standard view of commercial banks as financial intermediaries.

He argues that banks neither take custody of customer deposits nor lend out pre-existing capital to borrowers.

Instead, banking institutions expand the money supply by legally purchasing borrowers' promissory notes and recording corresponding liabilities as new customer deposits.

Key takeaways

  • Banks are not intermediaries: Commercial banks do not collect deposits to lend them out; they actively create the money supply through credit creation.
  • Deposits are unsecured loans to banks: Legally, a bank deposit is not held in custody or bailment, but represents an unsecured loan from the depositor to the bank.
  • Lending is a security purchase: When a borrower signs a loan contract, they issue a promissory note that the bank purchases rather than transferring existing funds.
  • Money creation via accounting: Roughly 97% of the money supply consists of bank deposits, which are generated when banks reclassify accounts payable liabilities from loan contracts as customer deposits.
