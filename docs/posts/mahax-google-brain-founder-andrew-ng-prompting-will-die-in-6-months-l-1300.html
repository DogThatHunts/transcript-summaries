The shifting bottlenecks of software development

Andrew Ng explains that the rapid evolution of AI building blocks—such as LLMs, agentic workflows, voice AI, and deep learning—allows developers to build incredibly powerful software faster than ever before. However, this speed has created a massive product management bottleneck.

Traditionally, tech companies maintained an engineer-to-product-manager (PM) ratio of 4:1 or 8:1, meaning one PM could write enough specifications to keep multiple engineers busy. Because AI has dramatically accelerated engineering while PM work remains largely un-automated, Ng observes these ratios trending down to 2:1 or even 1:1.

To maximize velocity, Ng advocates for collapsing these roles into a single 'engineer-PM' hybrid. Engineers who learn to talk directly to users, gather feedback, and develop deep empathy can iterate and build products at an unprecedented pace. While Ng acknowledges that not all engineers enjoy this human-centric work, those who embrace it represent some of the fastest-moving talent in Silicon Valley today.

Navigating the post-hype job market

Lawrence Moroney provides a reality check on the AI job market, tracing its trajectory from the pandemic-era hiring freeze to the massive 2022–2023 over-hiring boom, and finally to the 'great wake-up' of 2024–2025. Today, companies are highly cautious, optimizing for the bottom line and seeking genuine, deliverable skills rather than speculative AI talent.

To succeed in this competitive environment, Moroney outlines three pillars of success: * Understanding in depth: Developing a rigorous academic grasp of machine learning architectures and the ability to distinguish genuine technical signals from social media noise. * Business focus: Aligning personal output with the strategic goals of the business. Moroney advises, 'Don't let your output be for the job you have, let your output be for the job you want.' * Bias towards delivery: Prioritizing execution over cheap ideas.

Moroney also warns candidates against taking generic interview advice too literally. He shares a story of a highly skilled engineer who repeatedly failed interviews because he interpreted the advice to 'stand his ground' as a license to be hostile and uncooperative. Once coached to show collaborative teamwork, the candidate quickly secured a high-paying role.

The technical debt of 'vibe coding'

As tools make it easier to prompt code into existence, Moroney introduces a financial framework to evaluate 'vibe coding': technical debt. Just as taking on a mortgage can be 'good debt' if the asset appreciates, taking on technical debt to build a rapid prototype is acceptable if it delivers clear business value. However, 'bad debt'—such as spaghetti code, undocumented systems, or solutions looking for a problem—can ruin a project.

Moroney notes that AI code generators often introduce subtle bugs or hallucinate APIs (such as generating iOS code for a macOS application due to training set imbalances). To mitigate this, engineers must act as trusted advisors within their organizations. They must ensure that generated code remains understandable, well-documented, and strictly aligned with clear business requirements, rather than letting non-technical leaders check unverified, prompted code directly into production.

The future of AI: big vs. small and edge compute

Moroney predicts a major bifurcation in the AI industry over the next five years: * Big AI: Large, centralized, hosted models (like Gemini, Claude, and GPT) that will continue to grow in size as companies chase Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). * Small AI: Highly optimized, self-hostable, open-weights models (typically around 7B parameters) that run locally.

For many industries—such as Hollywood studios, law firms, and medical offices—sharing proprietary intellectual property or sensitive user data with third-party hosted models is a non-starter. Consequently, Moroney sees a massive, underserved market for developers skilled in fine-tuning small models for highly specific downstream tasks.

Furthermore, Moroney highlights a shift in hardware execution. While AI has historically been synonymous with GPUs, new technologies like Scalable Matrix Extensions (SME) allow low-power CPUs to execute AI workloads directly on-device. This shift, pioneered by Apple and Chinese mobile vendors like Vivo and Oppo, enables private, low-latency, and cost-effective edge AI processing (such as Alipay's local, on-device photo search).

Responsible AI and the evolution of safety

Moroney argues that responsible AI has matured from fluffy, abstract social concepts into concrete business risk mitigation. He illustrates this with early safety filter failures in Google's Gemini image generator. In an attempt to naively correct for training set biases, Google implemented filters that intercepted user prompts to inject diversity. This led to absurd over-corrections, such as the model refusing to generate images of 'Caucasian' people or drawing every Irish woman with red hair.

These naive solutions damaged the model's reputation and commercial viability. Moroney emphasizes that true responsible AI requires rigorous engineering, a deep understanding of edge cases, and the humility to learn from mistakes.
