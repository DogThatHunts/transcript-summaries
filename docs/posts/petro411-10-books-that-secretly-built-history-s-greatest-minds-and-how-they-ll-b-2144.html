Petro411 | 10 Books That Secretly Built History's Greatest Minds (and how they'll build yours)

An educator and scholar explores ten foundational texts that shaped the strategic, logical, and moral frameworks of history’s most influential leaders and thinkers.

In this lecture, scholar and educator Petro411 highlights ten foundational books that have repeatedly shaped the minds of history's most influential leaders, scientists, and philosophers. Rather than focusing on abstract theory, Petro411 examines the specific intellectual capacities each text builds and traces their historical lineage through the figures who read them.

Plutarch's Lives: Character modeling

Written around 100 AD, Plutarch's Lives has shaped more military and civil leaders than almost any formal academy. Napoleon Bonaparte referred to it as his manual of rule, George Washington modeled his public persona on its heroes, and Alexander Hamilton and James Madison drew heavily from it when writing the Federalist Papers. William Shakespeare also relied on Plutarch as a direct source for plays like Julius Caesar, lifting prose almost verbatim.

  • Capacity built: Character modeling. Plutarch teaches through lived examples and biographies rather than abstract principles, training readers to construct an internal standard of excellence to measure themselves against under pressure.

Euclid's Elements: first-principles reasoning

Though a geometry textbook written around 300 BC, Elements profoundly influenced non-mathematical minds. Abraham Lincoln carried a copy in his saddlebag as a traveling lawyer to master the concept of logical demonstration. Albert Einstein referred to it as his "holy little geometry book," and thinkers like Isaac Newton, Thomas Hobbes, Baruch Spinoza, Nicolaus Copernicus, and Galileo Galilei credited it with changing how they structured their thoughts.

  • Capacity built: Logical demonstration. It trains the mind to understand that a claim is not true knowledge until it has been proven step-by-step from first principles.

Lucretius's de rerum natura: Scientific thinking

Written around 60 BC, On the Nature of Things nearly disappeared during the Middle Ages until humanist Poggio Bracciolini rediscovered a manuscript in 1417. This single poem was hand-copied by Niccolò Machiavelli, heavily annotated by Michel de Montaigne, and studied by Thomas Jefferson, Isaac Newton, Galileo, and Charles Darwin. Petro411 notes that literary scholar Stephen Greenblatt argues this text's rediscovery helped ignite the scientific revolution and shape the American founding.

  • Capacity built: Scientific realism. It trains the mind to explain the physical world through natural causes, empirical evidence, and systematic method.

Boethius's consolation of philosophy: equanimity under adversity

Written in 524 AD while the author awaited execution for treason, this philosophical dialogue was the most widely read book in Europe after the Bible for a thousand years. It was translated by Alfred the Great, Geoffrey Chaucer, and Queen Elizabeth I, and read deeply by Dante Alighieri, Thomas Aquinas, and Thomas More while he was imprisoned in the Tower of London.

  • Capacity built: Mental equanimity. It teaches readers to separate their core identity from external fortune, maintaining internal peace when everything else is stripped away.

Montaigne's essays: Intellectual honesty

Published in 1580, Michel de Montaigne's self-reflective essays had a massive secular influence. William Shakespeare, René Descartes, Friedrich Nietzsche, Ralph Waldo Emerson, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Virginia Woolf, and Sigmund Freud all cited it as a direct influence. Blaise Pascal read it obsessively, even while arguing against its conclusions.

  • Capacity built: Intellectual honesty. By focusing on his own contradictions and uncertainties, Montaigne teaches the discipline of examining one's own mind and following a line of reasoning wherever it leads, rather than where one wishes it to go.

Xenophon's cyropedia: character-based leadership

Written around 370 BC, The Education of Cyrus served as a foundational leadership manual for Alexander the Great, Scipio Africanus, Julius Caesar, and Cicero. Niccolò Machiavelli's The Prince was partly written as a response to it. Thomas Jefferson owned two copies, Benjamin Franklin studied it, and modern management pioneer Peter Drucker considered it the finest book on leadership ever written.

  • Capacity built: Ethical leadership. It demonstrates that true leadership is a matter of personal character rather than mere technique, and that a leader's true measure is how they treat others when it costs them something.

Epictetus's enchiridion: the dichotomy of control

This Stoic handbook was the foundation of Marcus Aurelius's philosophy. It was carried on campaigns by Frederick the Great and Theodore Roosevelt, and carved into Montaigne's study. Most famously, Admiral James Stockdale credited his survival during seven and a half years of torture as a POW in Vietnam to having memorized and recited Epictetus's arguments in his cell.

  • Capacity built: The dichotomy of control. It trains the mind to distinguish between what is genuinely within one's power and what is not, preventing the waste of mental energy on external factors.

The analects of confucius: self-governance and social order

Compiled in the 5th century BC, this text shaped East Asian civilization for two millennia. When translated into Latin in 1687, it deeply impacted Enlightenment thinkers. Voltaire used Confucian rationalism to argue against European religious intolerance, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz drew on it for his metaphysics, and Ralph Waldo Emerson referenced it throughout his essays.

  • Capacity built: Systemic moral order. It teaches that individual self-governance is the foundational building block for interpersonal relationships, which in turn shape institutions and hold civilizations together.

Thucydides's history of the peloponnesian war: Political realism

Written around 400 BC, this historical account was kept by Alexander the Great on campaigns, studied by Frederick the Great, and translated into English by Thomas Hobbes, who called it the most instructive book he had ever read. John Adams considered it essential reading for any serious statesman.

  • Capacity built: Political realism. Thucydides was the first Western historian to strip away mythological and moral justifications, training readers to observe power dynamics, self-interest, and geopolitical realities as they actually operate.

The arabian nights: Narrative imagination

Compiled between the 9th and 14th centuries, this collection of stories permanently altered how Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, Charles Dickens, Marcel Proust, and Jorge Luis Borges approached narrative structure. It was also deeply read by Franz Kafka, J.R.R. Tolkien, and Stendhal.

  • Capacity built: Narrative imagination. Through the framing device of Scheherazade—who stays alive by keeping a murderous king captivated by cliffhangers—the book teaches how to think in complex structures and leverage the survival power of storytelling.
