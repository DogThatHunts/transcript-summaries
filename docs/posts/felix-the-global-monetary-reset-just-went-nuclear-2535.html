The global monetary reset just went nuclear

Overview

Felix Prehn, an economist and financial educator, argues that three converging developments signal an imminent monetary reset comparable to 1971, when the dollar lost 93% of its purchasing power. Rather than explicit policy announcements, these signals are embedded in government actions and institutional behavior that most observers have missed. Prehn contends that while the wealthy and politically connected will weather this transition, ordinary savers face significant erosion of wealth unless they understand what is happening and act accordingly.

The three warning signs

The strategic petroleum reserve is being drained

The U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve has fallen to its lowest level since 1982—a 44-year low—after 26 consecutive weeks of withdrawals. Prehn argues this is counterintuitive: the U.S. produces more oil than any nation and exports the surplus, so why deplete the emergency tank?

His answer: the government is using the reserve as a price-suppression tool. Rising oil prices would trigger inflation in diesel and freight costs, which would cascade through the entire economy (food, building materials, everything transported by truck). By releasing reserves, the administration artificially holds down the oil price to keep the official inflation number "socially acceptable," possibly before an election. Prehn characterizes this as "borrowing calm from tomorrow to make today's inflation number look acceptable."

The Venezuelan oil refill announced 22 days prior is, in Prehn's view, a political gesture: Venezuelan crude is too heavy and sulfur-rich to meet reserve specifications, and the Venezuelan oil industry would need 10–20 years to reach production levels that matter. This is not a genuine solution.

The trap: Once the reserve empties, any real shock—a hurricane, Middle East escalation, or shipping disruption—will cause oil and diesel prices to spike with no buffer. The "relative calm is manufactured," and the emergency tank is nearly gone.

The Treasury is buying its own debt at accelerating rates

The U.S. Treasury announced it is doubling its debt buyback program to $4 billion per transaction, rebranded as "liquidity support" to obscure its true nature. Prehn explains the mechanics:

  1. The Federal Reserve prints new money.
  2. The Fed uses that money to buy short-term Treasury debt from the government.
  3. The government then uses the cash to buy its own longer-term debt (10, 20, 30-year bonds).
  4. This artificially lowers interest rates on government borrowing.

Prehn calls this "money printing" dressed up in technical language. The pattern mirrors Japan's approach over the past 20 years. Historically, when a government becomes the primary buyer of its own debt, it crosses a threshold that history treats unkindly.

During COVID, the Fed added 40% more money to the system in just a couple of years, triggering official inflation of 11% (which Prehn argues was understated by orders of magnitude). The same mechanism is now reactivating, and every dollar held or earned will be "watered down just a little bit more."

Massive private wealth concentration behind closed doors

Three private companies—SpaceX, Anthropic, and OpenAI—are now worth more than every U.S. company that went public in the last 45 years combined. This represents an enormous wealth creation event, but one locked behind a door most retail investors cannot access.

Meanwhile, in the public stock market, American households hold a record share of their net worth in equities (approximately 25%), higher than during the dot-com peak or 2008. This concentration is extreme: the five largest companies in the S&P 500 now represent 30% of the entire index. Most retail investors believe they are diversified through index funds, but they are actually overexposed to five technology companies that perform similar functions.

Prehn warns that this bubble will crack—not imminently, but eventually. The dot-com crash took 15 years to recover. When the correction comes, there will be no buyers left (everyone will be trying to sell simultaneously), triggering sharp drawdowns.

The pattern: Gains from money printing are being steered to insiders and the wealthy before they reach ordinary people. The "safe, sensible, boring" retirement portfolio is now more concentrated in tech than at any point in American history, including 1929 and the dot-com mania.

What the skilled money is actually doing

Prehn advocates following institutional and insider behavior rather than media narratives. He cites Donald Trump's recent trades (observable via SEC filings) as a case study:

Buying: Berkshire Hathaway (insurance), Visa and MasterCard (payment toll booths that benefit from inflation), Home Depot, Tractor Supply, Republic Services (waste management), and Palantir.

Selling: Netflix, Meta, and other crowded tech stocks.

The pattern is clear: boring, essential businesses that generate cash regardless of conditions, and "toll booth" companies that capture a percentage of every transaction (benefiting when prices rise). On the exit, the skilled money is liquidating the crowded tech positions that retail investors are chasing.

Central banks and the institutions printing money are buying gold at the fastest pace since 1997, signaling they understand what will happen to the currency. In the 1970s, gold appreciated eightfold during a similar inflationary cycle.

The 1971 parallel and the stakes

Prehn frames this moment as comparable to 1971, when the U.S. abandoned the gold standard. According to the U.S. government's own accounting, the 1971 dollar is now worth 7 cents in purchasing power—a 93% loss. Prehn believes the actual figure is worse.

He argues the same mechanism will repeat: governments will print money, inflation will erode savings, and those who understand the pattern will protect themselves while those who don't will suffer. The pain will not arrive immediately (12–36 months out), which is why most people will not notice until it is too late.

The broader argument

Prehn's core claim is that a monetary reset is underway, executed quietly through policy mechanisms rather than explicit announcements. The wealthy, politicians, and those with inside information will emerge unscathed or enriched. Ordinary savers will be punished for not understanding what is happening. He frames financial literacy and proactive planning as moral imperatives, not optional luxuries.
