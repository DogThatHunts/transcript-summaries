Jamie Dimon's warning vs. jpmorgan's actions

Jeff Snider of EurodollarU highlights a recurring pattern: JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon publicly warns of sticky inflation and rising interest rates, yet his own bank repeatedly increases its holdings of Treasury and agency securities during these exact periods. Dimon recently cautioned that fiscal deficits, tariffs, military spending, and supply chain restructuring could keep inflation elevated, potentially forcing the Federal Reserve to raise rates further. However, Snider argues that these highly visible narrative risks often obscure the underlying economic reality, and that Dimon's warnings historically coincide with major turning points where yields actually fall.

The technical reality of "September cubed"

Snider attributes the rise in Treasury yields during September to technical and seasonal factors rather than a fundamental macroeconomic shift. He terms this phenomenon "September cubed," which is driven by three overlapping effects:

  • Seasonal tendencies: Treasuries historically experience sell-offs from late August through September.
  • Funding bottlenecks: Mid-September corporate tax payments and dealer balance sheet constraints put pressure on the repo market, making Treasury financing more difficult.
  • Post-bottleneck acceleration: Since 2021, long-term yields have frequently jumped in the second half of September immediately following these funding pressures.

As October begins, these seasonal and technical pressures are fading, and buyers are returning to the bond market, suggesting that September's sell-off exaggerated the underlying economic signal.

Historical precedents: the 2018 rate scare

This is not the first time Dimon's public warnings have diverged from his bank's actions. In the summer of 2018, Dimon warned that investors should prepare for the 10-year Treasury yield to hit 4% or 5% due to an accelerating economy and rising inflation. While the 10-year yield briefly reached 3.25% in October 2018, the global economy soon encountered monetary tightening pressures ("Eurodollar number four"), causing growth expectations to collapse and yields to plummet.

Crucially, while Dimon was warning the public about rising rates, JPMorgan Chase increased its reported Treasury and agency holdings by approximately 153%—from $68 billion in March 2018 to over $172 billion by June 2019.

What the market is actually pricing today

Despite the prevailing narrative of "higher for longer" inflation, market indicators such as the TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) market and inflation swaps show no signature of a sustained inflationary breakout.

Furthermore, the front end of the Treasury curve remains restrained compared to previous aggressive hiking cycles. Snider notes that while the market may allow for one or two more minor rate hikes, it is not pricing in a massive sequence. This is because the broader economy shows signs of financial restraint: credit stress is building among leveraged and smaller borrowers, banks are tightening lending standards, and consumer demand remains fragile. These factors naturally slow economic activity and price increases, regardless of Federal Reserve policy.

Lessons from the bond market head fakes of 2002 and 2007

Snider points to historical cycles where interest rates spiked sharply right before major economic slowdowns:

  • The 2002 recovery bounce: At the end of the dot-com recession in late 2001, temporary inventory rebuilding created the appearance of economic momentum. The 10-year yield surged by 120 basis points to 5.44% by April 2002 on expectations of Fed rate hikes. However, because final demand remained weak, the move reversed entirely, and yields fell to 3.61% by October 2002 as the Fed cut rates by 50 basis points.
  • The June 2007 peak: The 10-year yield rose back to 5.25% in mid-2007, even as subprime mortgage concerns were mounting. This served as a final "head fake" before the global financial crisis forced yields to collapse and pushed the Fed to cut rates to zero.

These episodes demonstrate that yields can rise sharply during the early stages of a slowdown because market participants temporarily misinterpret short-term fluctuations as permanent economic booms.

The institutional shift toward bonds

While Dimon continues to warn of sticky inflation, other prominent institutional voices are shifting their stance toward bonds:

  • Jim Bianco: The veteran market analyst recently suggested that the bond market is close to a peak in rates, indicating that the balance of risk is changing after a massive repricing.
  • PIMCO: Under CIO Daniel Ivascyn, the firm has begun trimming its underweight position in long-dated government debt, arguing that fiscal challenges are now fully reflected in current valuations.
  • Man Group: Research from the firm indicates that when inflation is stable between 2% and 4%, 10-year Treasuries historically generate strong nominal returns. The real danger to bonds is rapidly accelerating inflation, which market indicators suggest is highly unlikely.

Ultimately, Snider concludes that while Dimon's inflation concerns represent highly visible "low-hanging fruit" for headlines, the defensive behavior of major banks and the broader bond market points toward a lower-growth, lower-rate future.
