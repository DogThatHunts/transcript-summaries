The split-citizenship framework

Williams' litigation strategy relies on dividing himself into two separate legal entities with different constitutional standings:

The Glendale utility dispute and negotiable instruments

The factual basis of the lawsuit stems from a dispute with Glendale Water and Power. Williams asserts that he submitted hand-drafted "accepted drafts" (negotiable instruments) to pay his utility bills under California Commercial Code (CCC) Section 3104 (or District of Columbia Code Section 28:3-104 in federal court).

Williams claims that the city ignored these instruments, failed to properly apply or dishonor them, and subsequently shut off his electricity for several hours. To restore power, Williams paid using a credit card, which he argues constitutes being forced to pay twice. He asserts that municipal utilities have a legal duty to accept any instrument meeting the statutory definition of a negotiable instrument and cannot arbitrarily choose which forms of payment to accept.

Statutory claims: Peonage, involuntary servitude, and civil rights

Williams outlines several federal statutory causes of action he has included in the template, which he claims are specifically available to his sole proprietorship entity:

  • Peonage and forced labor: Williams argues that by turning off his power to force compliance with their payment demands, the city placed his sole proprietorship in a state of peonage and forced labor. He cites 18 U.S.C. § 1595 as providing a private right of action for these claims.
  • Deprivation of rights under 42 U.S.C. § 1983: Williams argues that Section 1983 and the Civil Rights Act of 1866 were historically established to protect newly freed slaves (non-whites) and legal fictions, not white state citizens. Therefore, he applies this statutory claim strictly to his sole proprietorship plaintiff, alleging that the city violated the entity's Fourteenth Amendment rights.

The Ninth Amendment and "sovereignty"

For his personal capacity claims, Williams relies heavily on the Ninth Amendment, which he characterizes as the "sovereignty amendment." Citing the Dred Scott v. Sandford decision, Williams argues that state citizens are "members of the sovereignty" and possess collective kingship.

Under his interpretation of the Ninth Amendment, state citizens possess unenumerated rights to engage in common callings—such as driving, traveling, practicing law, medicine, or running a business—without being subject to state licensing requirements, provided they do not violate common law or harm others. He alleges the city violated his Ninth Amendment rights by disrupting his personal and commercial activities when they cut off his electricity.
