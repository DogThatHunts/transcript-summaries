Napoleon Hill, The Law of Success (1928) — A Digest of the Eight Books

The system in sixteen lessons, how the lessons build on each other, and the claims worth checking.

A cross-reading of all eight books of Napoleon Hill's The Law of Success (1928), as read by Dan John Miller for the Master Key Society. It is built from the eight detailed book summaries and the notes made while reading each full transcript. Everything attributed to Hill is his claim, reported and not endorsed. The 1928 edition is in the US public domain, and a scan of it is on the Internet Archive.

How to read the references. Every timestamp is a link that opens that book's video a few seconds before the moment cited. "Book 3 [22:30]" means Book 3, at that point in its reading. The book summaries themselves are linked from the table in section 1.

Confidence key for section 6. HIGH the same claim is stated two opposing ways, and both can be quoted. MEDIUM a real tension that context or emphasis partly explains. LOW a difference of wording, or of edition numbering.

1. The eight books at a glance

Book Lessons What it adds
Book 1 Introduction: The Master Mind The theory. Success as power, power as organized knowledge, the Master Mind as a "third mind", the preview of the laws, the self-analysis chart and the six basic fears.
Book 2 2 A Definite Chief Aim · 3 Self-Confidence The engine. A written aim, read daily, saturates the subconscious through auto-suggestion; fear, planted by "social heredity", is removed by a signed Self-Confidence Formula.
Book 3 4 The Habit of Saving · 5 Initiative and Leadership The first habits. Save a fixed proportion of all income; do the right thing without being told; lead by cooperation (Lincoln), not force (Napoleon).
Book 4 6 Imagination · 7 Enthusiasm The hub and the steam. Every aim is built in imagination first; enthusiasm is contagious and works on others through suggestion.
Book 5 8 Self-Control · 9 Doing More Than Paid For The balance wheel and the multiplier. "Self-control is solely a matter of thought control"; extra service returns through the "law of increasing returns".
Book 6 10 Pleasing Personality · 11 Accurate Thought The person and the method. Sincere interest in "the other fellow's game"; separate facts from information, and relevant facts from irrelevant.
Book 7 12 Concentration · 13 Cooperation Focus and allied effort. The "magic key" of fixed attention; power as concentration, cooperation and coordination; harmony turns power into "superpower".
Book 8 14 Failure · 15 Tolerance · 16 The Golden Rule The governor. Temporary defeat is not failure; intolerance is ignorance; the Golden Rule keeps the power of the other lessons safe.

The books run 3 to 3¾ hours each, about 26½ hours in all. Each ends with an "after-the-lesson visit", a short essay (Discontentment, Intolerance, The Seven Deadly Horsemen, The Evolution of Transportation, The Master Mind, Failure, Your Standing Army, Indecision), and a page of quotations.

2. The system in one page

Success and power. Hill defines success as "the attainment of your definite chief aim without violating the rights of other people" (Book 1 [27:33]). Its means is power, and "Power is organized energy or effort" (Book 2 [21:22]). Knowledge on its own is not power: "Knowledge becomes power only through organization and use" (Book 5 [2:54:24]). The whole course is a method for organizing two things, the reader's own faculties and the help of other people.

The engine: aim, auto-suggestion, subconscious. The mechanism Hill returns to in every book has four steps:

  1. Choose one definite chief aim and write it down, with a date, a plan and the price you will pay for it (Book 2 [43:06], [1:17:42]: "Know what you want, when you want it, why you want it, and how you intend to get it").
  2. Repeat it to yourself night and morning, aloud and with feeling. This is auto-suggestion, "a degree of self-hypnotism" (Book 2 [43:37]).
  3. The subconscious mind takes it over. "A person's acts are always in harmony with the dominating thoughts of his or her mind" (Book 2 [42:40]), and the subconscious, "saturated with any definite purpose", tends "to attract all that is necessary" (Book 2 [49:30]). In Book 6 Hill goes further: the subconscious is "the intermediary between the conscious thinking mind and infinite intelligence" (Book 6 [1:46:19]).
  4. Act, and keep acting. The aim must be followed by work, and by persistence, "the passkey to achievement" (Book 2 [1:26:47]). "Up to this point, you must stand back of the desire and push it. Beyond this point, the desire will stand back of you" (Book 6 [2:35:22]).

The multiplier: the Master Mind. Hill's one claimed original law is that two or more minds blended "in a spirit of perfect harmony" for a common purpose create "a third mind which may be appropriated and used by one or all of the individual minds" (Book 1 [1:09:42]). Harmony is the indispensable term (Book 1 [1:54:01]). The Master Mind reappears in every book: as the student's "friendly alliance" that signs and affirms the aim (Book 2 [1:19:51]), as the cure for a weak imagination (Book 4 [39:06]), as "group concentration of mind-power" (Book 7 [1:02:11]), and as "the principle through which you may become an accurate thinker" (Book 6 [2:54:01]).

The governor: the Golden Rule. The last lesson says the power built by the others is safe only under the Golden Rule, because it rests on "an eternal law through the operation of which we reap that which we sow" (Book 8 [2:04:29]). Hill ties this back to the engine: the law behind the Golden Rule "is none other than the law through which the principle of autosuggestion operates" (Book 8 [2:31:02]). Every act toward others registers in one's own subconscious and builds one's own character.

Fifteen laws, sixteen lessons. The course counts "fifteen laws of success" across "sixteen lessons" (Book 2 [20:15], [19:15]). The introductory lesson, the Master Mind, is not counted as a law. So Self-Confidence is "the second of the fifteen laws" although it is Lesson 3 (Book 2 [2:58:14]). Hill says as much himself in Book 3: "the fifteen qualities covered by the sixteen lessons" (Book 3 [1:33:05]).

3. How the lessons build on each other

Hill keeps pointing forward and back, and the cross-references show a deliberate structure.

  • Imagination is the "hub". Book 4 says the chief aim, self-confidence, initiative and leadership must all first be created in imagination, and that every lesson leads to this one (Book 4 [02:10], [02:38]). "First comes the thought, then organization of that thought into ideas and plans, then transformation of those plans into reality" (Book 4 [03:25]).
  • Initiative is the force, imagination the steering. "Initiative is the moving force that pushes the leader ahead, but imagination is the guiding spirit that tells him which way to go" (Book 3 [2:53:23]).
  • Enthusiasm needs a balance wheel. "Enthusiasm is the vital quality that arouses you to action, while self-control is the balance wheel that directs your action so that it will build up and not tear down" (Book 5 [01:19]).
  • Concentration holds what the aim chose. "Autosuggestion is the tool with which we dig a mental path. Concentration is the hand that holds the tool, and habit is the map" (Book 7 [10:40]).
  • Saving and service are the same habit seen twice. Book 3 asks for a fixed proportion of income to be written into the chief aim (Book 3 [05:17]); Book 5 asks for service beyond pay, which returns "compound interest upon compound interest" (Book 5 [2:09:26]). Both say that those paid only in money "are always underpaid" (Book 3 [1:43:59]; Book 5 [2:07:46]).
  • Accurate thought and the Golden Rule share one law. Book 8 grounds the Golden Rule in the Lesson 11 law by which thoughts become reality, and calls prayer "the key with which one may open the secret doorway referred to in Lesson 11" (Book 8 [2:13:10]).
  • Failure sits under all of it. Hill says the course itself grew out of his seven "turning points", the defeats in Book 8 (Book 8 [45:15]). "A man has never failed until he accepts temporary defeat as failure" (Book 8 [50:29]).
  • The closing inventory brings it together. "Your Standing Army" in Book 7 lists the fifteen qualities as soldiers, with definite purpose in command (Book 7 [3:03:40]). Book 8 closes with a self-inventory against the fifteen laws, a twelve-point Code of Ethics, and the Personal Analysis Questionnaire, to be compared with the Book 1 chart (Book 8 [3:26:12], [2:37:15], [3:27:05]).

5. Recurring stories and figures

The same small cast carries the whole course.

  • Andrew Carnegie, who Hill says suggested the course and explained the Master Mind to him (Book 1 [09:54]; Book 2 [22:51]).
  • Henry Ford, Thomas Edison and Harvey Firestone, offered as a Master Mind "doubtful if these three men themselves even know" about (Book 5 [3:05:03]; Book 1 [1:26:09]).
  • Edwin C. Barnes, who rode a freight car to West Orange to become Edison's partner (Book 4 [23:55]; Book 7 [3:07:01]).
  • James J. Hill, said to have been a poorly paid telegraph operator at forty (Book 3 [1:21:36]; Book 4 [26:14]; Book 7 [3:08:01]).
  • Don R. Mellett, the Canton publisher murdered in 1926 while planning to promote the course (Book 1 [22:13]; Book 3 [35:12]; Book 5 [1:52:43]).
  • Edward Bok, cited on the value of poverty and for the American Peace Award (Book 5 [1:02:44]; Book 8 [26:30], [1:14:35]).
  • Chicago's "Big Six", Wrigley, Thompson, Lasker and the Yellow Cab men, as a business Master Mind (Book 1 [1:39:37]; Book 5 [3:06:21]).
  • The 16,000 analyses, Hill's study of people he says he analyzed, from which come the 95/5 split of failures and successes (Book 2 [39:38]; Book 3 [1:09:43], [2:34:16]).
  • Hill's own life: the $47,000 he spent without saving (Book 3 [27:26]), Hill's Golden Rule magazine (Book 4 [1:54:05]), and the seven turning points (Book 8 [03:44]).

6. Where the books pull apart

The course is consistent on its method and its maxims. The tensions sit mainly in its explanations and its examples.

1. Three things that each cause more failures "than all other causes combined." HIGH Book 3 says lack of reserve capital causes "more of the business failures than to all other causes combined" (Book 3 [22:30]), and later that "Lack of cooperation has destroyed more business enterprises than have all other causes combined" (Book 3 [2:00:39]). Debt, in the same book, "causes more suicides every year than all other causes combined" (Book 3 [14:39]).

2. Lincoln's purpose. HIGH In Lesson 13, "The saving of the Union was of far greater concern to him than was the freedom of the slaves of the South" (Book 7 [2:06:15]). In the essay that closes the same book, "Lincoln's major definite purpose was to free the black slaves of the South" (Book 7 [3:06:24]).

3. Established science, or a law no one can explain? HIGH Book 2 says "Science has established, beyond the slightest room for doubt, that through the principle of autosuggestion" a deep desire saturates body and mind (Book 2 [1:12:01]), then that "Back of this formula lies a law that no man can explain" (Book 2 [2:31:52]). Book 1 calls mind chemistry "a known fact" (Book 1 [1:16:14]) and then "not sufficiently developed to be classed by scientific men in their catalogue of known laws" (Book 1 [1:32:46]). Telepathy is as sure as "H2O will produce water" (Book 1 [1:01:53]) but also "this theory" (Book 1 [59:02]). In Book 7, "I have no scientific basis for this hypothesis" (Book 7 [29:56]) precedes "There is no doubt" about Dr. Gates's thought waves (Book 7 [41:06]).

4. Never suggest what you don't believe, except for a "trick" that works. HIGH Book 4 approves an insurance agent's medical scare, "the trick was all right, I needed the insurance anyway" (Book 4 [2:25:21]), minutes before its rule that "you cannot afford to suggest to another person by word of mouth or by an act of yours that which you do not believe" (Book 4 [2:43:29]).

5. Flattery repels, and Mark Antony is the model. HIGH "Cheap flattery... repels instead of attracting" (Book 6 [19:13]). Yet the lesson's showpiece is Antony, whose "entire appeal was based upon his knowledge of how to influence the minds of the Roman mob through flattery", and the reader "will do well to follow the same principles" (Book 6 [56:58], [57:34]).

6. Tolerance against forced belief, and a plan to force belief. HIGH Lesson 15 condemns beliefs forced on children by their elders (Book 8 [1:16:54]), then proposes that peace be "forced upon the minds of the oncoming generations with the same diligent care that we now force upon the minds of our young the ideal of our respective religions" (Book 8 [1:36:15]).

7. A recommended brand of leadership, and no recommendation. HIGH "There can be no doubt in your mind as to the brand of leadership that is recommended in this reading course", followed at once by "We make no recommendations on this subject, however" (Book 3 [1:55:06], [1:55:11]).

8. Test everything, or don't doubt? MEDIUM Students are not asked to accept anything "without having first satisfied himself or herself by tests, experiments, and analysis" (Book 2 [16:04]), but must not experiment with the study rules (Book 2 [1:18:19]). Book 7 says "you must put your foot on the neck of skepticism and doubt" (Book 7 [21:24]); Book 6 says "Do not question... Do not doubt, but believe" (Book 6 [2:43:44]) in the same lesson that demands facts over hearsay.

9. Harmony essential, or optional? MEDIUM Minds "will not blend... unless the element of perfect harmony is present" (Book 1 [1:17:00]), yet harmony may be "obtained through discipline, voluntary or forced" (Book 1 [1:17:29]), and a football team "may win consistently... even though the members of the team may be unfriendly and out of harmony" (Book 7 [2:54:16]). Hill's answer is that this is power, not "superpower" (Book 7 [2:55:06]).

10. Non-resistance, and force. MEDIUM The self-controlled person never seeks "revenge for any cause whatsoever" (Book 5 [27:45]), but "Sometimes it may be necessary to meet force with force" (Book 5 [1:22:36]). Negotiation "without friction and argument" is the mark of success (Book 5 [50:15]), while the master salesman "stays on the job until he breaks down the opposition and forces a yes" (Book 5 [47:51]).

11. Service for its own sake, or for its return? MEDIUM The six-month experiment asks for service "for which you neither expect nor accept monetary pay" (Book 5 [2:24:43]); the same lesson promises that "you can collect a king's ransom as your reward" (Book 5 [2:37:00]).

12. Money is not the measure, and money is everything. MEDIUM Hill would be "greatly disappointed" if a student thought success "is measured by dollars alone" (Book 3 [19:10]), then says a man is "no more than so many grains of sand... unless he is entrenched behind the power of money" (Book 3 [19:37]). Book 8 condemns "this mad desire for money" (Book 8 [1:29:02]) while praising the decision that made Ford "the most powerful man on Earth" (Book 8 [3:35:40]).

13. No occultism, and crystal-gazing. MEDIUM "I have no reference to occultism" (Book 7 [19:10]); then one may "tune in and understand that which is in the mind of any person", and "Crystal-gazing is nothing but concentrated attention" (Book 7 [57:55], [57:16]).

14. Alone, or together? MEDIUM "Nor can success be attained single-handed or independently" (Book 7 [2:10:14]), against "the first step must be taken without outside aid" (Book 7 [3:12:45]). Hill's sequence (self first, then others) partly reconciles them.

15. Lesson numbers. LOW Book 1's preview numbers A Definite Chief Aim as law 1 (Book 1 [29:03]) and later calls it "the second lesson" (Book 1 [2:39:54]). Book 8 cites "the principle of organized effort, as outlined in Lesson 2" (Book 8 [1:14:49]), which is the chief-aim lesson. Most of these follow from counting fifteen laws across sixteen lessons (section 2).

7. Claims worth checking

These are stated as fact in the readings. None is checked here.

Science. The luminiferous ether as the carrier of thought, and every thought "still in existence in the form of a wave in the ether" (Book 7 [41:35]; Book 1 [49:52]). Telepathy "established through psychological research" (Book 4 [05:21]). Mercury becoming platinum if a chemist "were to expel two of its positive electrons" (Book 1 [43:40]). A hearing range of "32,000 to 38,000 vibrations per second" (Book 1 [48:15]). Mountain air "consists largely of oxygen" (Book 1 [51:09]). "The eighty-three elements of which all physical matter consists" (Book 5 [2:56:49]). Thought possibly "exactly the same sort of energy" as a dynamo's (Book 7 [28:02]).

Health. Baldness from tight hat bands, stated in two books (Book 1 [2:50:26]; Book 2 [1:45:13]). Auto-suggestion able to "destroy the most deadly disease germs" (Book 2 [53:22]). Suggestion causing a fatal cancer (Book 4 [2:22:19]) and death in seven minutes (Book 4 [2:21:16]). "Approximately 75% of those who are ill are suffering from hypochondria" (Book 6 [2:02:13]). "Most headaches may be cured in an hour's time" by an enema (Book 7 [2:29:55]). Jealousy as "dementia praecox" (Book 4 [3:24:23]).

History and people. Hill's access to Carnegie and the men he names, "studied... at close range, in person" (Book 1 [10:22]), which biographers dispute. James J. Hill at the telegraph key at forty (Book 4 [26:14]), when by then he was a freight and steamboat businessman. Edison's "10,000 experiments" before the phonograph spoke (Book 2 [1:26:32]), a figure usually told of the light bulb or the battery. The Federal Reserve "practically ensures the United States against a money panic" (Book 7 [1:58:53]), a year before 1929. Alvin York winning "every decoration" (Book 7 [1:12:14]). Lincoln and Washington as "Southern men" (Book 6 [1:31:16]). Wilson as "the one and only man in the history of the world who offered a plan for abolishing war" (Book 6 [1:32:16]).

Statistics and arithmetic. The 16,000 analyses, with no method given, used for more than one dataset (Book 2 [39:38]; Book 3 [1:09:43], [2:34:16]). "92% of these unfortunate men and women are in prison because they lacked the necessary self-control" (Book 5 [01:37]). Eighty trophies at $250 each said to total $45,000, not $20,000 (Book 3 [1:00:54]). $25 a month growing to $5,000 in ten years, which needs about 9–10% compound interest (Book 3 [25:51]). Ford's $7.50 of service for every $5 of wages, for which Hill admits "no authentic figures" (Book 5 [2:11:59]).

Predictions. "Within five years every railroad crossing in the country will be amply protected against automobile accidents" (Book 5 [1:32:39]).

8. Dated material

The readings are of their time, and the summaries report them as read. They include racial categories and remarks (a "small colored boy" story, Book 4 [2:51:48]; Native Americans as making "no attempt whatsoever" to improve their environment, Book 7 [14:30]; the most "degrading" slanders of Lincoln and Harding, Book 6 [1:31:05]). There are gender assumptions throughout (a wife "may either make or break him", Book 1 [2:01:34]; "By nature all men are polygamists", Book 2 [1:48:07]). There are attacks on "sectarian teachings" and revivals alongside prayer as "the key" (Book 2 [1:42:35]; Book 7 [1:15:04]; Book 8 [2:13:10]). Mental illness is described in the terms of 1928 ("go to the psychopathic hospital", Book 4 [3:26:11]), and sex is treated as a mental stimulant and a motive (Book 1 [1:21:38]; Book 7 [2:59:52]).

9. The pattern underneath

The course works on two levels, and they can be read separately.

The practical level is a set of habits: write down one aim and read it daily; save a fixed share of income; do more than you are paid for; take an interest in other people before your own business; control temper and talk; separate facts from hearsay; treat defeat as information; work with others in harmony. Hill states these plainly, repeats them in every book, and ties each to an exercise.

The explanatory level is the machinery Hill offers for why the habits work: thought as vibration in the ether, telepathy, "mind chemistry", a subconscious with a line to "infinite intelligence". This is where nearly all the tensions in section 6 and the claims in section 7 sit, and it is where Hill himself hedges most, calling the same idea a fact in one passage and a hypothesis in the next. The habits do not depend on it: a reader can follow the toolkit in section 4 without accepting the ether.

The Golden Rule lesson joins the two levels. Hill's case for it is partly moral and partly mechanical: every act toward others is also a suggestion to oneself, so character is built "in exact duplicate" of conduct (Book 8 [2:32:07]). "There can never be success without happiness, and no man can be happy without dispensing happiness to others" (Book 8 [2:23:28]).
