Investing & wholesaling

Real estate investing & wholesaling — a second real-estate cluster outside the RE-V series

A synthesis of 108 transcribed videos (Queue rows 1372–2626, August 2026 → September 2026) that are real-estate-related but distinct from the RE-V macro-housing-market series (see the thematic index). 96 are from Richard Taylor ("RTaylor", Grandin Taylor LLC / Hold My Hand Wholesale) — live cold-calls, negotiations and creative-finance deals; the remainder are adjacent legal, landlord-tenant and commercial-distress content. Figures are as stated by the speakers; none are independently verified. Row-number references link to each video's published summary.

What this content is, and how it differs from RE-V

This cluster is practitioner content, not macro analysis: the bulk of it is Richard Taylor, a wholesaler who records live cold calls to listing agents and homeowners, negotiations, and creative-finance closings from his kitchen or studio (rows 1506, 1593, 1920, 2421). Where the RE-V macro series reasons about aggregates, this material is transactional — a single phone call, a single contract, a single assignment fee — and its unit of analysis is the deal (rows 1499, 1587, 2094). None of the numbers, claims or outcomes described below are independently verified; they are self-reported by the speakers on livestreams and podcasts, and should be read as attributed claims throughout.

The content is built on a volume premise: the speaker repeatedly asserts that roughly 11,000 houses sell per day in the United States, occasionally stated as 13,000, and that 1.4 million to 4 million properties sit listed on Zillow at any moment (rows 1467, 1481, 1482, 1499, 1527, 1587, 1916, 2094). The soft market is treated as the opportunity rather than the problem: secondary-market cities are described as having houses sitting for hundreds of days, often owned by out-of-state landlords bleeding money on taxes, insurance and mortgage payments (row 1476). In San Antonio, the speaker claims 4,000 listed properties have owners with 20% equity or less, meaning they lose money at sale (row 1649). One claim is that real estate no longer "works" as a rental investment in 90% of the United States because mortgage payments exceed rents (row 1918).

The adjacent, non-Taylor material rounds out the picture: commercial-distress commentary on banks dumping buildings (row 1372), UK-flavoured legal explainers on trusts and lease forfeiture (rows 1508, 1993), a 1099-A "debt discharge" segment of dubious provenance (row 1446), mortgage-policy commentary on the proposed 50-year mortgage (row 1845), and credit-market stress reporting (rows 2039, 2044).

Core business model: wholesaling mechanics

The stated definition is simple: find a property at a discount, put it under contract, and sell the contract position to an investor for a fee, with the value delivered being the discount itself rather than any capital deployed (rows 1499, 1587, 2094). No licence is claimed to be required — the speaker says a licence is only needed to publicly list properties and sign listing agreements (row 1483). Contracts are described as assignable by default unless explicitly marked non-assignable, so wholesalers are advised to verify the absence of a non-assignment clause rather than ask for one to be added (row 1652).

What distinguishes this operator from the wider wholesaling world is the channel. Rather than text blasting or skip-traced lists, the core method is calling listing agents on Zillow properties that are already on-market — described as his superpower, in contrast to partners who work off-market (rows 1471, 1480, 1587). The speaker says he text blasted 3,000 messages a day in 2021 and 2022 before abandoning it, citing TCPA changes in 2021 (rows 1468, 1587). A direct mail campaign on high-equity lists is described as costing $10,000 for 18,000–20,000 postcards and yielding two deals worth $50,000–$60,000 in assignment fees (row 1468). A tiered capital ladder is taught: with no money use government lists or Zillow; with $300 start text blasting; with $1,000 hire VAs; with $5,000 run ads (row 1471).

Formulas quoted, stated as given:

  • Rental underwriting: (Rent × 0.8 − PITI) × 12 ÷ Total Cost of Entry, with a deal considered viable above 20% (rows 2011, 1469).
  • The same, expanded: (Rent × 0.8 − PITI) × 12 ÷ (20% down payment + $10,000 closing costs), with investors typically seeking 20% or higher (row 1501).
  • Offer formula: approximately 70% of list price, or after-repair value multiplied by 0.6, minus renovation costs and desired profit (row 1483).
  • The "Zillow rule of 30": on-market deals need to be at least $30,000 below list to work for most buyers in current conditions (row 1468).
  • The "rule of 100" for cheap properties under $250K ARV: buyers typically pay $100,000 less than the after-repair value (row 1469).
  • The creative-finance offer template: 10% above list price, 10% down, monthly payments equal to 40% of rental income, on a 72-month (or 5–6 year) balloon (rows 2034, 2011, 1476).

Activity targets are equally explicit: submit 10 offers a day and have 10 conversations a day with realtors (row 1499); call 500 leads to guarantee a deal within 30 days, or expect to speak to roughly 15 people before one signs, versus 5 showing interest (row 1480); call 20–50 owners until one accepts a lowball (row 2094); of 20 lowball calls most are rejected, but some sellers return months later when the property still hasn't sold (row 1917). The counterpart discipline is buyer-side: identify at least 10 active buyers per market before locking contracts, spending roughly a week cold-calling from a buyer database (rows 1622, 1922, 2024, 2100).

Named tools and communities recur. BuyBox Cartel (buyboxcartel.com) is the speaker's own marketplace and buyer database, searchable by address to surface investors who bought in the past 12 months, priced at $69 per month, and said to have taken over a year to code from scratch (rows 1474, 1477, 1503, 1504, 1505, 1527, 1916, 1918, 2545). Proof-of-funds and pre-approval letters are generated free from WholesaleDoubleClose.com to submit offers without personal capital (rows 1470, 1478). PropStream is used to filter active on-market listings with 10–30% equity and, optionally, mortgage interest rate (row 1501). Deal flow and buyers are also farmed from Facebook groups: BRRRR investment groups with 325,000 members, "Sub 2 Everything," "Creative Finance" with 23,000+ members, "Sub 2 Buyers Club," and the speaker's own "Hold My Hand Wholesale" group (rows 1470, 1501, 1561, 1618, 1919). Section 8 buyers are found by joining ten Section 8 investing communities and searching for "just closed" posts (row 1480). Gold Key TC, a transaction-coordination company the speaker co-owns, is said to close mortgage-takeover deals in 48–50 states for roughly $950 in fees (rows 1501, 1593).

Reported economics vary widely by telling. Average assignment fee is variously given as $7,000–$8,000, $8,000, or $8,000–$18,000 depending on discount (rows 1468, 1481, 1922); deal volume as 15–25 per month, 15–20 per month, or 10–20 per month with $3,000–$4,000 take-home after splitting with students (rows 1471, 1608, 1920). Section 8 cash-deal assignment fees are put at $4,000–$6,000, with cheques typically $2,000–$7,000 (row 1480). The largest assignment fee the speaker says he has seen is $102,000 (row 1587). Disposition is outsourced to a four-person team in the Philippines, each selling one to three deals a week, with the dispo manager closing roughly 10 of 15–25 monthly deals; the dispo operation is said to net $20,000–$40,000 monthly on a 40% cut, against $8,000 weekly payroll (rows 1471, 1918). Deal mix is described as roughly 50–60% buy-and-hold rentals below $200,000 in the Midwest and South, 35–40% fix-and-flip, 10% creative finance — elsewhere as 70% cash and 30% creative, and elsewhere as 70–80% cash through the program (rows 1471, 1480, 1499).

Call craft is taught explicitly. Asking about roof age, HVAC and water heater condition immediately signals a wholesaler and triggers defensive behaviour; so does over-explaining credentials, DSCR terms or down-payment percentages (row 1652). Asking "why is the seller selling" is identified by an irritated agent as recognisable "wholesale speak" rather than a genuine buyer question (row 1655). The recommended alternative is a nonchalant, low-pressure tone and guiding the agent to conclude the deal is hard by asking "Is that the buyer profile you're looking for?" (row 1652). Contracts are structured with a seven-business-day inspection period and earnest money due only after inspection, allowing exit without upfront capital (row 1919); standard earnest money is stated as 1–3% of purchase price, not the 10% one seller demanded on a $65,000 property (row 1608).

Target markets are consistently the cheap, high-yield Midwest and South: Detroit, Cleveland, Akron, Dayton, Toledo, Columbus, Canton, Memphis, Indianapolis, Birmingham, Huntsville, St. Louis, Milwaukee and Atlanta, with the majority of deals in Michigan (rows 1480, 1502, 1593, 1622). Properties in those markets are said to be buyable at roughly 70% of list, typically $60,000–$80,000 after discount and fee, yielding $5,000 cheques within 5 to 10 calls (row 1480). Dayton and Akron are singled out for deal velocity; Flint faces buyer resistance on cash deals despite good returns, though seller-financed Flint deals move better (row 1502). Detroit is elsewhere disparaged by the same speaker — 30 shootings a day, brutal taxes, no real appreciation after dollar devaluation, with houses that sold for $30,000–$50,000 in 1990 fetching $80,000–$100,000 today (row 2092). Indianapolis is named the strongest market fit on triplexes and quads at $300,000–$400,000, rents of $1,200–$1,600 per unit, 6–8% cap rates and landlord-friendly eviction law, with the analysis attributed to an AI assistant (rows 2092, 2100).

The pipeline is fragile at the edges. A quadplex at 1024 East 74th in Cleveland failed inspection on a 25-year-old roof, an active plumbing leak with structural damage, outdated wiring, cockroach infestation and missing smoke detectors, and the buyer withdrew; the speaker estimated $60,000–$70,000 of repairs on a property spanning half a block, with $81,500 of deals in progress at the time (row 1920). On the flip side, a first fix-and-flip contractor took $7,000 for electrical work and refused to complete or refund it; the initial estimate was $25,000, $38,000 was sent, and $27,000 of work was left undone, with the speaker claiming it takes approaching 30 contractors to find one willing to take a job (row 1609). A separate $8,000 loan for a wholesale deal ended in a default judgment of roughly $34,000 collected by wage garnishment (row 1593).

The teaching business is inseparable from the deal business. The Hold My Hand Wholesale community is variously described as 130,000 members, 10,000 students, 7,000 members at $20, and a Discord that fell from 13,000 members two years prior to 7,570 after a platform collapse (rows 1477, 1502, 2024, 2094). The speaker states that only about 5% of those who attempt wholesaling succeed, and that roughly 1% attend livestreams (rows 1499, 1502). Most deals are said to be sourced from students rather than found personally, with splits like $18,000 divided $10,800 to the student and $7,200 to the house (row 1608). A promotional competition offers $70,000 cash or a 2020 Corvette Stingray to the VIP member posting the most deals, starting April 10th, 2026 and ending at 2,000 VIP members (row 1475). Finder's fees of $10,000 per property are advertised, with over $100,000 claimed paid out and $11,000 paid in a single week (rows 1478, 1482, 2091, 2545).

Creative finance: subject-to and seller financing

Creative finance is presented as the answer to a market where conventional buyers need 20% down at 7% and properties won't cash flow (rows 1482, 2091). The speaker's own portfolio is described at various points as 23 rental properties, 24 houses at age 24, 25 doors, roughly 30 properties, 20 subject-to and creative-finance holdings, and 16 buildings with 23 units — nearly all acquired without qualifying for a traditional mortgage, at rates between 0% and 3.6% (rows 1465, 1474, 1479, 1482, 1499, 1501, 1618, 1651, 2094). He claims 21 of 25 properties were bought subject-to with loans left in the original owners' names, including around eight VA loans plus FHA and conventional loans (row 1479).

The central legal argument offered for subject-to is that the HUD-1 closing statement explicitly lists "existing loans taken subject to" on lines 203 and 503, which the speaker treats as official acknowledgement that a property can be sold without paying off the existing mortgage (rows 1479, 1501). Pace Morby is named as the figure who popularised the method (row 1479).

The due-on-sale workaround described is trust-based: the property is placed in a trust, the investor is added with controlling interest while the original owner retains a small stake — variously 10% or a "watermark" interest, with the buyer controlling 90% — and the original owner then leases their portion to the investor (rows 1561, 1651, 1922). An attorney named Jeff is said to have executed this structure roughly 3,000 times since 2004 and to have litigated cases where the clause was called, with lenders backing off (row 1561). The seller's protection is a deed-of-trust forfeiture provision: the property reverts to the original owner automatically if the buyer misses two consecutive months of payments, or 45 days in other tellings, without foreclosure (rows 1479, 1496, 1625, 1650, 1651). One variant adds arranging a new loan for the homeowner to offset the original mortgage from their credit profile (row 2626). Payments across the portfolio are run through a servicing company that emails on payment or missed payment rather than logging into 23 separate mortgage portals (row 1651).

Assumption is distinguished from subject-to: in a true assumption the loan leaves the first person's name entirely, requiring qualification and income statements, whereas creative finance leaves the debt and the credit exposure with the seller (row 1650). FHA and VA loans are described as assumable by non-veterans and non-first-time buyers, with VA entitlement averaging $765,000 nationally and ranging from $300,000 in Ohio to $1.5 million in San Diego; FHA assumption is put at a 30–60 day close (rows 1501, 1561). FHA and VA loan modifications are said to allow missed payments to be moved to the back end of the loan, enabling $0-down takeovers even in pre-foreclosure (row 1501). Motivated sellers are claimed to sometimes pay buyers $5,000–$15,000 to assume their debt (row 1501).

The seller-side pitch is aimed squarely at negative-equity owners. Examples include the Orange Park, Florida nursing student and veteran who bought three years earlier at $235,000, now owes $230,000 against a $207,000 value, and would need roughly $10,000 to sell conventionally — the property was taken for $0 down at a price matching the balance (rows 1617, 1618, 2626 for the analogous structure). A Queen Creek, Arizona house listed at $390,000 had $395,000 owed and $35,000 of closing costs on a traditional sale (row 1922). Condos are said to face minimal seller resistance because they don't appreciate and owners are frequently underwater (row 1618). A flat $3,000 fee is charged to connect underwater sellers with buyers willing to take over payments; the speaker says he has sold four of his own properties this way and facilitated roughly 75 such trust deals for others (rows 1651, 1788).

Entry costs for buying into these positions from the community are stated at $0 to $15,000, $0 to $35,000, or $10,000 to $35,000 depending on the group (rows 1501, 1561, 1618). Structures quoted include 0% interest with balloons at year six or seven, interest-only notes, and restructuring an existing note — one proposal moved a Cleveland duplex note owing $145,000 at 5% interest-only with a 2033 balloon to 3% principal-and-interest over 40 years at $150,000, with a 7–8 year balloon and $7,500 down (row 1920). Sellers are sometimes offered above asking price in exchange for terms: $450,000 paid on a $380,000 Omaha listing to induce seller financing, and a general strategy of raising the price to win better-than-bank terms on stale secondary-market listings (rows 1482, 1619).

The limits are shown as often as the wins. A Clarksville, Tennessee seller wanting 4% over 30 years with $33,000 down on a $325,000 property produced a $1,561 payment against $1,500 rent — unworkable (row 1918). A Jacksonville multifamily at $600,000 with 10% down, $2,000 monthly and a 4-year balloon needed a 6-year balloon to be refinanceable (row 1496). An El Paso quadplex renting $775 a unit required accepting a $1,500 monthly loss at minimum down-payment terms unless rents rose roughly $1,000 per unit (row 1923).

Legal and regulatory friction

Disclosure law is the binding constraint on on-market wholesaling. Ohio implemented a requirement that wholesalers disclose their intent to the seller on a separate document, with title companies enforcing it and blocking double closes without it — which the speaker says has made on-market wholesaling in Ohio significantly harder and off-market deals comparatively easier (rows 1466, 1468, 1471). Cleveland specifically requires disclosure to the agent (row 1622). A state-by-state map given on one stream lists Oklahoma, Philadelphia, South Carolina, Oregon and Illinois as requiring disclosure, with Michigan and Ohio said not to require explicit disclosure if the wholesaler stays silent — a claim that sits in direct tension with the Ohio statements elsewhere in the same cluster (rows 1652, 1468, 1471). Illinois is described as limiting wholesalers to one deal per year for Illinois-incorporated LLCs doing business there, attributed to National Association of Realtors influence (row 1622).

Enforcement runs both ways. The speaker says he uses specific-performance lawsuits rather than filing memorandums to hold sellers to contracts, citing Texas and Florida; in one Clearwater case the seller tried to cancel alleging unlawful coercion, was served, and yielded (row 1469). He also reports dealing with two properties encumbered by memorandums in a single week, both at least $60K deals, one in Texas where the memorandum was four to five years old and the statute of limitations had run (row 1469).

Agent conflict is a recurring set piece. One realtor curses him out on a call about a property he had wholesaled two years earlier that was back on market after 85 days (row 1466); another refuses to present a loan-assumption offer on the grounds it isn't a clean sale, despite the seller facing a $10,000–$15,000 loss, which the speaker argues violates the Arizona rule that a licensee under a representation agreement must present any and all offers (row 1650). Another blocks him after a seller-financing offer, citing a 1031 exchange requirement (row 1957). A realtor refused to present offers on a negative-cash-flow triplex whose seller had rejected every creative structure (row 1588). Commission games are described too: the listing agent claiming to waive the buyer's-agent commission as a concession when in fact they are taking their standard 3% instead of splitting 6% (row 1524). The speaker says realtors mass-reported him for fraud (row 1478), and that his original YouTube channel — claimed at 14 million views a month and about $6,000 in monthly revenue — was banned on January 1, 2026 after a strike for claiming viewers could make $20,000 in 30 days, which he attributes to a competitor's bot filing thousands of reports (row 1481).

Adjacent legal content fills in doctrine the wholesaling videos assume. One explainer sets out that a house can have two owners — one holding legal title with the power to sign and sell, another holding beneficial interest and enjoyment — and that a trust is the mechanism binding the two, which is precisely the machinery the subject-to trust structures rely on (row 1993). Another covers commercial lease forfeiture, arguing that a tenant one day late through a bank error may lose the lease on strict contract terms, but that equity may bar forfeiture where enforcement would be unconscionable, while cautioning that equitable relief is discretionary and will not rescue a party from a bad bargain (row 1508). A separate segment presents the 1099-A as a debt-discharge instrument, claiming it functions like a three-party check signed on the back, that it must be obtained from the IRS as a scannable carbon-copy form, and that roughly one in twenty dealerships will accept one — claims presented by that speaker, not verified here (row 1446).

Policy context from the adjacent channels: qualified mortgages cannot exceed 30-year terms under Dodd-Frank, so a proposed 50-year product would require new legislation (row 1845). And a bipartisan affordable-housing bill barring large institutional investors from owning more than 350 single-family homes is reported as blocked, with New York City rent-freeze legislation cited as a countervailing intervention (row 2208).

The commercial/multifamily distress angle

The commercial commentary describes buildings trading at 80 cents on the dollar despite sound fundamentals, driven by three simultaneous pressures: rent softness from excess supply, rising operating expenses across utilities, insurance and property taxes, and higher interest rates on floating-rate debt (row 1372). Assets with debt service coverage ratios under 1.0 — 0.6 to 0.7 is the example given — are described as worth only 60 to 70 cents on the dollar, forcing owners either to fund negative cash flow or default, while deferred maintenance climbs because every dollar of cash flow goes to the mortgage (row 1372). Loan maturities are said to be resetting higher, with loans maturing in spring 2027 moving from the fours into the fives on identical properties (row 1372).

The equity destruction cited is severe: a 200-unit Class A building built at $400,000 per unit now valued at $250,000–$275,000 per unit, and a 100% vacant asset bought four years ago at $90,000 per unit now offered at $8,000 per unit with lenders taking large haircuts (row 1372). Multifamily supply is said to have hit a 50-year high in 2025, with absorption and rent growth projected to cross in late 2027 to early 2028 and a value-creation window in 2028–2029 — a forecast, not a fact (row 1372). Regionally, downtown Phoenix is described as offering 3–4 months free on 12-month leases while adjacent Tempe stays tight; Austin and Nashville are called overbuilt with falling prices, and the Midwest relatively stable (row 1372).

The wholesaling side meets this distress from below. A Buffalo student-housing facility marketed at $19,000 a month in rent turned out to be 18% occupied, requiring renegotiation with occupancy contingencies tiered at 50%, 75% and 90% (row 1524). A St. Louis duplex listed at $49,000 with tornado damage illustrates condition-based evaluation of genuinely distressed stock (row 2100). A 13-unit Detroit commercial property listed at $600,000 — with only 10 of 13 units occupied generating $5,000 a month, and an 87-year-old seller trying to retire against a ~$120,000 payoff — drew a $437,000 offer, a $163,000 discount (row 1959).

The broader credit backdrop from the adjacent channels is consistent with pressure rather than relief: Fitch is cited as putting the trailing 12-month default rate across 1,300 U.S. private credit borrowers at 6.3% in August, a record, with 45% of August defaults caused by stressed maturity extensions (row 2039). Blue Owl is reported to have written a senior loan down from par to near zero within months, with private credit valuations described as model-driven and slow to reflect reality (row 2044). Blackstone is reported to be facilitating a secondary sale for investors in a U.S. real estate fund with roughly $11 billion of NAV (row 2039). Non-residential private construction is described as losing momentum from late 2023 into modest contraction by late 2024, with residential construction spending falling sharply from around September 2025 (row 2047).

How this complements the RE-V macro picture

The two bodies of content describe the same conditions from opposite ends. The macro view sees elevated rates, record multifamily supply, stalled transaction volume and a first-time-buyer age of 40 (rows 1372, 1845); the practitioner view experiences those same facts as 249 average days on market for Detroit Section 8 listings, 200-day-old listings the agent can't move, and sellers who cannot clear their own payoff (rows 1655, 1918, 1649). Where macro analysis calls it a frozen market, this cluster calls it inventory.

The mechanism linking them is negative equity and payment shock. Rates rising from roughly 3% in early 2022 to 7.5% is given as the reason buyers withdrew and values flatlined (row 1618), and DSCR lending at around 7% on 30-year investment terms, with lenders qualifying on rent-to-mortgage coverage rather than credit score, is what makes ordinary listings unfinanceable (rows 1588, 2010). DSCR lenders are also said not to fund below $60,000–$65,000 because the cash flow is too small to be worth writing (row 1524). Creative finance is best read as the private-market arbitrage on legacy 2–3% mortgages that the macro data records as the lock-in effect: the speaker claims to have taken over mortgages between 2% and 3% with tenants paying in excess of those payments (rows 1649, 1465, 1561, 2011).

The distress pipeline also runs both directions. Macro commentary describes banks and lenders taking haircuts on commercial assets (row 1372); the practitioner cluster shows who buys at the bottom of that chain — a 13-unit Detroit building at a $163,000 discount, a tornado-damaged St. Louis duplex at $49,000, a misrepresented Buffalo student-housing asset at 18% occupancy (rows 1959, 2100, 1524).
