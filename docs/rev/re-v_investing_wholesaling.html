Where the RE-V series is macro/forecasting content aimed at buyers and passive observers, this cluster is practitioner content aimed at active investors — deal mechanics, negotiation scripts, financing structures, and business-operations detail. What this content is, and how it differs from RE-V

RTaylor's videos are a mix of: (1) live cold-calls to sellers and listing agents, negotiating wholesale assignments and creative-finance (subject-to, seller-finance) deals in real time; (2) group livestreams/podcasts with fellow wholesalers (Zach Ginn, Max Dier) debating strategy and reviewing deals phoned in by students; and (3) business-narrative/promotional content (a YouTube-ban recovery story, a "Buy Box Cartel" marketplace pitch, a rent-payment giveaway). The throughline across nearly all of it is wholesaling real estate — locking a property under contract (often at $0 down, via mortgage-takeover/subject-to structures) and assigning that contract to an end buyer for a fee, typically $1,500–$50,000, occasionally higher on creative-finance deals.

This is a genuinely different market segment from RE-V's housing-market-macro framing: RE-V describes why prices are falling and forecasts where; RTaylor's content describes how to transact inside a soft, high-inventory, high-cancellation market — and several of his own on-camera remarks (falling on-market margins, a new Ohio wholesale-disclosure law, buyers walking from cash-flow-negative deals) are consistent with, and implicitly corroborate, the macro correction RE-V documents.

Core business model: wholesaling mechanics

The basic trade. Lock a property under a short option/marketing agreement (sometimes just 5–7 days) before it's fully contracted, shop it to a buyer list, and assign the contract for a fee — the buyer closes directly with the seller (or, in a double-close, the wholesaler briefly takes title using lender-funded proof-of-funds before immediately reselling). Fees across the transcripts range from $1,500–$3,000 on thin on-market deals up to $13,000–$50,000+ on off-market or creative-finance deals (rows 1468, 1469, 1470, 1471, 1481, 1602, 1608).

On-market vs. off-market. On-market (MLS/Zillow-sourced) wholesaling is repeatedly described as dying as a standalone business by mid-2026: the "Zillow Rule of 30" (need a deal at least $30,000 below list to be assignable) and "Rule of 100" (buyers pay roughly $100,000 under ARV on sub-$250K properties) are cited as the viability bar, and one host (row 1471) states rejection rates on submitted on-market deals run 50–99%. Off-market (door-knocking, sticky notes, cold-calling pre-foreclosure/absentee-owner lists, text-blasting) is presented as the scalable, durable core of the business — cheaper to start (near-zero capital via sticky notes/door-knocking) and less exposed to new legal disclosure requirements (row 1468, 1469, 1470, 1471).

Reverse wholesaling. Several videos describe finding a buyer's specific "buy box" first (via Facebook investor groups like "BRRRR Invest," 325,000 members, searching "just closed" posts, or a proprietary tool called BuyBoxCartel.com), then sourcing a matching on-market listing — argued to work better on-market (large inventory to match against) than off-market (rows 1470, 1474, 1602).

Cash-on-cash return formula used repeatedly to price deals for rental-investor buyers: (monthly rent × 0.8 − PITI) × 12 ÷ total cost of entry. Buyer thresholds cited range 20–30%, with 20% treated as the Midwest/Detroit-market floor (rows 1468, 1602, 1608).

Creative finance: subject-to and seller financing

A large share of the higher-value deals use subject-to (the buyer takes title and continues paying the seller's existing mortgage, which stays in the seller's name/credit) rather than a cash purchase:

  • Mechanism explained via the "HUD statement" (closing disclosure) subject-to line items and an inheritance/next-of-kin analogy; property placed in a trust, with the closing attorney as trust beneficiary; a 45+ day missed-payment default lets the seller reclaim the property via quitclaim rather than formal foreclosure (row 1479).
  • Positive vs. negative liens matter: assistance-program/solar/HOA-approved liens can be taken subject-to; mechanic's/contractor liens must be paid off at sale (row 1602).
  • Deal examples: a Florida VA-purchaser seller ($218K balance + $12K assistance lien) sold subject-to for $0 down, assigned same-livestream for a $3,000 fee (row 1602); a San Antonio rental bought subject-to at 6.5%, $0 down, now cash-flow negative against a proposed "dueño a dueño" (owner-to-owner) refinance wrap (row 1470); a subject-to flip assuming a 3% mortgage at $271,000, resold at $365,000 for a $94,000 spread (row 1471).
  • Seller financing is treated more cautiously on distressed properties: buyer capital tied up in a large down payment plus separate rehab funding is judged worse than 100%-financed hard/private money, unless the seller-finance terms carry no balloon (indefinite term) — the one scenario the host says justifies bending the "no seller-finance on distressed inventory" rule (row 1608). On turnkey/rent-ready properties, seller financing at low-to-no interest is described as attractive and increasingly common as buyers shy from negative-cash-flow subject-to deals (rows 1470, 1471, 1474).
  • Assignable seller-finance listing example: a fully renovated San Antonio-area home offered at $0 down, $2,300/month, no credit check or qualification (row 1469).

Legal and regulatory friction (a recurring, escalating theme)

  • Ohio's new wholesale-disclosure law (effective ~January 2026): title companies now require a signed seller disclosure that the buyer is wholesaling before an MLS/realtor-involved deal can close; off-market (no-realtor) deals are unaffected, which the host cites as accelerating his shift away from on-market wholesaling toward flipping and off-market creative finance (rows 1466, 1468, 1471).
  • Memorandums of contract vs. suing for specific performance: memos (a lien-like recording tool) are described as legally weak, especially in Texas (short statute of limitations); the host says he strongly prefers threatening/filing a specific-performance lawsuit when a seller tries to back out, and reports a near-100% settlement/capitulation rate once served (rows 1469, 1470).
  • TCPA/cold-calling exposure: low-volume personal cold-calling from an individual cell phone is treated as materially lower legal risk than mass text-blasting, which the host says carries theoretical $500–1,500-per-violation exposure (rarely litigated given the cost of suing over a small claim) (rows 1468, 1469).
  • Fraud-adjacent flashpoints: a "mass reporting" campaign got the host's original ~100,000-subscriber YouTube channel banned for making documented-but-explicit dollar-figure income claims (row 1481); separately he was accused of fraud for paying uncredentialed "finders" fees and for using a proof-of-funds-generator service tied to a real lender (row 1478) — both defended on camera as long-standing, disclosed industry practices.
  • 21st Century Road to Housing Act (also covered from the buy-side in the RE-V series): a federal ban on entities owning 350+ homes from further purchases is referenced as reshaping the institutional-investor buyer pool wholesalers sell into (cross-reference RE-V rows 1326, 1336, 1360, 1518).

The commercial/multifamily distress angle (row 1372)

A separate, higher-altitude interview (host "Jeff" with multifamily operator Ken Macro, ~10,000 units under management) describes institutional-scale distress consistent with RE-V's macro narrative but from the lender/operator side:

  • Commercial assets trading at roughly 80 cents on the dollar despite otherwise-sound fundamentals; some properties show debt-service coverage of only 0.6–0.7.
  • A 100%-vacant Texas property near a military base offered at $8,000/unit (below land replacement cost), with an estimated $20,000–30,000/unit renovation cost and ~$60,000/unit stabilized value — a cash-only, non-financeable distressed-asset trade.
  • Multifamily new-construction supply hit a 50-year high in 2025; the supply/demand crossover (and expected rent-growth "value creation" phase) is forecast for 2028–2029.
  • Three drivers of CRE distress cited: rent softness from oversupply, rising operating expenses (utilities, insurance, property taxes), and rising rates on floating-rate debt — fixed-rate holders can "weather the storm" to maturity; floating-rate holders face forced sales.
  • Framed as a "not yet, but soon" buying window: the advice is to assemble capital, team, and target markets now, not to strike yet.

The landlord-tenant legal doctrine clip (row 1508)

A short explainer on the equitable doctrine of "relief against forfeiture" — using a hypothetical of a 20-year tenant whose rent arrives one day late due to a bank error, triggering a lease-forfeiture clause. Core point: equity can restrain a landlord from unconscionably enforcing a strict contractual right, but does not rescue a party from a genuinely bad bargain — a doctrinal note rather than an investing tactic.

How this complements the RE-V macro picture

Several details in this cluster corroborate, from ground level, the demand-recession and financing-stress themes documented across the RE-V series (see re-v_index.md):

  • Falling on-market wholesale margins and the shift to off-market/creative-finance deals mirrors RE-V's documentation of rising cancellation rates, falling contract-signing volume, and buyer walk-aways on cash-flow-negative purchases.
  • Buyer resistance to negative-cash-flow subject-to deals (row 1471: buyers who took over 3% loans 1–2 years ago on cash-flow-negative terms have since lost properties to foreclosure) is a street-level echo of RE-V's FHA/DTI-stress findings.
  • The institutional-investor pullback RE-V documents (Wall Street landlords net-selling, the 350-home ownership ban) directly shrinks one of RTaylor's core wholesale-buyer pools, which several 2026 videos explicitly reference when explaining why deal terms are getting harder to place.
  • Regional overlap: RTaylor's Detroit/Ohio/Tennessee/Florida deal geography overlaps heavily with RE-V's most-distressed metros, though the wholesaling content treats that distress as opportunity (steep discounts to lock up) rather than a risk to forecast.
