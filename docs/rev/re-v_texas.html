Texas housing: the state against the nation, and its five biggest cities compared

Texas is cooling faster than the US. Prices are flat to falling while the nation edges up, migration is at a 20-year low, and the state leads the country in both new permits and bank repossessions. Inside Texas, Fort Worth is the tightest market, Austin is the deepest correction, and San Antonio is the most oversupplied.

Five things investors should know

  • Texas prices are flat to down while US prices rise. The Texas median sale price was $339,000 in July, unchanged from a year earlier, and the typical Texas home value is down 1.6% year over year. The US median sale price is up 1.6% and the US typical value is up 1.2% D1 D4 D6. Texas home values are 6.1% below their August 2022 peak V3.
  • The migration engine has stalled. Texas gained only 67,299 net domestic migrants in the year to July 2025. That is the smallest gain since 2005 and 69% below the 2022 peak of 218,840 V12 V14. Harris County (Houston) and Dallas County each lost more than 43,000 people to other parts of the US D10.
  • Supply keeps coming. Texas issued 199,258 building permits (all unit types) in the 12 months to August 2026, the most of any state, ahead of Florida's 164,496 V10. The state has 5.5 months of supply against 4.9 nationally D1 D6.
  • Distress is concentrated here. In August 2026 Texas had more bank repossessions (REOs, 1,835) than any other state, and Houston, Dallas and San Antonio were the top three large metros for REOs nationally. Texas also had the most foreclosure starts in the first half of 2026 and the shortest average foreclosure timeline, at 155 days D13 D14.
  • Carrying costs are higher than the national norm. Effective property-tax rates in the five big counties run from 1.31% to 1.55%, against about 0.90% nationally on ATTOM's (different) method. The average Texas homeowners premium was $3,489 in 2025. The 30-year mortgage rate is 7.28%, up from 6.34% a year ago D15 D16 D17 D7.

Austin at a glance

  • Austin has fallen further than any big US housing market. Typical home values are 26.8% below their June 2022 peak, the largest drop among the 100 largest US metros. The next two, Cape Coral and North Port in Florida, are down about 19% and 17% V1.
  • Asking prices are still falling, and fast. Travis County's median list price is down 9.3% year over year (September 2026), about seven times the US decline of 1.35% D2.
  • The worst landlord math in Texas. A year's typical rent covers only 3.8% of a typical home's value, against 7.1% in Houston and 6.3% nationally, and that's before Austin's 1.31% property tax and insurance D4 D5 D15.
  • Rents have reset too. Austin metro rents are more than 20% below their 2022 peak, the softest of the large US rental markets V7.
  • Buyers are coming back, but slowly. Pending sales were up 17% year over year in early July, the biggest jump of any metro Redfin tracks V30. Yet homes still take 80 days to sell, the longest of the five cities, and Zillow forecasts another 2.1% decline over the next 12 months D2 V5.

City profiles

Houston: the biggest volume and the best yield. Houston has the most listings in Texas (35,898 in the metro) and the highest gross yield of the five (7.1%) D2 D5 D4. It also has the most bank repossessions of any large US metro (448 in August) D13. Net arrivals from other states have shrunk to 7,308 in 2025, down from 40,015 in 2023, and builders are pulling back: city single-family permits are down 16.5% this year V15 D12.

San Antonio: the most oversupplied, and the weakest rents. San Antonio has the most months of supply of the five (6.2), listings up 6.0% year over year, the steepest rent decline (−1.4%), a pending-sale cancellation rate of 17.8%, and the third-most REOs of any large metro D1 D2 D5 V21 D13. It is also the cheapest of the five, with a typical value of $247,453, and its gross yield is 6.7% D4.

Dallas: tight supply, but people are leaving. The Dallas division has 4.2 months of supply, and Dallas County's median list price is roughly flat (+0.2%) D3 D2. But the city's population shrank, and Dallas County had the largest domestic out-migration of the five counties (−46,226) D8 D10. In the suburbs, Collin County's typical value is 11.0% below its July 2022 peak, and some Collin ZIP codes are down 21–26% V4.

Austin: the deepest correction in large-metro America. Austin metro values are 26.8% below their June 2022 peak, the largest decline among the 100 biggest US metros V1. Travis County's median list price is down 9.3% year over year, homes take 80 days to sell, and the gross yield is only 3.8% D2 D4 D5. Austin metro rents are more than 20% below their 2022 peak V7. Buyers are returning: pending sales were up 17.0% year over year in the four weeks to July 5, 2026, the largest gain of any metro Redfin tracks V30.

Fort Worth: the strongest of the five. Fort Worth has the fastest population growth (+1.93%), the tightest supply (3.4 months in its division), Tarrant County listings down 4.1%, and the only rise in single-family permits (+8.9%) D8 D3 D2 D12. Even so, sellers there cut prices most often (27.6% of listings), and 18.1% of pending sales were cancelled, the second-highest rate in the US D2 V21.

What the numbers mean for investors and wholesalers

These are readings of the figures above, not forecasts.

  • The most distressed supply is in Houston, Dallas–Fort Worth and San Antonio. Those three led the nation's large metros in August REOs. Texas leads the country in foreclosure starts and has the shortest foreclosure timeline, so distressed properties move through the process here faster than in most states D13 D14.
  • Motivated sellers are easy to find. Between one listing in five and more than one in four carries a price cut, from 19.8% in Harris County to 27.6% in Tarrant County. Fort Worth and San Antonio also rank second and fourth in the country for cancelled contracts, so homes that were already under contract keep returning to the market D2 V21.
  • For buy-and-hold, Houston and San Antonio have the best gross yields and Austin the worst. Houston is at 7.1% and San Antonio at 6.7%; Austin is at 3.8%. With property taxes of 1.31–1.55% of value, an average insurance premium of $3,489 and mortgages at 7.28%, gross yield overstates the net return everywhere in Texas D4 D5 D15 D17 D7.
  • Rent growth won't rescue a thin deal. Rents are flat to negative in all five cities, against +2.5% nationally D5.
  • Austin is where prices have reset furthest, and its buyers are coming back. Values are 26.8% off the peak and pending sales were up 17% in early July. But Zillow still forecasts a further 2.1% decline, and its yield remains the lowest of the five V1 V30 V5.
  • Fort Worth has the strongest fundamentals: population, permits and tight supply. Even so, Tarrant County has the highest price-cut share of the five counties (27.6% of listings), and 18.1% of Fort Worth's pending sales were cancelled, the second-highest rate in the US D8 D12 D3.

Limits of this report

  • Mixed dates and areas. The figures run from May 2026 (Redfin) to September 2026 (Realtor.com), plus July 2025 for Census population. Some are for cities, some for counties, some for metros, and Redfin splits Dallas–Fort Worth into two divisions. The tables label each one.
  • Different price measures. List prices (Realtor.com), sale prices (Texas A&M, Redfin, NAR) and modelled typical values (Zillow) are not interchangeable.
  • Zillow revises its series every month, so figures quoted in the videos at the time may not match the current series.
  • Not found: investor share of purchases for any Texas metro (Redfin's quarterly table has none), Texas statewide rent, REOs for Austin and Fort Worth separately, and a Houston-specific insurance premium.
  • The gross yield is calculated here, not published by Zillow, and ignores taxes, insurance, vacancy and maintenance.
