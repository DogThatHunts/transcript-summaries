Verification Report · Deep Research

RE-V fact-check & market verification report

An adversarially verified fact-check of the load-bearing claims in the RE-V/ReVenture housing series and the other real-estate transcripts in the corpus, against primary sources (U.S. Census Bureau, BLS, BEA, MBA, NY Fed, SSA, Congress.gov, company SEC filings and earnings calls), plus an independent survey of 2025–2026 nationwide housing trends including evidence that cuts against the series' correction narrative. The original pass (September 21, 2026) was produced via a 108-agent deep-research workflow (5 search angles → 26 sources fetched → 122 claims extracted → 25 top claims verified by 3-vote adversarial panels) plus three targeted follow-up passes. Refreshed September 25, 2026: seventeen further claim groups, (m)–(ae), drawn from the 94 RE-V transcripts and the other channels added since, were verified by targeted research against the primary releases, and groups (e), (f) and (l) were re-checked against the latest MBA, Redfin and BEA data. Every verdict links to the numbered source table at the end. Data current through mid-September 2026 releases.

31 claim groups checked · 71 sources · September 2026

01

Scoreboard

Of the twelve claim groups checked, most of the RE-V series' core factual claims held up — often to the exact figure, including the entire D.R. Horton/DHI Mortgage block, verified against SEC filings and federal HMDA regulatory data. The failures are consistently of one kind: the series takes a real, verified number and stretches its historical framing ("worst since 2011-12" when the source says "widest spread since 2021"; "first in 35 years" when the Census says "first this decade"; a stock-of-delinquent-balances figure presented in a way that invites comparison with flow measures). The most surprising confirmation: the "21st Century ROAD to Housing Act," which sounded like the least plausible claim in the set, is real law — enacted July 11, 2026.

The September 25 refresh adds seventeen groups from the newer transcripts, and the pattern holds. Twelve of the seventeen confirm outright — often to the exact figure (May payrolls +172,000; Alphabet's $44.9 billion capex; Fitch's 6.3% private-credit default rate; Morgan Stanley's June 16 affordability paper; a 2032 Social Security depletion date). Two are refuted, and both are from the same non-RE-V video: the claim that Trump "killed" the housing bill (it became law on July 11) and that inflation is running at 4.1% (August CPI was 3.4%). The series' own characteristic error recurs in the new material as well: Freddie Mac's "more than 9 million" boomer homes becomes "nearly 10 million", and its explicit "tide, not tsunami" caveat is dropped.

Part 1 — Claim-by-claim verdicts

(a) D.R. Horton / DHI Mortgage PARTIAL CONFIRMED (5 of 6 sub-claims exact; one figure corrected)

The block the first pass couldn't verify turns out to be almost entirely accurate, confirmed against SEC filings and federal HMDA regulatory data:

  • 82% capture rate: CONFIRMED exactly. The fiscal Q3 2026 10-Q: DHI Mortgage handled financing for 82% of homes closed in the quarter (19,729 first-lien loans against 23,983 closings); 81% on a trailing-twelve-month basis per the investor presentation. The "~50% a decade ago" baseline also checks out — the FY2016 10-K reports capture rates of 55%/51%/50% for fiscal 2016/2015/2014. S41 S42 S43
  • 63% FHA/VA share: CONFIRMED exactly. Federal HMDA data (FFIEC/CFPB Data Browser, DHI Mortgage's LEI): calendar-2025 originations were 45.9% FHA + 17.2% VA = 63.1%. DHI Mortgage was the #2 FHA lender in America in 2025. S44 S45
  • FHA mix shift 24%→45%: CONFIRMED (2022 baseline is 25.4% by HMDA, trivially off the claimed 24%). Conventional fell 57.1% (2022) → 35.2% (2025), matching the claimed 58%→35% almost exactly; D.R. Horton's own Q3 FY2026 presentation confirms the "46% FHA loan mix" endpoint. S44 S42
  • 20% cancellation rate: CONFIRMED exactly — the Q3 FY2026 earnings release: 20% vs. 17% a year earlier; the CFO named buyer qualification as the biggest driver. S46 S47
  • ~570,000 lots: CONFIRMED — 568,500 at June 30, 2026 (22% owned, 78% optioned; ~6.7 years of supply), which management rounds to 570,000. S46 S47
  • 87,000 closings in 2025: PARTIALLY TRUE / OVERSTATED. Actual fiscal-2025 closings were 84,863 — the ~87,000 figure was D.R. Horton's earlier FY2026 guidance (86,000–87,500, issued April 2026), which the company subsequently cut to 83,800–84,300. "#1 builder by volume since 2002" is confirmed. S48 S37

One context figure the verification surfaced that sharpens the briefing's credit-risk point: 64-65% of DHI Mortgage's FY2026 closings were to first-time buyers, at an average LTV of 90% and average household income of $94,800. S42

(b) Lennar price cuts, costs, cycle time CONFIRMED

  • Average sales price: $371,000–$372,000 in Q2 2026 (vs. $389,000 a year earlier and a 2022 peak of ~$491,000 per SEC filings) — a −24.4% decline. The series claimed ~$370k vs ~$491k and "25–27%"; the dollar figures are exact, the percentage slightly overstated. Verified 3-0. S1 S2
  • Construction costs: down 13% over two years, to $81/sq ft (−7% YoY) per the earnings release and call — the series said 12%. Verified 3-0. S1 S2
  • Cycle time: a record-low 121 days in Q2 2026 (122 the prior quarter, 132 a year ago) — the series' 122-day figure matches the prior quarter exactly. Nuance: Lennar's metric is construction start-to-completion, not contract-to-close. Verified 3-0. S1 S2

(c) Builder months-of-supply at 9–10+ months CONFIRMED CONFIRMED (core figure)

Census/HUD's August 25, 2026 release puts new single-family months-of-supply at 9.6 months (July 2026, seasonally adjusted), with 2026 monthly readings ranging 8.5–9.9 — squarely supporting the series' "9–10+" claim. Verified 3-0. The companion claims (5.8-month long-run average; comparable only to 2008-09, 1981-82, 1973-74) were not independently verified — plausible but unchecked. Caveat: July is preliminary and the month-over-month change's confidence interval includes zero. S3

(d) New homes cheaper than existing homes PARTIAL CONFIRMED (direction); magnitude is time-dependent

The inversion is real and NAHB itself calls it a reversal of the historical pattern: existing-home median prices have exceeded new-home medians for four consecutive quarters through Q1 2026 (six of the past eight), against a 2010–2019 norm of a ~$66,000 new-home premium. July 2026 monthly data: new median $393,800 vs. NAR existing median $434,100 — a ~9.3% gap. But the series' fixed "~5.5%" figure was refuted as a point estimate (the Q1 2026 quarterly gap was only ~0.3%): cite the inversion, not a fixed percentage. The inversion is also partly compositional — builders are delivering smaller homes with a Southern mix skew. Inversion verified 3-0; the 5.5% figure voted down 1-2. S3 S4 S5

(e) FHA delinquency "11.9–15.2%, highest since 2011-12" PARTIAL PARTIALLY TRUE / OVERSTATED

  • The 11.9% end is exact: MBA's Q1 2026 National Delinquency Survey shows the FHA seasonally adjusted delinquency rate at 11.88% (+126 bps YoY). The 15.2% end is wrong for the present — the recent FHA high was 15.7% in Q2 2020 (pandemic), not now.
  • The historical framing is overstated: MBA's own characterization is the widest FHA-vs-conventional spread since 2021, not the highest delinquency since 2011-12.
  • The strongest counter-nuance: conventional delinquency actually fell 14 bps to 2.75%, with overall 1-4-unit delinquency at 4.44%. Distress is concentrated in government-backed loans, not broad-based.
  • The 44%-DTI-above-2006-peak sub-claim remains unverified. Verified 3-0 (as stated here). S6 S7

Re-checked 2026-09-25 against MBA's Q2 2026 National Delinquency Survey: the FHA delinquency rate eased 9 bps to 11.79% (overall delinquency 4.37%, −7 bps q/q but +44 bps y/y), while FHA serious delinquencies rose more than 225 bps year over year — the core '11.9%' figure and the concentration-in-FHA reading both hold. S68

(f) Investor purchases "down ~50% from 2022 peak" PARTIAL PARTIALLY TRUE / OVERSTATED

Redfin's Q1 2026 investor report confirms the direction — purchases fell 6% YoY to the lowest level since 2020 — but publishes no comparison to the 2022 peak, and investor market share barely moved (19% vs. 20% a year earlier). A counter-datapoint cuts directly against the "Wall Street net-selling exodus" framing: investor-owned homes were just 7.8% of U.S. listings in Q1 2026, the smallest share in five years — shrinking, not growing, listing exposure. The cap-rate-below-debt-cost sub-claim was not verified. Verified 3-0 on the pullback; the exodus framing weakened 1-2. S10

Re-checked 2026-09-25: Redfin had published no Q2 2026 investor report as of this date — the Q1 2026 edition (May 28) remains the latest — so the verdict stands unchanged. S70

(g) The "21st Century ROAD to Housing Act" CONFIRMED CONFIRMED, and it's now law

The least-plausible-sounding claim in the series checks out almost entirely:

  • H.R. 6644, 119th Congress — the "21st Century ROAD to Housing Act" — is real. Title X prohibits for-profit entities with direct or indirect control of 350+ single-family homes from purchasing more, with civil penalties up to $1 million per violation or 3× purchase price (whichever is greater), effective ~January 7, 2027 and sunsetting 15 years later. S18 S19 S20
  • Vote counts match: Senate 85-5 (June 22, 2026), House 358-32 (June 23, 2026). The series' alternate 89-5/89-10 figures were wrong for final passage.
  • Carve-outs confirmed: newly constructed homes, build-to-rent programs, renovated/rehabilitated properties, lease-to-own, 55+ senior housing, investor-to-investor sales — validating the homebuilder briefing's point that the law structurally favors build-to-rent construction over buying existing stock.
  • Status update the series didn't have: it became law on July 11, 2026 without the President's signature (neither signed nor timely vetoed). S19 S21

  • Invitation Homes acquired ResiBuilt Homes, LLC — announced January 16, 2026, ~$89M plus up to $7.5M earn-out, confirmed in its FY2025 10-K. The framing matters: INVH presents it as expansion into homebuilding/BTR ("increase housing supply through new construction"), which cuts against a simple "Wall Street exiting housing" narrative. Verified 3-0. S14 S15 S16 S17
  • American Homes 4 Rent (AMH) is a top-40 builder: #39 on Builder Magazine's 2024 Builder 100 (2,317 homes completed in 2023), named a 2025 Top U.S. Homebuilder in its own investor materials. The "2,000+ in the last year" figure aligns with 2025 delivery guidance (2,200–2,400) but the final actual wasn't verified — and 2026 guidance drops to 1,700–2,100, so the claim is borderline going forward. S31 S32 S33

(i) Migration claims PARTIAL MIXED

  • Florida: CONFIRMED. Net domestic migration collapsed from 310,892 (2022) to 22,517 (2025) — −92.8%, matching the series' "~90% down from peak." S11 S13
  • California: CONFIRMED exactly. −229,077 net domestic migrants (July 2024–July 2025), the largest state loss in the country (New York second at −137,586), per Census Vintage 2025 estimates. S12

(j) Austin and Cape Coral price declines CONFIRMED CONFIRMED (Austin arguably understated)

  • Austin: down −23.8% on MLS median sold price ($538,000 May-2022 peak → $410,000), −26.4% on price per sq ft, and −27.2% below peak on Zillow's metro-level ZHVI per ResiClub — bracketing the series' 24-26% and arguably exceeding it. The series' specific "$553k → $420k" pairing matches no published source (a paraphrase). S26 S27
  • Cape Coral/Fort Myers: down ~19.3% from the April 2022 peak ($437,000 → ~$352-375,000), within the claimed 15-20%. (k) Mortgages above 6% now outnumber those below 3% CONFIRMED

Realtor.com (January 14, 2026, using FHFA National Mortgage Database Q3 2025 data): 21.2% of outstanding mortgages above 6% vs. 20.0% below 3% — the first such crossover, which the release itself calls "a turning point in the rate-lock-in era." The series' claim is exact. S22

(l) Savings rate and credit cards PARTIAL CONFIRMED FIGURES, ONE CONFLATION

  • Savings rate: figures confirmed. BEA 2026 monthly readings: 3.6% (Mar), 2.6% (Apr), 3.0% (May), 2.7% (Jun), 3.0% (Jul) — the April reading was the lowest since March 2008. "Near record low" is a mild overstatement (the record is 1.4%, July 2005); "lowest since 2008" is the accurate framing. S23 S24 S25
  • Credit cards: the 13% figure conflates two metrics. The NY Fed's flow into serious delinquency was 6.97% in Q2 2026 — essentially flat YoY (6.93%). The ~13% figure is the stock of balances 90+ days delinquent (~12.9%) — a different measure the NY Fed published a Liberty Street Economics post specifically to disambiguate. Comparing today's stock against 2008-09 flow-based narratives overstates the deterioration. S8 S9

Re-checked 2026-09-25 against BEA's July 2026 release: the personal saving rate was 3.0% in July (release of August 26, 2026), consistent with the readings above; the August figure had not yet been published. S69

(m) Existing-home sales at roughly 4 million annualised CONFIRMED CONFIRMED (core figure)

The May 2026 print is exact: NAR reported existing-home sales up 3.2% to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 4.17 million, the highest since December 2025, with 1.55 million units of inventory (4.5 months' supply) and a median price of $429,300. (o) Freddie Mac: 'nearly 10 million' boomer homes over the next decade PARTIAL PARTIALLY TRUE / OVERSTATED

Freddie Mac's research (February 2024) put the figure at 'more than 9 million' homes coming to market over the decade as boomers downsize — and explicitly rejected the 'silver tsunami' framing: 'the tsunami is more like a tide, bringing a gradual exit that will mostly be offset by new entrants', with a net decline of ~300,000 boomer homeowner households in the first year accelerating thereafter. (p) May 2026 payrolls +172,000 CONFIRMED

Exact. BLS reported total nonfarm payroll employment up 172,000 in May 2026 (April: 179,000), far above the ~80,000 consensus, with unemployment steady at 4.3% and gains led by leisure and hospitality, local government and health care; financial-activities employment fell 22,000. S53 The series' claim that the print triggered a 4.2% NASDAQ decline was not verified.

(q) JOLTS: hires fell below 5.1 million in July PARTIAL CONFIRMED (figure); framing overstated

Hires slipped to 5.1 million in July 2026 from 5.3 million in June, and the hires rate fell from 3.4% to 3.2% — but BLS/Indeed characterise that as the weakest hiring month since February, not 'among the weakest of the entire cycle'. Openings were little changed at 7.3 million and layoffs fell to a six-month low of 1.7 million: a 'low fire, low hire' market. S54

(r) Social Security's retirement fund runs dry in 2031/2032 CONFIRMED CONFIRMED (2032; one video said 2031)

The 2026 Trustees Report projects the OASI trust fund depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032, one year earlier than the prior report (the 2025 tax changes lowered beneficiaries' tax liability), after which continuing income covers ~78% of scheduled benefits; combining the funds would push depletion to 2034 but requires an act of Congress. (w) Morgan Stanley: affordability will not return to pre-2022 levels CONFIRMED

Part 2 — What the independent survey found, including the counter-case

The strongest structural finding of the whole exercise: the distress is real but concentrated, not systemic. The verified picture through August 2026:

  • New-home demand is genuinely weak: July 2026 sales at a 607,000 SAAR, −10.5% MoM and −6.3% YoY — though Census's own confidence intervals include zero on both changes, and it notes ~4 months are needed to establish a trend. S3
  • But overall consumer credit is stable: the NY Fed's official characterization is that "delinquency rates across most products have held steady over the past two years." Aggregate delinquency is ~4.7% of outstanding debt; the mortgage serious-delinquency flow is 1.52% (up from 1.29%) — far below the 5%+ flows of 2008–2010. Auto and card delinquencies are "elevated but steady." S8
  • Conventional mortgages are improving, not deteriorating: delinquency fell to 2.75% even as FHA rose — the bifurcation is between borrower cohorts, not a general credit cycle. S6
  • The investor "exodus" is mostly a purchase slowdown, not a liquidation: purchases at the lowest level since 2020, but share nearly flat and listing exposure at a five-year low — while the largest institutional landlord is buying a homebuilder to add supply. S10 S14
  • Named stabilization views exist: NAHB's 2026 outlook (chief economist Robert Dietz, February 2026) forecasts "cautious optimism and incremental gains" — single-family starts +1.0% to 940,000, mortgage rates slightly above 6% trending lower, inventory normalizing toward a balanced ~4.6 months. Goldman Sachs' Jan Hatzius has argued the affordability-strained/no-crash view (depressed sales through 2026, recovery ~2027, modest positive price growth) — though that forecast dates to December 2023 and should be cited as such. Fannie Mae's 2026 outlook similarly projects moderate growth, not a crash. S34 S35 S36 S39 S40

September 2026 update. The macro backdrop moved against the series' rate-relief thesis and toward its credit-stress thesis at the same time: the Fed raised rates on September 16 for the first time since 2023, with a majority of officials signalling another hike S63, even as August CPI came in at 3.4% (core 2.4%) S64; JOLTS hiring slipped to 5.1 million in a "low fire, low hire" market S54; and Fitch's private-credit default rate set a record at 6.3% S62. Existing-home inventory reached 4.9 months' supply, the highest in over a decade S71 — the clearest supply-side confirmation of the series' overhang narrative since the original pass.

Bottom line: the RE-V series is substantially accurate on its numbers — Lennar's cuts, the supply overhang, the price inversion, the migration collapse, the rate crossover, and even its most implausible-sounding legislative claim all verify against primary sources. Where it errs, it errs directionally the same way every time: real data, stretched historical framing. And the counter-evidence it underweights is specific: conventional credit is fine, the consumer picture is "steady at elevated," and the institutional money it portrays as fleeing is partly redeploying into building. A reader should treat the series as a reliable aggregator of real data with a consistent bearish editorial thumb on the historical-comparison scale.
