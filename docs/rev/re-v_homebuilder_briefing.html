01

Executive summary

The two largest builders are running the housing market's most aggressive discount-and-finance operation in at least a decade, and it is working well enough to keep sales volume near normal even as list prices fall — but it's doing so by leaning harder on government-backed, low-down-payment financing than at any point since the run-up to 2008. D.R. Horton and Lennar together account for roughly a quarter of new-home production; both have cut net prices 12–27% from 2022 peaks, and for the first time in modern data, the median new-build now sells for less than the median existing home. That price discipline is buying volume at the cost of margin and mortgage-book risk: D.R. Horton's captive lender now finances 82% of its own closings (up from ~50% a decade ago), 63% of them FHA or VA, and FHA delinquency nationally is running at 2011-12 levels. Builder lot supply sits at 9–10+ months against a 5.8-month long-run average — a level seen only three other times in 60+ years (2008-09, 1981-82, 1973-74) — and that overhang is concentrated overwhelmingly in the Sun Belt, while the Northeast and Midwest remain structurally under-supplied and still appreciating. Meanwhile institutional single-family landlords (Invitation Homes, American Homes for Rent) are themselves becoming builders even as their home-buying slows, and a federal ban on large-scale investor home-buying — the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, since verified as enacted law (July 11, 2026) — carries build-to-rent carve-outs that matter directly to any builder with a rental pipeline, effective January 2027. The core claims of this briefing were subsequently verified against SEC filings, federal HMDA data, and Census/MBA/NY Fed releases; see the fact-check & verification report for claim-by-claim verdicts and the linked source table.

02

D.R. Horton — the financing engine, and its risk

D.R. Horton is the #1 U.S. builder by volume and the most aggressive on both price and financing. ✓ Fact-check: the series' "87,000 closings in 2025" figure was earlier company guidance, later cut — actual fiscal-2025 closings were 84,863; the "#1 by volume since 2002" claim is confirmed. Everything else in this block verified against the fiscal Q3 2026 10-Q and federal HMDA data — the 82% capture rate, 63% FHA/VA share, 20% cancellation rate, and ~570,000 lots (568,500) are all exact (verification report).

  • Price: net selling prices cut 12% from peak (row 1319); site-level examples ran as low as $107–$129/sq ft in Georgia and Texas (rows 1333, 1359) — well under the National Association of Home Builders' cited national replacement-cost benchmark of $162/sq ft (row 1359).
  • Financing capture: DHI Mortgage's capture rate — the share of D.R. Horton buyers financed through the builder's own captive lender — hit 82% in Q3 2026, up from roughly 50% a decade ago (row 1319). That's a direct margin lever (captured origination revenue) but also a concentration risk: the builder now owns the credit outcome of the large majority of its own sales.
  • Loan mix shift: FHA/VA share of DHI Mortgage originations rose from 24% (June 2022) to 45%, while conventional share fell from 58% to 35% over the same period (row 1319). 63% of DHI Mortgage-financed purchases are now FHA or VA — 0–5%-down products.
  • The resulting book quality signal: FHA delinquency nationally reached 11.9–15.2% across various 2026 readings — the highest since 2011-12 excluding the pandemic spike, and roughly 5x conventional (Fannie/Freddie) delinquency (rows 1319, 1382). Debt-to-income on government-backed originations reached 44%, above the 2006-07 bubble's 39% peak (row 1319). ✓ Fact-check: MBA's Q1 2026 data confirms only the low end — 11.88% — and frames it as the widest FHA-vs-conventional spread since 2021, not the highest since 2011-12; conventional delinquency actually fell to 2.75%, meaning the stress is concentrated in the government-backed cohort rather than broad-based. One verified figure that sharpens the risk point: 64-65% of DHI Mortgage's FY2026 closings were first-time buyers at an average 90% LTV.
  • Demand-side stress: contract cancellation rates as high as 20% cited on D.R. Horton's own earnings disclosures (row 1319); the company lowered revenue guidance in the same period.
  • Land bank: D.R. Horton controls over 570,000 buildable lots (owned + option) against roughly 80,000 annual closings — a multi-year pipeline the host flags as a standing overhang regardless of near-term sales pace (row 1366).

Read for other builders: D.R. Horton's playbook — cut price, hold volume via aggressive in-house FHA/VA financing — is a strategy other volume builders can copy, but it transfers pricing risk into credit risk on the builder's own balance sheet (or its captive lender's), at a moment when that exact loan cohort (FHA, 2022-2026 vintage) is the one showing the fastest-rising delinquency in the market.

Lennar — the price-cut leader, now the cheapest in eight years

Lennar (#2 U.S. builder, 82,000 closings in 2025) has cut prices even more aggressively than D.R. Horton on a percentage basis, and has been explicit about it on earnings calls. ✓ Fact-check: this block verified almost exactly against Lennar's Q2 2026 earnings release and call — average sales price $371,000 (a −24.4% decline from the $491,000 peak, slightly under the series' 25-27% framing), construction costs down 13% over two years to $81/sq ft, and a record-low 121-day cycle time (122 the prior quarter).

  • Net price cuts: 25–27% off the 2022 peak across multiple readings in the series — from $491,000 (2022) to $370,000 by 2026 (rows 1319, 1333, 1359), the cheapest level Lennar has sold at in eight years (pre-pandemic pricing).
  • Guidance: Lennar cut its full-year 2026 delivery guidance mid-year, citing "stubborn housing market headwinds" (row 1333) and "high interest rates and geopolitical uncertainty" in a separate reading (row 1333) — the second major builder/real-estate company to downgrade guidance in the same month as Zillow.
  • Construction-cost relief: Lennar's CEO stated on an earnings call that construction costs are down 12% from peak, and average delivery time has compressed to 122 days (row 1359) — i.e., some of the margin lost to price cuts is being clawed back on the cost side, not just absorbed.
  • Financing incentives: a "summer value days" promotion offering a 5-year fixed 3.4% rate (5/1 ARM, FHA, 3.5% down) on $150,000–$200,000 homes, adjusting after year six (row 1333) — a rate buydown strategy that defers the borrower's rate-reset risk rather than eliminating it.
  • Geographic examples of the discount: San Antonio Lennar product at $140,000–$148,000, cut as much as 25% (row 1329); a 1,400 sq ft/3bd/2bd unit cut from $197,000 to $148,000 ($107/sq ft) (row 1333).

Read for other builders: Lennar's combination of price cuts plus falling construction costs plus compressed delivery time is the more defensible version of the D.R. Horton playbook — it's absorbing margin pressure through operational efficiency rather than relying as heavily on financing-arm risk-taking. The 3.4%-rate buydown product is worth watching as a template, but it pushes payment-shock risk out to loan year six rather than resolving it.

04

The historic crossover: new homes now cheaper than existing homes

The single most structurally significant data point in the series for builders: as of March 2026, new-build homes were 5.5% cheaper than existing homes — the U.S. Census Bureau data the host cites shows this has essentially never happened before (new builds normally carry a ~15% premium over existing homes) (row 1344).

  • Median new-build sale price fell from $460,000 (October 2022 peak) to $387,000–$392,000 by 2026 — a 14–16% cut, tracked consistently across rows 1344, 1359, 1366.
  • The prior comparable downturn (2007-2010) saw builders cut prices 22% — this cycle's 14–16% cut is smaller in magnitude but is happening against a backdrop of existing homeowners who are not cutting price nearly as fast (the "lock-in effect" — see below), which is what's producing the crossover.
  • South-region homebuilders held nearly 300,000 homes for sale at the end of 2025 — the highest builder inventory level ever recorded in the South, exceeding the 2006 bubble peak (row 1366).
  • The NAHB's chief economist is quoted (via the host) expecting resale prices to "falter" in much of the country in 2026 as existing-home sellers are forced to compete with builder pricing (row 1366) — i.e., builders are now the price-setters pulling the broader market down, not the other way around.

Read for builders: this crossover is a double-edged data point. It validates the price-cut strategy as working — new-build sales volume has held up better than existing-home volume across the series — but it also signals builders are now competing on price with a resale market that is comparatively slow to reprice, meaning further cuts may be needed to maintain the gap as existing sellers eventually capitulate.

Builder lot supply — the scale of the overhang, region by region

Builder months-of-supply readings recur across the series at 9–10.3 months against a 5.8-month long-run average (rows 1319, 1329, 1333, 1359) — a level the host repeatedly notes has only occurred three other times in 60+ years: the 2008-09 GFC, the 1981-82 double-dip (18% mortgage rates), and the 1973-74 oil-shock recession.

  • Not-yet-started/permitted-home supply reached 21 months in one Census Bureau reading (row 1329) — the deepest part of the pipeline, implying the overhang is not a near-term liquidation problem but a multi-year one if demand doesn't recover.
  • Homes under construction: ~15 months of supply (~259,000 homes); completed, unsold homes: 3.9 months of supply (~115,000 homes) (row 1329) — the completed-and-unsold figure is the most immediately price-sensitive category.
  • Regional concentration: building-permit data by state (trailing 12 months) puts Texas #1 (200,000 permits), Florida #2 (168,000), well ahead of California (104,000) and New York (39,000) (row 1333) — both Texas and Florida permitting has been drifting down from 2022-2023 peaks but remains structurally elevated relative to housing stock. North Carolina, Georgia, Arizona, South Carolina, and Tennessee also rank high.
  • The demand side of that supply: Austin (-23 to -26% from peak), Cape Coral/Fort Myers (-15 to -20%), and Punta Gorda (-20 to -27%) are the most severely corrected metros in the series — all high-permit Sun Belt markets that built into a demand air-pocket.

Site-selection read: the series' clearest counter-signal is the Northeast/Midwest supply shortage — inventory down 25-70% from normal in Connecticut, New Jersey, and Illinois, with the fastest home-value appreciation in the country concentrated in Rockford IL (+32%), Syracuse NY (+31%), and a cluster of Wisconsin/Connecticut/upstate-New-York metros (row 1351). Builders have "largely ignored" these markets during the pandemic build-out in favor of Florida/Texas/Georgia/Arizona, per the host's own read (row 1348) — the RE-V series treats this as the more underbuilt, less-competed opportunity relative to the saturated Sun Belt.

Product-mix and price-point signals

  • Smaller floor plans, working-class price points: D.R. Horton and Lennar are both cited as the most aggressive on price cuts and on shrinking floor plans, explicitly targeting blue-collar/working-class buyers (row 1333) — median new-build size has drifted down from a 2015 peak of ~2,500 sq ft to ~2,100 sq ft by 2025 (row 1333), still well above the 1950s average of 900-1,100 sq ft cited via a Zillow-sourced figure.
  • Luxury/higher-income builders have not cut price — Toll Brothers (average selling price ~$960,000) and Pulte (~$566,000, down only slightly from 2024) are both cited as holding or raising prices, concentrated in the Northeast/Midwest and higher-income Southern submarkets that are not seeing the correction (row 1333).
  • KB Home reported a 25% YoY revenue drop in its most recent quarterly earnings cited in the series (row 1329) — the sharpest builder revenue decline named in the transcripts, though without the same level of price/volume detail given to D.R. Horton and Lennar.
  • NVR is named as the #4 builder by volume (29,000 closings) without further detail (row 1319).
  • Rate-buydown incentives as low as 3.99% are cited as a general builder tactic beyond Lennar's specific 3.4% product (row 1329), suggesting the financing-incentive route (vs. sticker-price cuts) is now a standard tool across the builder cohort, not just the two largest players.

Read for builders: the market is bifurcating by price point in the same way it's bifurcating by region — entry-level/working-class product in oversupplied Sun Belt markets is where the price war is concentrated; luxury and Northeast/Midwest product has pricing power. A builder's optimal move depends heavily on which side of that line its existing land position sits.

Institutional landlords are becoming builders — and are now selling

The series documents single-family rental REITs moving directly into homebuilding, which changes the competitive landscape for entry-level/rental-adjacent product specifically.

  • American Homes for Rent (AMH), the 3rd-largest Wall Street landlord, completed construction of 2,000+ homes in the prior year and is now ranked a top-40 U.S. homebuilder in its own right (row 1518) — a build-to-rent operator competing directly with traditional builders for lots, labor, and materials in the same submarkets.
  • Invitation Homes, the largest Wall Street landlord (85,000-90,000 homes), purchased homebuilder ResiBuilt in 2026 (row 1518) — vertically integrating build-to-rent supply rather than buying existing stock.
  • But the broader institutional-investor cohort is now net-selling: Wall Street landlords' homes-for-sale listings doubled over five months in 2026 per CNBC, and Wall Street was net-selling more homes than it bought year-to-date 2026 (row 1518). Investor purchases overall are down ~50% from the 2022 peak nationally, and down 58-77% in the hardest-hit metros (Jacksonville, Atlanta, Charlotte, Orlando, Nashville) per Redfin (rows 1336, 1518). ✓ Fact-check: Redfin's own Q1 2026 report supports a purchase slowdown (−6% YoY, lowest since 2020) but publishes no ~50%-from-peak comparison, and two datapoints cut against an "exodus" framing: investor market share was nearly flat (19% vs. 20% a year earlier) and investor-owned homes were just 7.8% of listings — a five-year low, i.e. shrinking rather than growing listing exposure. Read it as institutional demand withdrawal, not liquidation.
  • Why: single-family rental cap rates (~4.8%) now sit below mortgage/debt costs — an inversion of the 2010-2021 relationship that made buy-to-rent profitable, turning existing rental holdings into "money losers" per the host's framing (row 1518).

Policy risk to watch — now RESOLVED and verified: the "21st Century ROAD to Housing Act" (H.R. 6644, 119th Congress) is real and became law on July 11, 2026 without the President's signature (neither signed nor timely vetoed) — the series tracked its signing status across several videos without resolution (rows 1326, 1336, 1339, 1360, 1361, 1518), and the fact-check closed the loop against Congress.gov and legal analyses. Verified provisions: entities with direct or indirect control of 350+ single-family homes are barred from purchasing more, effective ~January 7, 2027, sunsetting after 15 years, with civil penalties up to $1M per violation or 3x purchase price; final votes were Senate 85-5 (June 22, 2026) and House 358-32 (June 23, 2026). The confirmed carve-outs — newly constructed homes, build-to-rent programs, and renovated/rehabilitated property — directly benefit build-to-rent operators like AMH and Invitation Homes/ResiBuilt over investors buying existing stock. With enactment confirmed, any builder with (or considering) a rental-portfolio arm should treat the January 2027 effective date as a planning deadline, not a hypothetical (verification report).

Read for builders: the institutional-landlord pullback removes a bulk buyer that historically absorbed entry-level product in Sun Belt metros — one more source of demand softness builders are competing into. At the same time, the AMH/Invitation Homes build-to-rent pivot, combined with the now-enacted investor ban's new-construction carve-out, signals build-to-rent as a growth product category specifically exempted from the regulatory headwind on the buy-existing side.

Demand-side signals builders should watch

  • Mortgage rate-lock-in: a large share of existing owners hold sub-3-4% rates (as high as 27-30% of California mortgage holders) and are reluctant to sell — this is part of why new-build has been able to undercut existing-home pricing structurally: existing sellers are slower to reprice because they have less urgency (rows 1332, 1344, 1350, 1358, 1360).
  • The rate distribution has flipped: the share of U.S. mortgages above 6% now exceeds the share below 3% nationally, concentrated especially in Alabama, Arkansas, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Kentucky, Georgia, Florida, and Texas (rows 1332, 1344, 1350, 1358, 1360) — these are the same states carrying the heaviest builder lot-supply overhang, suggesting rate-driven affordability stress and oversupply are compounding in the same geography rather than offsetting.
  • AI CapEx as a regional demand wildcard: San Francisco is repeatedly cited as the one major West Coast market still appreciating (rows 1330, 1339, 1350), attributed directly to AI-company concentration (Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI) — a reminder that tech-driven local income growth can locally override the national correction, worth tracking in any market with a comparable employer concentration.
  • Buyer sentiment as a leading indicator: a Redfin survey cited in the series found 59-60% of Americans believe AI advances will hurt job prospects and housing affordability (rows 1356, 1357) — a demand-confidence headwind independent of rates or price.
09

Bottom line for a builder audience

  1. The price-cut-plus-captive-financing playbook (D.R. Horton) is winning volume but concentrating credit risk in FHA/VA paper that is already delinquent at 2011-12-comparable rates — a strategy other builders can emulate on the pricing side, but one that requires either genuine underwriting discipline or a captive lender willing to hold that risk.
  2. Lennar's cost-and-cycle-time compression is the more durable margin lever — construction costs down 12%, delivery time to 122 days — worth benchmarking internally regardless of whether a builder matches Lennar's headline price cuts.
  3. The new-build-cheaper-than-existing crossover is structural, not temporary, driven by the rate-lock-in effect suppressing existing-home repricing — builders should expect this pricing advantage (and the pressure to defend it with further cuts) to persist as long as existing owners stay locked into sub-4% mortgages.
  4. Site selection should weight the Northeast/Midwest supply shortage against the saturated, still-correcting Sun Belt — the same states (FL, TX, TN, GA, AL, AR, OK, KY) carrying both the heaviest lot overhang and the worst rate-affordability mix are the ones to underweight for new land positions.
  5. Build-to-rent is emerging as a distinct, regulation-favored category — AMH and Invitation Homes are moving from landlord to builder specifically because new construction is carved out of the institutional-investor ban now in force (effective January 2027), a structural tailwind for any builder positioned to sell into that channel even as the broader buy-existing investor pool shrinks.
