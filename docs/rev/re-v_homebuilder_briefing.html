01

Executive summary

The two largest builders are running the housing market's most aggressive discount-and-finance operation in at least a decade, and it is still working well enough to keep volume near normal even as list prices fall — but the second half of 2026 has made the cost of that strategy visible. D.R. Horton and Lennar together account for roughly a quarter of new-home production; both have cut net prices 12–25% from 2022 peaks, both cut their 2026 forecasts (row 1644), and by July 2026 the median new build sold for $394,000 against $434,000 for the median existing home — a $40,000 discount where a 17% premium was the 25-year norm (rows 1774, 2420). That price discipline is buying volume at the cost of margin and mortgage-book risk: D.R. Horton's captive lender finances 82% of its own closings, 63% of them FHA or VA, its cancellation rate has risen to 20% from 17% a year earlier (rows 1319, 1774), and FHA delinquency nationally is holding near 11.8–11.9% while conventional delinquency falls. Builder lot supply sits at 9.2–9.6 months against a 5.8-month long-run average (rows 1319, 1621) — a level seen only three other times in 60+ years — and the overhang has now spread to the resale market, where existing-home supply reached 4.9 months in August, the highest in over a decade (row 2419). The overhang remains concentrated overwhelmingly in the Sun Belt, and the newest transcripts add a second-tier casualty list — Bentonville, Memphis, Nashville, Raleigh, Las Vegas, Southwest Florida — while the Northeast and Midwest remain structurally under-supplied and still appreciating. Institutional single-family landlords (Invitation Homes, American Homes for Rent) are themselves becoming builders even as their buying slows, and the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act — verified as enacted law on July 11, 2026, despite a September video claiming it was killed — carries build-to-rent carve-outs that matter to any builder with a rental pipeline, effective January 2027. The macro backdrop moved against rate relief: the Fed raised rates on September 16 and Morgan Stanley's June analysis finds affordability does not return to pre-2022 levels under any rate path it modelled. Claim-by-claim verdicts and the linked source table are in the fact-check & verification report.

02

D.R. Horton — the financing engine, and its risk

D.R. Horton is the #1 U.S. builder by volume and the most aggressive on both price and financing. ✓ Fact-check: group (a) — the series' "87,000 closings in 2025" figure was earlier company guidance, later cut; actual fiscal-2025 closings were 84,863, and "#1 by volume since 2002" is confirmed. Everything else in this block verified against the fiscal Q3 2026 10-Q and federal HMDA data: the 82% capture rate, 63% FHA/VA share, 20% cancellation rate and ~570,000 lots (568,500) are all exact (verification report).

  • Price: net selling prices cut 12% from peak (row 1319); site-level examples ran as low as $107–$129/sq ft in Georgia and Texas (rows 1333, 1359) — well under the National Association of Home Builders' cited national replacement-cost benchmark of $162/sq ft (row 1359).
  • Financing capture: DHI Mortgage's capture rate — the share of D.R. Horton buyers financed through the builder's own captive lender — hit 82% in Q3 2026, up from roughly 50% a decade ago (row 1319). That's a direct margin lever (captured origination revenue) but also a concentration risk: the builder now owns the credit outcome of the large majority of its own sales.
  • Loan mix shift: FHA/VA share of DHI Mortgage originations rose from 24% (June 2022) to 45%, while conventional share fell from 58% to 35% over the same period (row 1319). 63% of DHI Mortgage-financed purchases are now FHA or VA — 0–5%-down products.
  • The resulting book quality signal: FHA delinquency nationally reached 11.9–15.2% across various 2026 readings — the highest since 2011-12 excluding the pandemic spike, and roughly 5x conventional delinquency (rows 1319, 1382). Debt-to-income on government-backed originations reached 44%, above the 2006-07 bubble's 39% peak (row 1319). ✓ Fact-check: group (e), re-checked September 25 — MBA's Q1 2026 survey confirmed only the low end (11.88%) and framed it as the widest FHA-vs-conventional spread since 2021, not the highest since 2011-12; the Q2 2026 survey eased FHA delinquency to 11.79% while FHA serious delinquencies rose more than 225 bps year over year and conventional delinquency kept falling. The stress is concentrated in exactly the cohort D.R. Horton's captive lender is writing. One verified figure that sharpens the point: 64–65% of DHI Mortgage's FY2026 closings were first-time buyers at an average 90% LTV.
  • Demand-side stress: contract cancellations rose to 20% from 17% a year earlier on the company's own disclosures (rows 1319, 1774); the company lowered revenue guidance in the same period, and later readings have both D.R. Horton and Lennar cutting their 2026 forecasts (row 1644).
  • Resale losses on its own product: the newer transcripts track D.R. Horton homes reselling well below their 2022 prices — a Davenport, Florida home bought for $331,000 in March 2022 listed at $200,000 (row 1319), a $394,000 purchase foreclosed and relisted at $270,000 (row 1342), an Ocala short sale from $365,000 to $227,000 (row 1362). Each such comp reprices the builder's remaining inventory in that subdivision.
  • Land bank: D.R. Horton controls over 570,000 buildable lots (owned + option) against roughly 80,000 annual closings — a multi-year pipeline the host flags as a standing overhang regardless of near-term sales pace (row 1366).

Read for other builders: D.R. Horton's playbook — cut price, hold volume via aggressive in-house FHA/VA financing — is a strategy other volume builders can copy, but it transfers pricing risk into credit risk on the builder's own balance sheet (or its captive lender's), at a moment when that exact loan cohort (FHA, 2022–2026 vintage) is the one showing rising serious delinquency while everything else improves. The rising cancellation rate says the marginal buyer is now failing qualification, not changing their mind.

03

Lennar — the price-cut leader, now the cheapest in eight years

Lennar (#2 U.S. builder, 82,000 closings in 2025) has cut prices even more aggressively than D.R. Horton on a percentage basis, and has been explicit about it on earnings calls. ✓ Fact-check: group (b) — this block verified almost exactly against Lennar's Q2 2026 earnings release and call: average sales price $371,000 (a −24.4% decline from the $491,000 peak, slightly under the series' 25–27% framing), construction costs down 13% over two years to $81/sq ft, and a record-low 121-day cycle time (122 the prior quarter).

  • Net price cuts: 25–27% off the 2022 peak across multiple readings in the series — from $491,000 (2022) to $370,000 by 2026 (rows 1319, 1333, 1359), the cheapest level Lennar has sold at in eight years (pre-pandemic pricing).
  • Guidance: Lennar cut its full-year 2026 delivery guidance mid-year, citing "stubborn housing market headwinds" and "high interest rates and geopolitical uncertainty" (row 1333) — the second major builder/real-estate company to downgrade guidance in the same month as Zillow.
  • Construction-cost relief: Lennar's CEO stated on an earnings call that construction costs are down 12% from peak, and average delivery time has compressed to 122 days (row 1359) — i.e., some of the margin lost to price cuts is being clawed back on the cost side, not just absorbed.
  • Financing incentives: a "summer value days" promotion offering a 5-year fixed 3.4% rate (5/1 ARM, FHA, 3.5% down) on $150,000–$200,000 homes, adjusting after year six (row 1333) — a rate buydown strategy that defers the borrower's rate-reset risk rather than eliminating it.
  • Geographic examples of the discount: San Antonio Lennar product at $140,000–$148,000, cut as much as 25% (row 1329); a 1,400 sq ft/3bd/2bd unit cut from $197,000 to $148,000 ($107/sq ft) (row 1333).

Read for other builders: Lennar's combination of price cuts plus falling construction costs plus compressed delivery time is the more defensible version of the D.R. Horton playbook — it's absorbing margin pressure through operational efficiency rather than relying as heavily on financing-arm risk-taking. The 3.4%-rate buydown product is worth watching as a template, but it pushes payment-shock risk out to loan year six rather than resolving it — and with the Fed now hiking rather than cutting (row 2563), the reset it defers has become more expensive, not less.

04

The historic crossover: new homes now cheaper than existing homes

The single most structurally significant data point in the corpus for builders, and one the newer transcripts have watched widen: as of March 2026 new builds were cited as 5.5% cheaper than existing homes (row 1344); by July 2026 the gap was $40,000 in absolute terms — a $394,000 builder median against $434,000 for existing homes — for the first time since at least the late 1990s, where builder product has historically carried a ~17% premium over the last 25 years (rows 1774, 2420). ✓ Fact-check: group (d) — the inversion is verified by NAHB and Census/NAR data (four consecutive quarters through Q1 2026; July 2026 new median $393,800 vs. NAR existing median $434,100, a ~9.3% gap), but the series' fixed "5.5%" figure was refuted as a point estimate: cite the inversion, not a percentage. It is also partly compositional — builders are delivering smaller homes with a Southern mix skew.

  • Median new-build sale price fell from $460,000 (October 2022 peak) to $387,000–$394,000 by 2026 — a 14–16% cut — with builder price per square foot down from $201 in March 2024 to $180 (rows 1344, 1359, 1366, 1774). Realtor.com is cited for new-home prices at a five-year low with builders having cut nearly 15% over four years (row 1774); the same period saw existing owners raise asking prices ~15%, which is the arithmetic behind the crossover (row 2420).
  • The prior comparable downturn (2007–2010) saw builders cut prices 22% — this cycle's 14–16% is smaller in magnitude but is happening against existing homeowners who are not cutting nearly as fast (the "lock-in effect" — see below).
  • New-home sales are cited as roughly 40% below the pandemic peak and below the 60-year median (row 1774); NAR's June pending-sales index dropped 5.4% to 72.5, the steepest fall of 2026 (row 1322). ✓ Fact-check: group (t) — the June pending-sales decline is verified exactly (−5.4%, first decline in five months, all four regions down).
  • South-region homebuilders held nearly 300,000 homes for sale at the end of 2025 — the highest builder inventory ever recorded in the South, exceeding the 2006 bubble peak (row 1366).
  • The NAHB's chief economist is quoted (via the host) expecting resale prices to "falter" in much of the country in 2026 as existing-home sellers are forced to compete with builder pricing (row 1366) — i.e., builders are now the price-setters pulling the broader market down, not the other way around. The newest readings say that has begun: existing-home months' supply reached 4.88–4.9 months in late summer 2026, an 11-year high (row 2419). ✓ Fact-check: group (ad) — NAR's August 2026 data confirm 4.9 months' supply, the highest in over ten years.

Read for builders: this crossover is a double-edged data point. It validates the price-cut strategy as working — new-build volume has held up better than existing-home volume across the series — but the resale market is now finally repricing (supply at a decade high, list prices down 2.5% year over year per Realtor.com, row 1326), which means the $40,000 gap will have to be defended with further cuts as existing sellers capitulate. The builders who set the price war are about to meet competition from the other side of it.

05

Builder lot supply — the scale of the overhang, region by region

Builder months-of-supply readings recur across the series at 9–10.3 months against a 5.8-month long-run average (rows 1319, 1329, 1333, 1359), with the Census Bureau's trailing three-month average cited at 9.2 months in the newer transcripts (row 1621) — a level the host repeatedly notes has occurred only three other times in 60+ years: the 2008-09 GFC, the 1981-82 double-dip and the 1973-74 oil-shock recession (rows 1774, 2420). ✓ Fact-check: group (c) — Census/HUD's July 2026 release puts new single-family months-of-supply at 9.6 months (2026 readings ranging 8.5–9.9), squarely supporting the series; the 5.8-month long-run average and the three-precedent comparison were not independently verified.

  • Not-yet-started/permitted-home supply reached 21 months in one Census Bureau reading (row 1329) — the deepest part of the pipeline, implying the overhang is not a near-term liquidation problem but a multi-year one if demand doesn't recover.
  • Homes under construction: ~15 months of supply (~259,000 homes); completed, unsold homes: 3.9 months of supply (~115,000 homes) (row 1329) — the completed-and-unsold figure is the most immediately price-sensitive category. A later reading has roughly 500,000 builder homes for sale against a monthly sales pace of 50,000–60,000 (row 1621).
  • Regional concentration: building-permit data by state (trailing 12 months) puts Texas #1 (200,000 permits), Florida #2 (168,000), well ahead of California (104,000) and New York (39,000) (row 1333). North Carolina, Georgia, Arizona, South Carolina and Tennessee also rank high. Residential construction spending is cited as falling sharply from around September 2025, coinciding with inflation-adjusted personal income moving into contraction (row 2047).
  • The demand side of that supply: Austin (−23 to −26% from peak), Cape Coral/Fort Myers (−15 to −20%) and Punta Gorda (−20 to −27%) remain the most severely corrected metros in the series — all high-permit Sun Belt markets that built into a demand air-pocket. ✓ Fact-check: group (j) — Austin's decline verifies at −23.8% (MLS median) to −27.2% (Zillow ZHVI), Cape Coral at ~−19%.
  • A second tier of oversupplied markets emerges in the newer transcripts: Nashville listings at 12,000 in July 2026 against a normal 7,500 (row 1406); Bentonville, Arkansas inventory up 114% in two years to the highest in a decade, with prices 27% overvalued (row 1455); Memphis inventory up more than 50% since 2024 (row 1405); Raleigh new-build values down as much as 16% in three years, with a 2022 new build sold at $396,000 relisted at $325,000 (rows 2098, 2327); Las Vegas new builds reselling $100,000 below purchase (row 2023). In Southwest Florida the series reports builders abandoning projects as easy-money construction loans go into default (row 2022).

Site-selection read: the series' clearest counter-signal is the Northeast/Midwest supply shortage — inventory down 25–70% from normal in Connecticut, New Jersey and Illinois, with the fastest home-value appreciation in the country concentrated in Rockford IL (+32%), Syracuse NY (+31%) and a cluster of Wisconsin/Connecticut/upstate-New-York metros (row 1351); Albany listings have fallen from 4,000 pre-pandemic to 1,500, producing bidding wars (row 1404), and Zillow's own forecast ranks Syracuse and Rochester as the hottest markets in the country (row 1338). Builders have "largely ignored" these markets during the pandemic build-out in favor of Florida/Texas/Georgia/Arizona, per the host's own read (row 1348) — the RE-V series treats this as the more underbuilt, less-competed opportunity relative to the saturated Sun Belt.

06

Product-mix and price-point signals

  • Smaller floor plans, working-class price points: D.R. Horton and Lennar are both cited as the most aggressive on price cuts and on shrinking floor plans, explicitly targeting blue-collar/working-class buyers (row 1333) — median new-build size has drifted down from a 2015 peak of ~2,500 sq ft to ~2,100 sq ft by 2025 (row 1333), still well above the 1950s average of 900–1,100 sq ft cited via a Zillow-sourced figure.
  • Luxury/higher-income builders have not cut price — Toll Brothers (average selling price ~$960,000) and Pulte (~$566,000, down only slightly from 2024) are both cited as holding or raising prices, concentrated in the Northeast/Midwest and higher-income Southern submarkets that are not seeing the correction (row 1333).
  • KB Home reported a 25% YoY revenue drop in its most recent quarterly earnings cited in the series (row 1329) — the sharpest builder revenue decline named in the transcripts.
  • NVR is named as the #4 builder by volume (29,000 closings) without further detail (row 1319).
  • Rate-buydown incentives as low as 3.99% are cited as a general builder tactic beyond Lennar's specific 3.4% product (row 1329), suggesting the financing-incentive route (vs. sticker-price cuts) is now a standard tool across the builder cohort.
  • Below-replacement-cost comps are multiplying: a Calhoun, Georgia new build at $140/sq ft (row 1329), a north-Tampa listing at $123/sq ft (row 1615), American Homes 4 Rent selling at $127–$144/sq ft against a cited $160 replacement cost (row 1360). Where resale clears below what it costs to build, the new-build price floor is set by the secondary market, not by cost-plus.

Read for builders: the market is bifurcating by price point in the same way it's bifurcating by region — entry-level/working-class product in oversupplied Sun Belt markets is where the price war is concentrated; luxury and Northeast/Midwest product has pricing power. A builder's optimal move depends heavily on which side of that line its existing land position sits.

07

Institutional landlords are becoming builders — and are now selling

The series documents single-family rental REITs moving directly into homebuilding, which changes the competitive landscape for entry-level/rental-adjacent product specifically.

  • American Homes for Rent (AMH), the 3rd-largest Wall Street landlord, completed construction of 2,000+ homes in the prior year and is now ranked a top-40 U.S. homebuilder in its own right (row 1518) — a build-to-rent operator competing directly with traditional builders for lots, labor and materials in the same submarkets. ✓ Fact-check: group (h) — confirmed (#39 on the 2024 Builder 100), though 2026 delivery guidance falls to 1,700–2,100.
  • Invitation Homes, the largest Wall Street landlord (85,000–90,000 homes), purchased homebuilder ResiBuilt in 2026 (row 1518) — vertically integrating build-to-rent supply rather than buying existing stock; its Q1 2026 disclosures show renewal rents up 3.7% while new-lease rents fell 3% (4.3% in Florida) (row 1336).
  • But the broader institutional-investor cohort is now net-selling: Wall Street landlords' homes-for-sale listings doubled over five months in 2026 per CNBC, and Wall Street was net-selling more homes than it bought year-to-date (row 1518). Investor purchases overall are cited as down ~50% from the 2022 peak nationally, 50–70% in Florida over four years, and 58–77% in the hardest-hit metros (rows 1336, 1518, 1600). ✓ Fact-check: group (f), re-checked September 25 — Redfin's Q1 2026 report (still the latest edition) supports a purchase slowdown (−6% YoY, lowest since 2020) but publishes no ~50%-from-peak comparison, and investor market share was nearly flat (19% vs. 20%) with investor-owned homes just 7.8% of listings, a five-year low. A later video's claim that investors fell from 21% to 15% of buyers (row 2589) is not verified and conflicts with Redfin's share data — group (ae). Read it as institutional demand withdrawal, not liquidation.
  • Why: single-family rental cap rates (~4.8%) now sit below mortgage/debt costs — an inversion of the 2010–2021 relationship that made buy-to-rent profitable, turning existing rental holdings into "money losers" per the host's framing (row 1518). The credit side is deteriorating in parallel: Fannie Mae's multifamily serious-delinquency rate is at its highest since 2010 outside the pandemic (row 1324) — ✓ Fact-check: group (v), confirmed at 0.78% in Q1 2026 — and Fitch's private-credit default rate set a record 6.3% in August (row 2039), group (y).

Policy risk to watch — RESOLVED, verified, and now mis-reported downstream: the "21st Century ROAD to Housing Act" (H.R. 6644, 119th Congress) is real and became law on July 11, 2026 without the President's signature — the series tracked its signing status across several videos without resolution (rows 1326, 1336, 1339, 1360, 1361, 1518), and the fact-check closed the loop against Congress.gov and legal analyses, group (g). A September 21 video from outside the RE-V series then reported that Trump had "killed" the bill (row 2208); ✓ Fact-check: group (aa) — that is refuted: he declined to sign it in protest over a stalled voter-ID bill and let it take effect, and the "communist" language came from House conservatives, not a veto. Verified provisions: entities with direct or indirect control of 350+ single-family homes are barred from purchasing more, effective ~January 7, 2027, sunsetting after 15 years, with civil penalties up to $1M per violation or 3x purchase price; final votes were Senate 85-5 and House 358-32. The confirmed carve-outs — newly constructed homes, build-to-rent programs, and renovated/rehabilitated property — directly benefit build-to-rent operators like AMH and Invitation Homes/ResiBuilt over investors buying existing stock. Any builder with (or considering) a rental-portfolio arm should treat the January 2027 effective date as a planning deadline, not a hypothetical (verification report).

Read for builders: the institutional-landlord pullback removes a bulk buyer that historically absorbed entry-level product in Sun Belt metros — one more source of demand softness builders are competing into. At the same time, the AMH/Invitation Homes build-to-rent pivot, combined with the enacted investor ban's new-construction carve-out, signals build-to-rent as a growth product category specifically exempted from the regulatory headwind on the buy-existing side.

Demand-side signals builders should watch

  • The rate story reversed: rather than the cuts the series anticipated through the spring, the Federal Reserve raised rates on September 16, 2026 — its first hike since 2023 — with Boston Fed and Richmond Fed officials warning inflation risk now outweighs job-loss risk (row 2563). ✓ Fact-check: group (z) — the hike is confirmed; a companion claim that inflation is running at "4.1%" (row 2208) is refuted (August CPI: 3.4% headline, 2.4% core). Every builder buydown product is now underwriting against a rising, not falling, base rate.
  • Affordability is not coming back on any rate path: Morgan Stanley's June 16, 2026 analysis modelled rates settling at 4%, 5% and 6% and found carrying costs stay well above pre-2022 norms in every case, with gains stalling around 2027 (row 1328) — ✓ Fact-check: group (w), confirmed exactly. The series' own affordability read — mortgage payment at ~38% of income, price-to-income at 4.2–4.3 against a 3.5 norm (rows 1328, 1339) — points the same way.
  • Mortgage rate-lock-in: a large share of existing owners hold sub-3–4% rates (as high as 27–30% of California mortgage holders) and are reluctant to sell — part of why new-build has been able to undercut existing-home pricing structurally (rows 1332, 1344, 1350, 1358, 1360).
  • The rate distribution has flipped: the share of U.S. mortgages above 6% now exceeds the share below 3% nationally, concentrated especially in Alabama, Arkansas, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Kentucky, Georgia, Florida and Texas (rows 1332, 1344, 1350, 1358, 1360) — the same states carrying the heaviest builder lot-supply overhang, so rate stress and oversupply compound in the same geography. ✓ Fact-check: group (k) — confirmed exactly against FHFA National Mortgage Database data (21.2% above 6% vs. 20.0% below 3%).
  • Turnover, not price, is the demand gauge: existing-home sales ran at 3.9–4.2 million annualised through 2026 — 4.17 million in May, cited as 2008-09 crash levels relative to the housing stock (rows 1328, 1335). ✓ Fact-check: group (m) — the May figure is exact (NAR). Zillow's own forecast turned negative (−0.1% over the next twelve months) with 27 of the top 50 metros forecast down (rows 1338, 1349); group (n) confirms the negative print, not the metro count. Zillow then cut 7% of its workforce, citing a second-half slowdown, with site traffic down 2% (row 1406).
  • Boomer supply is a slow tide, not a wave: the series cites Freddie Mac for "nearly 10 million" boomer homes reaching the market over the next decade (rows 1800, 2090). ✓ Fact-check: group (o) — Freddie Mac says "more than 9 million" and explicitly calls it "a tide, not a tsunami", mostly offset by new household formation. Builders should not plan for a resale flood on that basis.
  • AI CapEx as a regional demand wildcard: San Francisco is repeatedly cited as the one major West Coast market still appreciating (rows 1330, 1339, 1350), attributed directly to AI-company concentration — a reminder that tech-driven local income growth can locally override the national correction; the Memphis inventory surge around a single large data-center project (row 1405) is the same force in reverse.
  • Buyer sentiment as a leading indicator: a Redfin survey cited in the series found 59–60% of Americans believe AI advances will hurt job prospects and housing affordability (rows 1356, 1357); Bank of America Institute data show Florida home values down 3.7% year over year with 14–15 states now declining (row 1334) — group (ac), confirmed.
09

Bottom line for a builder audience

  1. The price-cut-plus-captive-financing playbook (D.R. Horton) is winning volume but concentrating credit risk in FHA paper whose serious delinquencies are rising while every other cohort improves — and a 20% cancellation rate says the marginal buyer is failing qualification. Other builders can emulate the pricing side, but only with genuine underwriting discipline or a captive lender willing to hold that risk.
  2. Lennar's cost-and-cycle-time compression is the more durable margin lever — construction costs down 13% to $81/sq ft, cycle time to 121 days — worth benchmarking internally regardless of whether a builder matches Lennar's headline price cuts.
  3. The new-build-cheaper-than-existing crossover is structural, and the gap is now $40,000 — but the resale market has finally started to reprice (existing supply at a decade-high 4.9 months), so the discount will have to be defended with further cuts rather than simply enjoyed.
  4. Site selection should weight the Northeast/Midwest supply shortage against a Sun Belt whose casualty list keeps lengthening — Austin and Southwest Florida first, now Nashville, Bentonville, Memphis, Raleigh and Las Vegas — with the same states (FL, TX, TN, GA, AL, AR, OK, KY) carrying both the heaviest lot overhang and the worst rate-affordability mix.
  5. Build-to-rent is a distinct, regulation-favored category — AMH and Invitation Homes are moving from landlord to builder specifically because new construction is carved out of the institutional-investor ban in force from January 2027; that carve-out is law, whatever downstream commentary claims.
  6. Underwrite for higher-for-longer, not for cuts — the Fed is hiking, Morgan Stanley finds no rate path that restores pre-2022 affordability, and every buydown product on the market defers rather than removes the reset. The demand that price cuts are buying today is being financed at rates that are rising.
